# Continental Ecology and Biogeography

**Status:** ESTABLISHED environmental framework; regional species inventories remain incomplete  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY  
**Baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Primary references:** [Agriculture, Food Supply, and Seasonal Work](../world/Agriculture_Food_and_Seasonal_Work.md), [Continental Geography](../geography/README.md), [Creature Registry](Creature_Registry.md)

This document translates established continental geography, climate tendencies, land use, and economic dependencies into a usable ecological framework. It is not a species catalogue and does not establish animal populations, plant distributions, migration routes, or supernatural habitats. Its purpose is to help the GM reason consistently about what kinds of environments exist, how people use them, and what ecological questions matter when a location enters play.

## 1. Rules for reading the natural world

1. **Geography constrains ecology.** Elevation, water, soil, exposure, and distance from the sea matter. A low ridge is not an alpine range; an arid march is not automatically a desert; a wet delta is not one endless swamp.
2. **Human use is part of the habitat.** Farms, coppice, grazing, mines, roads, ports, canals, settlements, and abandoned works change vegetation and animal behavior.
3. **A plausible species is not a confirmed occurrence.** Use PROPOSED until direct observation, reliable documentation, or explicit GM approval establishes presence.
4. **A single sighting is local evidence.** It does not establish a regional population, frequency, breeding ground, or migration.
5. **Season names do not dictate weather.** The campaign's seasons vary in timing and duration, including across regions. Ecological events should follow actual conditions rather than a fixed month chart.
6. **Ordinary life dominates most landscapes.** Most people encounter crops, livestock, working animals, birds, rodents, fish, and familiar wild animals far more often than monsters.
7. **Supernatural ecology is exceptional.** A monster, undead presence, magical plant, or unusual migration needs a specific canon basis. Do not infer one from atmosphere alone.

## 2. Northern Wilderness and Frozen Expanse

**Established environmental tendency:** severe cold, snow, ice, exposed terrain, and difficult travel beyond the northern settled territories. The Northern Holds and Pine Marches include cold uplands, forests, valleys, and frontier settlements.

**Ecological implications:** short or uncertain growing periods, high value placed on fodder and stored food, seasonal limits on surface travel, and strong dependence on shelter, fuel, clothing, and transport. Forests and valleys can provide timber, grazing, game, and other resources, but availability varies sharply with exposure and distance from settlements.

**Human relationships:** households must plan around winter stores, animal survival, fuel, road maintenance, and the cost of moving supplies. House Veyr's responsibility for the Ancient Barrier adds a provisioning burden. This does not establish a current shortage or mean all northern communities are starving.

**Open:** exact tree line, species ranges, local crop limits, migration patterns, and the ecological character of the Frozen Expanse. The Barrier's builders, purpose, and any threat beyond it remain protected unknowns.

## 3. Western Highlands, High Peaks, and Green Valleys

**Established environmental tendency:** mountain and forest country with elevation-driven variation, wooded valleys, upland pasture, and passes. Conditions differ between sheltered valleys, exposed slopes, high passes, and lower forest margins.

**Ecological implications:** vegetation and animal life should change with elevation, aspect, water, and exposure. Timber, grazing, streams, and mineral access shape human use. Snowmelt, storms, slope failures, and pass conditions affect both wildlife movement and travel, but do not imply that every pass closes each winter.

**Human relationships:** woodland rights, grazing customs, mining access, and local routes may be more important than a neat regional boundary. Isolated communities possess practical knowledge of paths and seasons that outsiders lack.

**Open:** precise forest composition, high-altitude species, pass-by-pass seasonal conditions, and the location of any important breeding or migration grounds.

## 4. Western Heartlands, Golden Fields, and Riverlands

**Established environmental tendency:** temperate agricultural plains, productive valleys, pasture, and river systems. House Edarth's territory is a principal agricultural heartland, with grain, livestock, dairy, wool, hides, and agricultural manpower among its strengths.

**Ecological implications:** the landscape is heavily shaped by cultivation, grazing, hedgerows, orchards, drainage, mills, and transport. Wild habitat persists in river margins, uncultivated slopes, woodland remnants, and field boundaries. Crop pests, livestock disease, predators, scavengers, and pollinating insects are practical concerns, but no specific outbreak or population is established.

**Human relationships:** harvest labor, seed reserves, hay, fodder, storage, milling, rents, and transport shape the landscape as much as rainfall or soil. Productive land does not guarantee equal prosperity. Access to food depends on ownership, labor, debt, local rights, and the ability to move and store goods.

**Open:** exact crop varieties, yields, woodland coverage, major river fish, and local wildlife inventories.

## 5. Central Crownlands and Royal Basin

**Established environmental tendency:** an inland river basin with central routes, markets, administrative centers, and substantial demand from the Crown, court, garrisons, and urban populations.

**Ecological implications:** settled corridors and managed farmland are likely to be more prominent than uninterrupted wilderness near major roads and cities. Riverbanks, wetlands, wooded estates, parks, and less intensively cultivated margins provide habitat. Water management, road building, urban waste, and provisioning influence local ecology.

**Human relationships:** the Crownlands' ecological importance is inseparable from administration and supply. Food may be collected, taxed, stored, transported, or requisitioned for institutions and armies. A river or road can be an ecological corridor and a political/economic artery at once.

**Open:** exact Crownland river network, flood behavior, forest distribution, and local species occurrences. Do not use the working regional label to infer exact political boundaries.

## 6. Central Inland Sea Basin

**Established environmental tendency:** a large inland sea supporting substantial shipping, ports, coastal settlements, fishing, and naval commerce. Storms, fog, and navigation hazards are established features of the maritime system.

**Ecological implications:** the sea connects coastal habitats and human communities, but does not make every shoreline ecologically identical. Sheltered inlets, exposed coasts, river mouths, rocky shores, wetlands, and islands may support different plant and animal communities. River inflows and port activity affect local water and shore conditions.

**Human relationships:** fishing, shipping, salt work, ship maintenance, harbor access, and coastal settlement shape the shore. Sudden storms can strand vessels, damage landing places, and alter local work. Maritime access is an advantage, not a guarantee of food security.

**Open:** salinity, depth, currents, named fish species, marine mammals, spawning grounds, and seasonal movement. No sea monster, supernatural current, or uniform sea ecology is established.

## 7. Northeastern Mountain System and Khar-Dum

**Established environmental tendency:** high mountains containing the ancestral Kharadorn homeland and extensive dwarven surface and subterranean settlements. Mining, engineering, and specialist manufacture are important cultural and economic activities.

**Ecological implications:** exposed slopes, sheltered valleys, mountain streams, forested lower elevations, and underground environments should be treated as distinct habitats. Surface agriculture and livestock may be constrained by terrain and elevation. Underground settlement does not remove dependence on surface food, water, trade, and transport.

**Human relationships:** mines and tunnels are not ecologically separate from the surface. Drainage, spoil, timber demand, pack animals, roads, and food imports connect subterranean communities to surrounding landscapes. Mining can alter watercourses and slopes, but do not assume every mine pollutes or every tunnel connects to a natural cave system.

**Open:** exact elevation bands, surface vegetation, endemic species, underground food production, cave ecosystems, and the distribution of subterranean waters.

## 8. Eastern Forests and Eastern Shore

**Established environmental tendency:** forested coastal and river country with maritime settlements and important shipping connections. Merrowen's maritime reach is significant, but it does not establish sovereignty over every port or shoreline.

**Ecological implications:** woodland resources, rivers, estuaries, sheltered bays, exposed shore, and coastal wetlands may occur in different combinations. Wind, salt exposure, storms, and river levels affect vegetation and human use. Timber extraction and shipbuilding can create strong demand for particular woodland resources.

**Human relationships:** ports and coastal communities combine fishing, trade, ship service, salt work, and agriculture in different proportions. Pilotage and local weather knowledge are valuable. Commercial shipping routes are not automatically wildlife migration routes.

**Open:** forest composition, major river systems, coastal species, and the degree of human modification around individual ports.

## 9. Eastern River Delta and Great Delta

**Established environmental tendency:** low-lying branching waterways, wetlands, silting, and flood-prone ground. The Great Delta is shaped by channels and water movement rather than a single stable riverbank.

**Ecological implications:** channels, backwaters, marshes, reed beds, floodplain woodland, mudflats, and cultivated islands are possible habitat types, but their exact distribution is not yet mapped. Sediment, water level, and channel movement can change access and land use. Do not assign a specific channel shift or catastrophic flood without an established event.

**Human relationships:** fishing, water transport, ferrying, reed cutting, agriculture, drainage, and settlement compete for space and water access. Local knowledge of channels and seasonal conditions is essential. A route safe for a shallow boat may not be safe for a barge or seagoing vessel.

**Open:** channel geography, flood cycles, settlement patterns, crop systems, species ranges, and local jurisdiction.

## 10. Southwestern Marches

**Established environmental tendency:** arid highlands, dry valleys, plateaus, and oases. Water access, wells, cisterns, and caravan movement are structural concerns.

**Ecological implications:** plant and animal life must be understood in relation to water availability, shade, elevation, and seasonal runoff. Oases and springs are ecological concentrations as well as human settlement anchors. Dry country is not lifeless, and aridity does not imply that all land is sand or that every water source is permanent.

**Human relationships:** wells, cisterns, grazing, caravan routes, and water rights may be central to local power. Sudden runoff can be dangerous even in generally dry terrain. The value of a water source depends on reliability, access, quality, and the rights governing it.

**Open:** local plant communities, drought-adapted livestock, water-source reliability, seasonal movement, and the boundaries between dry valleys and higher country. No current drought is established.

## 11. Southern Principalities

**Established environmental tendency:** fertile lowlands, forests, rivers, and independent cities under politically fragmented rule. There is no single southern agricultural or ecological authority.

**Ecological implications:** fertile river valleys, cultivated lowlands, woodland, and local wetland environments support varied production. The same broad region can contain different land use, crops, and water management because local rulers and communities make independent decisions.

**Human relationships:** local tolls, rights, markets, and political arrangements affect access to forests, rivers, pasture, and farmland. Fertility does not guarantee stability or equal prosperity. Flooding, market access, and local conflict can affect one valley without defining the whole south.

**Open:** local climate variation, named ecosystems, forest species, crop specializations, and region-specific wildlife.

## 12. Southern Islands

**Established environmental tendency:** island chains, sheltered harbors, fishing settlements, and maritime principalities dependent on sea movement and safe anchorage.

**Ecological implications:** island habitats vary with size, elevation, freshwater availability, exposure, and human settlement. Small islands may have limited freshwater and land for grazing or cultivation; larger islands may support more varied agriculture and woodland. Do not assume all islands are lush, barren, or identical.

**Human relationships:** fishing, inter-island trade, ship repair, water storage, and access to mainland markets matter. Storms and wind can isolate a settlement even when the next island appears close. Safe anchorage and freshwater may be as important as fertile soil.

**Open:** island-specific vegetation, freshwater sources, endemic species, crop patterns, and seasonal sea conditions.

## 13. Ecological questions to ask when adding a creature or plant

Before adding a species to a regional record, establish only what matters:

- What named region and habitat support it?
- Is the occurrence observed, documented, reported, rumored, or merely proposed?
- What food, water, shelter, and breeding conditions does it require?
- How does elevation, season, weather, or human land use affect it?
- What signs would a local person recognize?
- Does it compete with livestock, crops, fisheries, or another species?
- Is it hunted, feared, protected, traded, domesticated, or ignored?
- What do ordinary people believe about it, and what is actually known?
- Would its presence have consequences for settlement, transport, disease, or local rights?
- Does the claim require map geometry or a historical event that has not been established?

Do not answer every question for every species. A common bird seen in the background does not need a complete ecological dossier. Use the detail proportionate to its importance.

## 15. Protected unknowns

The following remain unresolved unless another authoritative record establishes them:

- Detailed climate classifications and weather normals.
- Exact forest boundaries, tree composition, and woodland coverage.
- Named regional plant and animal inventories outside Tallowmere.
- Migration routes, breeding grounds, and population sizes.
- The ecology and biological limits of the Frozen Expanse.
- The nature of underground ecosystems and their relationship to surface habitats.
- The inland sea's detailed marine ecology.
- Disease reservoirs, endemic animal diseases, and major historical epizootics.
- Magical ecosystems, supernatural migrations, and the present-day survival of the fallen dynasty's dragons.

**GM principle:** use the continent's established geography and human economies to make ecological decisions plausible. Leave the rest open until play gives it a reason to matter.
