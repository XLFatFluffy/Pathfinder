# Deep Bracken — Common Fauna Dossier

**Status:** ESTABLISHED regional occurrence; natural-history portrayal guidance  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY  
**Region:** Deep Bracken, beyond the managed Brackenwood  
**Snapshot:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Authority:** [Wilderness Beyond Tallowmere](../locations/starting_city/Wilderness_Beyond_Tallowmere.md)

The wilderness record identifies deer, wild boar, foxes, badgers, and wolves as common animals in the Deep Bracken. This dossier develops practical natural-history guidance for portraying those animals consistently.

**Canon boundary:** Their ordinary occurrence in the Deep Bracken is established. Exact population sizes, species or subspecies, breeding territories, den locations, seasonal movements, hunting quotas, and individual animals are not. The behavioral material below is grounded in ordinary animal ecology and is guidance for believable portrayal, not a claim that every detail has been separately documented by Tallowmere naturalists.

The Deep Bracken is older and less-managed woodland beyond Brackenholt and the managed Brackenwood. It contains denser cover, old clearings, intermittent charcoal camps, game trails, and unreliable paths. The five common animals in this dossier are established for the Deep Bracken only, not as a confirmed species list for Brackenwood. Occasional camps show that people sometimes use the outer forest; they do not establish continuous management, a complete supply chain to Tallowmere, or the exact reach of customary woodland rights. The animals live within a landscape also used by hunters, charcoal burners, gatherers, livestock keepers, and occasional travelers.

## 1. Deer

**Regional status:** Common in the Deep Bracken.  
**Pathfinder use:** Use an appropriate ordinary deer or herd animal stat block if mechanics are needed; do not substitute a magical beast without cause.

### Natural history and behavior

Deer are browsing and grazing herbivores. They feed on leaves, shoots, grasses, herbs, buds, and seasonal woodland foods such as fallen fruit or nuts where available. Their feeding changes with the growth of vegetation and the availability of browse. They favor places where food and cover are close together.

They are alert and wary. Their first response to disturbance is usually to freeze, watch, and withdraw rather than attack. A startled animal may crash through undergrowth, cross a path suddenly, or bolt in a direction that creates danger for a traveler or horse. A cornered or wounded deer can injure a person, but it is not a predator.

### Signs and recognition

- Narrow cloven hoofprints in mud, soft earth, and snow.
- Browsed shoots and stripped leaves at reachable height.
- Repeated narrow paths through brush and between feeding and cover.
- Droppings and resting places in sheltered cover.
- Hair caught on rough bark or fencing.

A single track does not prove a herd is nearby. Tracks may be old, and several animals using the same trail can make numbers difficult to judge.

### Human relationship

Deer are valuable game, but hunting access is governed by local authority, custom, and the rights attached to particular woodland or estates. Hunters value meat, hide, and antler. Farmers may resent browsing near orchards and young plantations. Charcoal burners and gatherers generally prefer deer to remain undisturbed around active work sites.

A local hunter may read a trail's freshness and direction. That skill does not automatically reveal how many animals are present or where they will go next.

### Seasonal portrayal

In spring and summer, fresh growth provides browse and cover. In autumn, woodland food and fallen mast may attract animals to particular stands. In winter, feeding becomes more constrained and tracks may be easier to see in snow or soft ground. Do not assign a precise rutting calendar or migration route without deciding it for a specific local herd.

## 2. Wild boar

**Regional status:** Common in the Deep Bracken.  
**Pathfinder use:** Use the appropriate ordinary boar stat block. Do not silently substitute a dire boar or enlarge its size.

### Natural history and behavior

Boar are omnivorous and opportunistic. They root for bulbs, roots, fungi, invertebrates, and other food, and may eat fallen fruit, nuts, carrion, or crops when accessible. Their rooting disturbs soil and leaf litter and can damage gardens, field margins, and woodland regrowth.

They are intelligent, strong, and potentially dangerous, but not naturally eager to fight people. They usually avoid humans when they can. A surprised animal may charge if it feels trapped; a sow defending young is especially dangerous. Wounded boar should be treated as unpredictable.

### Signs and recognition

- Broad, churned patches of soil where the ground has been rooted.
- Cloven tracks, often broader and more rounded than deer tracks.
- Mud wallows and rubbing marks on trees.
- Broken stems, disturbed leaf litter, and feeding signs.
- Paths leading between cover and feeding grounds.

Rooting can be mistaken for human digging or the work of a larger creature. Look for hoofprints and repeated feeding patterns before drawing conclusions.

### Human relationship

Boar provide meat and can be hunted, but they may damage crops and threaten dogs or livestock. Their presence creates tension between hunters, woodland users, and farmers whose fields border the forest. A lord may value a controlled hunt while a tenant farmer wants the animals kept away from turnips and grain.

A boar hunt is dangerous work. Dogs may be used to locate or hold an animal, but handlers risk serious injury. A wounded boar may retreat into dense cover rather than flee into open ground.

### Seasonal portrayal

Food availability changes where boar root and forage. Fallen nuts and fruit may concentrate activity in autumn. In lean periods they may range more widely and investigate human food. Avoid inventing a fixed annual population cycle or a specific sounder territory.

## 3. Foxes

**Regional status:** Common in the Deep Bracken.  
**Pathfinder use:** Use an appropriate ordinary fox or small predator stat block; do not grant speech, supernatural cunning, or magical abilities.

### Natural history and behavior

Foxes are adaptable omnivores and predators. They take rodents, rabbits, birds, eggs, insects, carrion, fruit, and unattended scraps. Their diet shifts with what is available. They can use woodland edges, clearings, charcoal camps, farm margins, and abandoned structures.

They are cautious and generally avoid close contact with people. A fox that watches a camp may be assessing food opportunities, not plotting against the party. Habituation can occur around repeated human activity, especially where scraps are left out.

### Signs and recognition

- Small, neat pawprints in mud or soft soil.
- Droppings left conspicuously on paths, stones, or raised objects.
- Feathers, eggshells, or small prey remains near cover.
- Narrow runs through grass and undergrowth.
- A sharp musky odor around dens or frequently used routes.

A fox den is not necessarily occupied year-round. Do not place cubs or a den at a site unless the scene requires and establishes them.

### Human relationship

Poultry keepers dislike foxes that learn to raid henhouses. Hunters may trap or hunt them for their pelts. Charcoal burners and travelers may tolerate a fox that scavenges scraps, provided it does not steal food or threaten domestic animals.

Foxes are useful background signs of a functioning food web. They should not automatically be treated as omens, familiars, spies, or shapeshifters.

### Seasonal portrayal

Foxes may be more visible when searching for food or when young animals begin moving beyond a den. Their coat, behavior, and local visibility can change with season, but exact breeding dates and den locations remain undefined.

## 4. Badgers

**Regional status:** Common in the Deep Bracken.  
**Pathfinder use:** Use an appropriate ordinary badger stat block. Do not automatically use the dire badger.

### Natural history and behavior

Badgers are powerful burrowing omnivores. They eat earthworms, insects, small animals, roots, fruit, and other accessible food. Their burrows, known as setts, can be extensive and used over long periods. They favor suitable banks and well-drained ground near feeding areas.

They are mostly nocturnal or active around dusk and dawn. They generally avoid people, but can bite fiercely if cornered, trapped, or handled. Their claws and strength make an injured or confined badger a poor animal to approach.

### Signs and recognition

- Distinctive broad pawprints with visible claw marks.
- Large excavated burrow entrances and spoil heaps.
- Worn paths leading to and from a sett.
- Latrines or repeated droppings in small excavated pits.
- Rooted soil and disturbed leaf litter.

A sett may have several entrances and may be used by more than one animal. Not every hole in a bank is a badger sett.

### Human relationship

Badgers can damage banks, gardens, and some field structures through digging. They may also consume pests and are part of the woodland's ordinary ecology. People may avoid disturbing a known sett, but no universal legal protection or taboo is established for Tallowmere.

A charcoal burner or forester may know old setts as landmarks. A city visitor may mistake an occupied sett for an abandoned animal hole.

### Seasonal portrayal

Badgers may reduce activity during severe cold or poor weather and can use sheltered setts for rest. Do not assume they hibernate in the strict sense or disappear for the entire winter.

## 5. Wolves

**Regional status:** Common in the Deep Bracken.  
**Pathfinder use:** Use the ordinary wolf stat block unless a specific, separately approved supernatural or advanced creature is intended.

### Natural history and behavior

Wolves are social predators. They hunt suitable prey and may scavenge when opportunities arise. Their diet and hunting behavior depend on local prey, pack size, season, and disturbance. They avoid unnecessary injury and generally avoid people, especially where people travel in groups or carry fire and weapons.

Wolves are not mindless attackers. A wolf may observe a traveler from cover, follow at a distance, test an isolated animal, or retreat when challenged. A starving, habituated, injured, diseased, or unusually bold individual may behave differently, but those conditions need a cause.

A pack is a social unit, not a fixed number chosen for encounter balance. Do not assume every wolf sighting involves a pack, or that a pack's presence means an attack is imminent.

### Signs and recognition

- Pawprints, often following game trails or woodland paths.
- Scat containing hair, bone fragments, or other prey remains.
- Tracks around a carcass or feeding site.
- Howling or calls that may carry over long distances.
- Disturbed prey trails, scattered remains, or signs of a chase.

Tracks can overlap with those of large dogs. Experienced hunters compare stride, track shape, gait, and context rather than relying on one print.

### Human relationship

Wolves can threaten livestock, especially animals left unattended near woodland. Shepherds, foresters, and hunters may know where losses have occurred, but a missing sheep is not proof of wolves. Dogs, disease, theft, exposure, and accidents are alternatives.

Local responses may include stronger night enclosures, shepherding, traps, organized hunts, or payment for proof of a kill, but none of these policies is established as universal in Tallowmere. Do not invent a bounty or a standing wolf-hunt without a local authority decision.

Wolves carry cultural weight in many communities, but Tallowmere-specific sayings, religious meanings, and noble heraldic associations remain open. Ordinary wolves are not automatically connected to House Veyr, the Ancient Barrier, or supernatural forces.

### Seasonal portrayal

Prey availability, snow, livestock movement, and human activity affect where wolves travel. Harsh conditions may bring them nearer to settlements, but do not use winter as an automatic excuse for attacks. A wolf pack's range and seasonal movement should be established only if it becomes important to play.

## 6. Reading the woodland as a whole

The five animals occupy overlapping but different ecological roles:

- Deer browse vegetation and provide prey for large predators.
- Boar root and forage broadly, disturbing soil and sometimes damaging cultivated ground.
- Foxes take smaller prey and scavenge.
- Badgers forage in soil and leaf litter, using stable burrow systems.
- Wolves prey on suitable larger animals and may affect livestock near the woodland edge.

These relationships help the GM describe a believable woodland, but they do not justify a numerical food-web simulation. A deer track, rooted patch, fox scent, badger sett, or distant wolf call can make the landscape feel inhabited without requiring an encounter.

## 7. What local people know

Knowledge varies by occupation:

- **Hunters and trappers** recognize tracks, feeding signs, likely routes, and the difference between fresh and old disturbance.
- **Charcoal burners and foresters** know where animals cross work paths, where setts and wallows have been seen, and which clearings are recently disturbed.
- **Shepherds and smallholders** care most about livestock losses, dogs, fencing, and whether predators are becoming unusually bold.
- **Merchants and townspeople** may know common stories but often confuse tracks or repeat exaggerated accounts.
- **Nobles and officials** may know hunting rights and the value of game without possessing practical fieldcraft.

No single local authority has been established as maintaining a complete wildlife census. Most knowledge is personal, occupational, and uneven.

## 8. Protected unknowns

- Exact number and distribution of deer, boar, foxes, badgers, and wolves.
- Pack territories, den sites, breeding sites, and seasonal ranges.
- Whether any particular animal has become habituated to a settlement or work camp.
- Local hunting seasons, quotas, bounties, and reserved-game rules.
- Any unusually large, diseased, magical, or supernatural individual.
- Whether wolves or other predators are responsible for a particular livestock loss.

When one of these details matters to play, establish it for the specific place and moment rather than pretending a complete regional survey exists.
