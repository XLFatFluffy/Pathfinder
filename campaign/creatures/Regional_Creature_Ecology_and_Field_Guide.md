# Regional Creature Ecology and Field Guide

**Status:** GM reference; established facts and proposed regional candidates are explicitly separated  
**Baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Ruleset:** Pathfinder First Edition

This guide connects creatures to the places people live, work, travel, and fear. It is not a catalogue of every Pathfinder monster that could exist. A suitable habitat makes a creature plausible; it does not prove that it lives there.

## 1. Evidence before ecology

Use these distinctions consistently:

- **Established creature:** exists in the setting or established history.
- **Established regional occurrence:** canon confirms it occurs in a named region or habitat.
- **Observed:** directly encountered in play at a recorded place and date.
- **Prepared:** included in GM preparation but not yet encountered.
- **Reported / Rumored:** claimed by a witness or local story, not independently confirmed.
- **Candidate:** terrain and ecology could support it, but no occurrence is established.
- **Unknown:** no supported conclusion is available.

A single sighting does not establish a population. A creature may be transient, escaped, summoned, displaced, or an unusual individual.

## 2. Creature ecology already established

| Creature or group | Status | Established relationship |
|---|---|---|
| Deer, wild boar, foxes, badgers, wolves | ESTABLISHED ordinary fauna | Common in the Deep Bracken only; no measured population or wider distribution implied. |
| Giant spiders | ESTABLISHED regional creature | Uncommon; associated with the Deep Bracken and isolated wooded frontier areas. |
| Giant frogs | ESTABLISHED regional creature | Uncommon; localized wetland pools, including portions of the Siltfen. |
| Stirges | ESTABLISHED creature; rare locally | Rare in suitable wet woodland, caves, marsh margins, and abandoned structures. |
| Dire animals | ESTABLISHED as occasional individuals | Rare unusual specimens in deeper woodland and remote frontier settings; no broad population or mutation system. |
| Trolls | ESTABLISHED creature; local population unconfirmed | The prepared Selwick encounter is PREPARED, not proof of a local population. |
| Fallen-dynasty dragons | ESTABLISHED historical creatures | Twelve existed at the dynasty's height; most were killed. Present-day survival and distribution remain UNKNOWN. |
| Vampirism | ESTABLISHED supernatural condition | Wider origins, prevalence, and ecology remain UNKNOWN. |

The localized giant spider, giant frog, and stirge associations are approved exceptions to the general rule that habitat alone does not establish a monster population. They remain uncommon or rare and localized. They do not justify populating every suitable habitat with them.

## 3. Tallowmere and its hinterland

### Deep Bracken

**Established:** deer, wild boar, foxes, badgers, wolves; uncommon giant spiders; occasional rare dire animals.

The Deep Bracken is older and less-managed than Brackenwood. Game trails, old clearings, intermittent charcoal camps, and unreliable paths create a mosaic of human use and quieter habitat.

Field signs include tracks in soft ground, boar rooting, fox and wolf trails, badger setts, heavy webbing, wrapped prey, and unusually large tracks. A large track alone does not prove a dire animal is present.

### Brackenwood

**Established habitat:** managed woodland, coppice, charcoal burns, mature stands, clearings, and gathering areas.

Ordinary woodland wildlife is plausible. The Deep Bracken's established fauna does not automatically transfer to Brackenwood. Giant spiders may occur only in isolated, quieter pockets consistent with the regional bestiary. No common monster population is established.

Active woodland work should displace some wildlife. A creature living beside a charcoal camp needs a reason to tolerate repeated human presence.

### Northfields

**Established habitat:** farms, pasture, orchards, hedgerows, ditches, and scattered copses.

No dangerous monster population is established. Ordinary farm and hedgerow wildlife is the expected baseline. Missing poultry is not evidence of a monster. Repeated losses, tracks, damaged enclosures, and independent witness accounts may justify investigation.

### River Vale

**Established habitat:** navigable lower river, mill reaches, pools, gravel bars, tributaries, flood meadows, fisheries, and riverbank settlements.

No specific dangerous river-monster population is established. Ordinary aquatic resources are documented in the River Vale and Siltfen Aquatic Ecology record. The river itself is a hazard: current, floodwater, submerged debris, cold shock, damaged banks, and unsafe crossings. Do not add aquatic monsters merely because a river encounter would be dramatic.

### Siltfen

**Established habitat:** wet meadows, reed beds, shallow pools, muddy channels, alder stands, and seasonal flooding. Eels, frogs, and waterfowl are established resource categories.

**Established dangerous creatures:** uncommon giant frogs in localized wetland pools; rare stirges may use suitable marsh margins or sheltered structures. No broader infestation or uniform monster population is established.

Field signs include disturbed reed margins, feeding disturbances around a pool, unusual concentrations of small animal remains, and stirge roost signs in sheltered structures. A wetland worker may know a particular pool is best avoided without knowing precisely what lives there.

### Greyback Ridges and quarry country

**Established habitat:** low stony hills, worked quarries, prospecting cuts, caves, abandoned workings, and rough upland margins.

No specific monster population is established. The terrain could support burrowing, cave-dwelling, or cliff-associated creatures, but no ankheg infestation, cave predator population, or lair is canon.

A cave is a geological feature, not an automatic monster room. Most abandoned workings should be empty, unstable, flooded, or occupied by ordinary animals.

### Eastern Shore and Outer Eastern Shore

**Established habitat:** low bluffs, shingle coves, landing places, salt-working sites, fishing huts, exposed water, and isolated camps farther out.

No specific dangerous marine monster or supernatural coastal population is established. Ordinary hazards include storms, rip currents, unstable cliffs, cold water, poor visibility, and damaged boats.

## 4. Continental regional ecology

The following are ecological planning notes, not new canon occurrences. They prevent arbitrary monster placement when the campaign travels.

### Vaelorian territories and Western Highlands

Established landscape includes western highlands, wooded and upland country, long-maintained estates, commercial settlements, and the Vaelorian seat at Aurelith.

Cultivated estate grounds and roads should primarily show ordinary wildlife, working animals, pests, and human security. Managed woodland supports ordinary forest fauna. Remote slopes bring exposure, falls, weather, and difficult travel before supernatural threats. Old structures may contain bats, birds, rodents, and vermin unless a specific supernatural cause is established.

**No specific regional monster population is confirmed.** Do not assume every old elven structure is haunted, magical, or protected by fey.

### Kharadorn Empire, Dragonstone, and Khar-Dum

These are distinct settings, not one generic dwarven mountain.

**Dragonstone** is the conquered former stronghold of the fallen dragon-riding dynasty. Its established layers include ancient foundations, royal remains, and dwarven occupation. Its history and strategic occupation matter more than an invented resident monster ecology.

**Khar-Dum** is the Kharadorn ancestral homeland in the northeastern mountains. Dwarven underground settlements, clan halls, mines, and mountain infrastructure are established in broad terms.

Potential hazards for future development include mine collapse, gas, flooding, heat, poor ventilation, ordinary cave and mountain animals, vermin in stores, and deliberately selected deep subterranean threats. No specific aberration, subterranean predator, undead population, or dragon population is established for either location. A dwarven mine is a workplace with engineering, maintenance, labor, and safety concerns, not automatically a monster den.

### Edarth Western Heartlands

The expected ecology is agricultural and managed: livestock, field animals, game, predators, pests, and woodland species. Dangerous creatures are most plausible in less-settled woods, broken uplands, or abandoned places, but no named regional monster population is established.

Ecological conflicts may concern predators taking livestock, boar damaging crops, hunting rights, woodland access, animal disease, and damage to mills, fences, or stored grain.

### Veyr Northern Territories and the Ancient Barrier

Cold-weather survival, exposure, food stores, dangerous travel, and frontier logistics should dominate ordinary danger. The existence of the Ancient Barrier does not by itself establish undead, giants, or any particular monster population beyond it.

Cold-adapted animals and deliberately chosen supernatural threats may be developed later. Until approved, they remain candidates. Do not import the entire Pathfinder cold-region monster list as northern canon.

A frontier veteran may know practical warning signs that a southern courtier has never encountered, but does not possess universal knowledge of everything beyond the frontier.

### Calderan Crownlands and Valedorn

The most common creature interactions should involve domestic animals, urban pests, market animals, horses, vermin, and ordinary countryside wildlife. Royal menageries, exotic imports, or magical specimens require specific establishment.

Urban supernatural incidents should be exceptional, investigated, and politically consequential rather than routine background noise.

### Merrowen maritime territories

The sea is dangerous without requiring sea monsters. Weather, navigation, currents, ship failure, piracy, disease, hunger, and political disputes provide substantial maritime risk.

No specific sea-monster species or population is established. A reported creature sighting should remain REPORTED or RUMORED until supported. A wreck, missing ship, or strange disturbance is not proof of a monster.

### Southern Reaches

The Southwestern Marches, Southern Principalities, and Southern Islands are politically fragmented and ecologically varied. Do not assume a uniform hot desert, jungle, or tropical ecology.

Use the established distinctions: dry conditions, wells, cisterns, and caravan movement in the Marches; varied fertile and riverine settings among the Principalities; maritime and island conditions in the Southern Islands. No continent-wide southern monster population is established.

## 5. How ordinary people describe dangerous creatures

Most people do not use Pathfinder creature categories. They describe what they saw and what it did.

- "A spider big enough to take a dog" may refer to a giant spider, an exaggerated report, or a false story.
- "A frog that took a duck" may be an accurate description of a giant frog, but does not tell the witness its species or range.
- "Blood flies" may refer to stirges, ordinary biting insects, or a confused account.
- "A wolf as tall as a horse" may be exaggeration, an unusually large animal, or a rare dire individual.
- "A man who would not stay dead" may be a vampire, another undead creature, a survivor mistaken for dead, or deliberate deception.

Record the witness's words separately from the GM's identification.

## 6. Evidence and field signs

| Evidence | What it can support | What it does not prove by itself |
|---|---|---|
| Tracks | A creature passed through | Exact species, number, or current presence |
| Droppings | Diet clues and repeated use | Exact population size |
| Carcass | Predation, scavenging, disease, or violence | Identity of the killer without more evidence |
| Webbing | Spider activity | That a giant spider is currently present |
| Shed skin, feathers, or hair | A creature was present | Whether it remains nearby |
| Damaged fence or door | Force was applied | Whether cause was animal, person, weather, or monster |
| Missing livestock | Loss occurred | Predation rather than theft, escape, or disease |
| Witness account | A report worth assessing | Objective identification |
| Independent reports | Stronger reason to investigate | A confirmed species or population without corroboration |

A hunter, healer, priest, natural philosopher, soldier, and local farmer do not have identical expertise.

## 7. Monster encounters should have causes

Before placing a dangerous creature, decide:

1. What does it eat?
2. Where does it find water?
3. Where does it shelter, nest, or rest?
4. Why is it here now?
5. Why have people not removed it already?
6. Does it avoid people, tolerate them, exploit them, or actively hunt them?
7. What signs would it leave?
8. What would local people know?
9. What would be misunderstood?
10. What happens if the PCs leave it alone?

A creature may approach people because its habitat has been disturbed, prey has become scarce, food waste or livestock attracts it, seasonal conditions change its movement, or it is injured, displaced, or unusually bold.

Do not use "it is evil" as a substitute for ecology unless the creature is genuinely intelligent and morally motivated.

## 8. Intelligent creatures and social consequences

Intelligent monsters should not be treated as animals merely because they appear in a Bestiary.

Establish language, social organization (if any), needs, resources, motives, relationships with nearby communities, public knowledge, negotiation, and whether violence is ordinary, defensive, political, or exceptional.

Do not assume creature type determines moral character or social role. Do not turn every nonhuman community into a monster encounter.

## 9. Undead and supernatural phenomena

Undead are serious because ordinary people may have incomplete knowledge and because their presence intersects with funerary practice, religion, law, inheritance, and public fear.

The existence of a supernatural condition does not establish a regional outbreak. For any undead occurrence, record the actual kind of being, its origin if known, whether it is unique or part of a population, evidence, public belief, specialist knowledge, and unresolved facts.

Do not assume every old graveyard, battlefield, ruin, or site of tragedy produces undead.

## 10. Dragons are not ordinary Pathfinder dragons

The dragons of the fallen dynasty are a distinct setting element established in world history. They were enormous flying reptilian predators, physically real and capable of breathing fire. They were not spellcasters, talking sages, or interchangeable with standard Pathfinder dragon species. Their bond was exclusive to eligible members of the fallen Targaryen bloodline, with one rider bonded to one dragon through telepathic communication and influence.

Twelve existed at the dynasty's height. Most were killed during the Great Rebellion. Present-day survival remains unresolved.

Do not assign standard Pathfinder dragon age categories or spellcasting by assumption; invent a surviving population, nests, or eggs; treat rumor as proof of a sighting; or equate obsydian (ordinary black onyx) with dragonglass (a separate dragon-made material).

## 11. Registry and occurrence workflow

When a creature becomes relevant:
1. Check Creature Registry and its dedicated dossier.
2. Check the relevant regional ecology record.
3. Create an occurrence record only when evidence supports a named place.
4. Use Regional Occurrence Template.
5. Record session date and evidence for observed creatures.
6. Keep player-facing knowledge separate from GM-only truth.
7. Update frequency only when repeated evidence supports it.

Do not duplicate full Pathfinder stat blocks. Record the source and any approved changes.

## 12. Open development priorities

Future creature work should be driven by campaign needs: complete ordinary wildlife by habitat; decide whether proposed aquatic species are approved; develop specific regional bestiary entries only when a creature has a clear ecological or narrative role; add creature knowledge to NPCs based on occupation and experience; create encounter sites only after geography and access are established; and record observed creatures and consequences after play.

## 13. Core principle

The wilderness is not empty, but it is not a monster dispenser. Most signs have ordinary explanations. Some do not. Most people know their own patch of land better than distant scholars do. When something genuinely unnatural appears, nearby people should have reasons to fear it, misunderstand it, profit from it, hide it, or try to remove it.
