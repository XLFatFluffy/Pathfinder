# Tallowmere Region — Proposed Ordinary Wildlife and Natural History

**Snapshot:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Status:** PROPOSED / GM reference; not yet a canon species register  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY  
**Geographic authority:** [Regional Geography, Routes, and Points of Interest](../locations/starting_city/Regional_Geography_and_Points_of_Interest.md)  
**Ecological baseline:** [Tallowmere Regional Ecology](Tallowmere_Regional_Ecology.md)

This catalogue develops plausible ordinary wildlife for the established Tallowmere landscape. It does **not** change the established habitat baseline or confirm that every listed species occurs locally. Entries are candidate natural-history details for GM approval. A species can be used as inconsequential background description without becoming a consequential canon fact; repeated appearances, economic importance, unusual behavior, or plot relevance should be recorded and approved.

The region is not untouched wilderness. It is a productive, heavily used basin of farms, managed woodland, river traffic, quarries, shore settlements, and a walled city. Wildlife distribution should reflect that human footprint.

## 1. How to use this catalogue

- **Candidate:** a plausible species or ecological relationship, not yet confirmed.
- **Ordinary scene dressing:** low-impact description that does not establish a population, rare behavior, or plot fact.
- **Canon occurrence:** requires direct observation in play, reliable documentation, or explicit GM approval.
- **Frequency:** remains UNKNOWN until evidence supports COMMON, UNCOMMON, RARE, or SEASONAL.
- **Local belief:** a story, proverb, warning, or folk remedy is not proof that the belief is true.
- **Rules:** ordinary animals use appropriate Pathfinder First Edition statistics where needed. Do not add supernatural traits merely to make an animal more interesting.

Do not import a real-world species' entire biology uncritically if it conflicts with the setting's climate, geography, or established fantasy ecology. Conversely, do not make every animal exotic. Most rural people encounter ordinary animals far more often than monsters.

## 2. Northfields — farms, pasture, orchards, and hedgerows

**Established habitat:** open agricultural country north and northeast of Tallowmere, broken by low rises, drainage hollows, hedgerows, ditches, small copses, orchards, farm lanes, and scattered hamlets.

### Candidate mammals and birds

- **Deer:** likely to use copses, orchard margins, and field edges, especially where woodland connects to quieter ground. They avoid busy roads and open fields when people are active. Signs include tracks in soft earth, browsed shoots, droppings, and narrow paths through hedges. Hunting pressure and access rights would matter more than any assumed abundance.
- **Hares and rabbits:** plausible in rough pasture, field margins, banks, and scrub. Hares rely on speed and concealment; rabbits favor warrens in suitable well-drained ground. Burrows can damage banks and field edges, while grazing may bring them into conflict with gardeners.
- **Foxes:** adaptable hunters and scavengers. They take rodents, birds, eggs, carrion, and unattended scraps. Poultry keepers regard them as a practical nuisance, not a supernatural omen. A fox near a settlement may be hungry, habituated to refuse, or simply moving along a hedge.
- **Badgers:** nocturnal or crepuscular burrowers favoring banks and wooded margins. Their setts can be old and extensive. Farmers may tolerate them away from fields but resent damage to banks or poultry yards.
- **Field mice and voles:** important prey for owls, foxes, snakes, and other predators. Their numbers can rise around grain stores, stubble, and uncut field margins. A rodent-rich harvest can attract predators without implying an infestation.
- **Crows, rooks, and smaller songbirds:** forage in fields, orchards, and village margins. Rooks may nest colonially in tall trees; crows are adaptable scavengers. Sparrows and finches are common candidates around grain, thatch, and hedges.
- **Owls and hawks:** useful predators of rodents and smaller birds. Their presence may be welcomed by some farmers and resented by poultry keepers.

### Human relationships

The Northfields are shaped by ownership, tenancy, customary grazing, hunting rights, harvest timing, and the need to protect stored grain. Game is not automatically free for anyone to hunt. A peasant's practical access may depend on local custom, a lord's grant, or whether the animal is treated as vermin or reserved game. Avoid assuming one uniform hunting law across every holding.

Farm animals likely to be encountered include cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, horses, donkeys or mules, chickens, geese, and dogs. Their breeds, size, and quality vary by wealth and purpose. A prosperous manor may keep specialized breeding stock; a smallholder may own only a few animals and share access to a plough team.

## 3. Brackenwood — managed woodland and coppice

**Established habitat:** a worked woodland west of Tallowmere, with coppice, mature stands, clearings, charcoal burns, gathering areas, and older unmanaged pockets. Woodland rights and customary users shape access.

### Candidate wildlife

- **Deer:** browse young growth and use quieter woodland pockets. They are more likely to be seen at dawn or dusk than in the middle of a busy workday.
- **Wild boar:** plausible in larger, less disturbed patches where mast, roots, and cover are available. They are omnivorous, destructive in cultivated plots, and potentially dangerous when cornered or when a sow defends young. Their presence should not automatically mean a large sounder is nearby.
- **Foxes and badgers:** use woodland edges, clearings, and burrows. Foxes may follow charcoal burners' camps for scraps; badgers may use old banks and root systems.
- **Squirrels and small rodents:** feed on nuts, seeds, fungi, and other woodland resources. Their activity varies with mast seasons.
- **Owls, hawks, woodpeckers, crows, and songbirds:** occupy different woodland layers and habitats. Woodpecker drumming, alarm calls, and sudden silence can be useful sensory details, but should not be treated as reliable supernatural warnings.
- **Bats:** may roost in hollow trees, old structures, or suitable caves. Their presence is ordinary wildlife, not evidence of vampirism or undead activity.
- **Amphibians and reptiles:** may occur around damp hollows, streams, leaf litter, and sunlit clearings. Exact species remain open.

### Woodland ecology and work

Coppicing creates a patchwork of young regrowth, mature standards, brush piles, and open rides. Charcoal burns temporarily disturb small areas and leave ash-rich clearings. Timber cutting, pole gathering, pannage, hunting, and plant gathering alter animal movement. Wildlife may avoid a newly active work site and return after it quiets.

A woodland path can be familiar to charcoal burners and nearly invisible to a city visitor. Local guides recognize coppice boundaries, old stumps, cut marks, streams, and the smell of a recent burn. A stranger's lostness is more likely to result from poor local knowledge and shifting work paths than from enchanted woods.

## 4. River Vale and tributaries — a working freshwater system

**Established habitat:** a navigable lower river, slower pools, gravel bars, mill reaches, seasonal streams, flood meadows, drainage channels, and tributaries. Barges, mills, ferries, fishing, and riverbank settlements create regular human activity.

### Candidate fish and aquatic life

- **Carp and other hardy freshwater fish:** plausible in slower pools, backwaters, and lower reaches. They may be caught by net, line, trap, or managed pond systems.
- **Pike or comparable predatory fish:** possible in weedy margins and slower water. They are valued as food but can make shallow fishing places less pleasant for children and livestock.
- **Eels:** plausible in the river system, though migration and life cycle details should be set consistently if they become important to trade or local lore.
- **Small fish and invertebrates:** support larger fish, birds, and mammals. Most are not individually named in local records.
- **Frogs and other amphibians:** likely candidates around ditches, ponds, flood meadows, and quiet tributaries.
- **Ducks, geese, herons, and kingfisher-like birds:** may use quiet reaches, banks, and shallows. Herons hunt patiently in shallow water; diving birds favor fish-rich reaches.
- **Otters:** possible along quieter banks and less disturbed tributaries. Their tracks, slides, and feeding remains can be mistaken for signs of other animals by inexperienced observers.
- **Beavers or similar water-engineering mammals:** possible in quieter, less managed reaches, but remain an optional and potentially consequential addition. If established, their dams would affect drainage, land use, mills, and local disputes. Do not casually introduce them without considering those consequences.

### River food, work, and hazards

Fishing is not a single uniform occupation. Some households fish for their own table; others sell catches, work from boats, supply inns, or hold specific rights to a reach, weir, or landing. Mill structures, ferry traffic, barge schedules, seasonal water levels, and private fishing rights can conflict.

Floods change the river. They carry silt, uproot bankside growth, move gravel, leave debris, and contaminate shallow wells. Fish may be easier or harder to catch depending on water level and clarity. After heavy rain, currents can be deceptive even where the surface appears calm.

## 5. Greyback Ridges and quarry country

**Established habitat:** low stony hills, exposed ridges, worked quarry faces, prospecting cuts, caves, and rough upland margins. The region is not high mountain country.

### Candidate wildlife

- **Foxes and badgers:** may use scrubby slopes, banks, and the edges of worked ground.
- **Ravens and other scavenging birds:** can exploit carrion, quarry refuse, and exposed nesting ledges. Their presence is ordinary unless a specific unusual pattern is established.
- **Hawks and smaller raptors:** may hunt over open slopes and quarry margins.
- **Bats:** suitable caves, cracks, and abandoned workings can provide roosts. A roost is not a lair.
- **Snakes and lizards:** plausible in warmer, exposed stone and scrub. Whether venomous species exist locally is unresolved; do not imply that every bite is lethal.
- **Sure-footed grazing animals:** wild goats or similar animals are possible only if the local terrain and broader regional ecology support them. The Greybacks are low ridges, not alpine mountains; do not automatically populate them with mountain fauna.

### Quarry interaction

Quarry work displaces animals through noise, dust, blasting or stone-breaking, traffic, and the removal of shelter. Abandoned cuts may collect water and become dangerous to livestock or children. Loose stone, unstable faces, and hidden shafts are sufficient hazards without adding monsters.

## 6. Eastern Shore — coves, bluffs, and salt-working places

**Established habitat:** low bluffs, narrow beaches, shingle coves, small landing places, salt-working sites, fishing huts, and exposed water subject to sudden storms.

### Candidate wildlife

- **Gulls and other seabirds:** scavenge around boats, fish-cleaning places, landings, and refuse. They are noisy, opportunistic, and familiar to shore households.
- **Cormorants or similar diving birds:** may fish in deeper water and rest on exposed rocks or posts.
- **Shore crabs, shellfish, and molluscs:** plausible in suitable rocky, sandy, and shingle habitats. Harvesting rights and seasonal caution may shape local practice.
- **Coastal fish:** species and seasonal runs remain open. A local fishery should be developed around actual food demand, boats, preservation, and market access rather than an arbitrary list of exotic fish.
- **Seals or other coastal mammals:** possible if consistent with the inland sea's wider ecology, but not yet established.
- **Coastal scavengers and insects:** gather around fish waste, salt works, and settlements.

Storms are a known hazard. They can strand boats, move shingle, damage landing places, and expose wreckage. A storm does not imply a curse, sea monster, or divine displeasure.

## 7. Tallowmere — urban and commensal wildlife

**Established habitat:** harbor, warehouses, streets, refuse, cellars, gardens, granaries, stables, and the decentralized Underways.

Candidate urban animals include rats, mice, pigeons, sparrows, flies, beetles, stray cats and dogs, and animals associated with stables and markets. Their abundance depends on refuse handling, grain storage, building maintenance, predators, and seasonal weather.

- **Rats and mice** exploit food stores, warm cavities, drains, cellars, and neglected structures. Their presence is a sanitation and storage issue, not proof of ratfolk activity.
- **Cats** may be kept for companionship or rodent control, but no single citywide policy or organized cat guild is established.
- **Dogs** serve as guards, herders, hunting companions, scavengers, and household animals. Strays may form loose groups around food sources.
- **Pigeons and small birds** nest in roofs, ledges, towers, and warehouses. Some may be kept for food or message use, but a formal messenger-pigeon network is not established.
- **Flies and other insects** gather around food waste, dung, drains, fish markets, and poorly maintained stables. Their seasonal abundance is a practical nuisance.
- **Stable animals** include horses, mules, donkeys, and possibly oxen used for hauling. Their feed, dung, injuries, and disposal are everyday urban concerns.

Urban pests reveal failures in infrastructure: leaking grain stores, blocked drains, careless refuse disposal, damaged roofs, and overcrowded cellars. A sudden increase should have a mundane cause unless play establishes otherwise.

## 8. The outer frontier belt — less-managed habitats beyond the hinterland

The frontier environments are established in [Wilderness Beyond Tallowmere](../locations/starting_city/Wilderness_Beyond_Tallowmere.md). The candidate wildlife below is consistent with those descriptions, but remains PROPOSED unless a specific occurrence is approved or observed. The frontier is less continuously worked and protected than the settled basin; it is not uniformly dangerous.

### Deep Bracken — older woodland

The Deep Bracken continues beyond the managed Brackenwood. Older stands, denser cover, abandoned clearings, and fewer maintained paths allow wildlife to use larger and quieter territories.

- **Deer, wild boar, foxes, and badgers** are the ordinary mammals already identified as plausible or common in the wider woodland framework. Their signs may be easier to find than the animals themselves.
- **Wolves** are identified in the wilderness framework as common animals in the deeper woodland. They should be treated as wary predators with territory, prey needs, and reasons to avoid people, not as creatures that automatically attack every traveler.
- **Owls, hawks, ravens, woodpeckers, bats, and small woodland birds** remain plausible, with local variation by tree cover and disturbance.
- **Mature-tree fungi, deadwood insects, and cavity-nesting animals** may be more evident in older stands than in regularly coppiced woodland.

Hunting, trapping, timber cutting, and gathering are possible, but paths, rights, and local knowledge become less reliable farther from settled holdings. A wolf sign does not establish a pack at the site; a carcass does not establish a permanent hunting ground.

### Upper Vale — headwaters and upland pasture

The Upper Vale contains stony streams, ravines, broken hills, upland pasture, and scattered shepherd shelters. It is not uniformly high mountain country.

- **Wolves, boar, and foxes** are plausible around woodland margins, grazing routes, and quieter valleys.
- **Bears and mountain cats** are possible in suitable remote cover, as identified by the wilderness framework. Their presence should be tied to appropriate terrain and prey rather than assumed throughout the valley.
- **Deer and other grazing or browsing animals** may use sheltered slopes and woodland edges.
- **Raptors and large scavenging birds** may use cliffs and open slopes; the wilderness reference lists giant eagles as a possible Pathfinder threat, not ordinary background fauna.
- **Freshwater fish and aquatic life** may occur in suitable reaches, but fast headwater streams should not be treated as equivalent to the lower navigable River Vale.

Livestock, shepherds, and seasonal grazing shape the valley. Predation, lost animals, difficult crossings, and weather can create conflict without requiring a monster encounter.

### Greyback Heights — broken uplands beyond the quarries

The Heights are rougher and more exposed than the low Greyback Ridges near Stonehollow. The wilderness framework identifies bears, mountain lions, giant eagles, monstrous vermin, and cave-dwelling creatures as possible threats.

Ordinary candidate wildlife includes foxes, badgers, ravens, hawks, smaller birds, rodents, and reptiles suited to exposed stone and scrub. Grazing animals may use accessible upland pasture. Do not populate the Heights with alpine species merely because the terrain is rough; the exact elevation and climate are not fully established.

Quarrying and prospecting disturb habitat. Abandoned cuts can provide shelter or trap animals, but a cave or adit is not automatically a lair. Tracks near a shaft may belong to an animal using it for shelter rather than a creature living permanently underground.

### Siltfen — wet meadows, reed beds, and channels

The Siltfen is a working wetland of shallow pools, muddy channels, reed beds, alder stands, wet meadows, and firmer ridges. Its ordinary ecology should be built around water level, vegetation, insects, fish, and waterfowl.

- **Frogs and other amphibians** are strong candidates around pools and wet meadows.
- **Waterfowl and wading birds** may use shallow channels and open pools; fish-eating birds follow suitable water.
- **Eels and other freshwater fish** are candidates in connected channels, though exact species and seasonal movements remain open.
- **Snakes, small mammals, and wetland insects** may occupy reed margins and drier hummocks.
- **Otters** are possible along quieter connected waterways, but are not confirmed.
- The wilderness framework mentions giant frogs, giant insects, stirges, and crocodiles only in suitable warmer waters as possible Pathfinder threats. These are not ordinary fauna or confirmed populations. Do not assume crocodiles inhabit the Siltfen without a deliberate climate and canon decision.

Reed cutting, fishing, seasonal grazing, and boat access alter habitat and create competing rights. Floodwater may erase paths and change where animals feed. A wetland that looks empty may still be full of calls, insects, movement in reeds, and signs at the water's edge.

### Outer Eastern Shore — exposed coast beyond Saltwick

Beyond the more regularly used Saltwick shore, isolated coves, shingle beaches, low cliffs, and temporary fishing camps provide habitat under harsher exposure.

Gulls and other seabirds, shore crabs, shellfish, coastal fish, and scavenging birds remain plausible. Seals or similar coastal mammals are possible but unconfirmed. The exact marine and shoreline species are unresolved. Do not assume every cove supports the same fishery or that a wreck site has a resident predator.

Storms, surf, cliff erosion, and limited fresh water shape both wildlife and human access. Wreck salvage, fishing, and temporary camps may leave food waste that attracts scavengers. A sudden concentration of birds or scavengers can have a mundane explanation such as a fish run, discarded catch, or carcass.

The frontier wildlife list is not a monster-placement map. Any specific lair, nest, breeding site, or dangerous population must be established separately.

## 9. Seasonal patterns — working expectations, not fixed calendar law

The calendar's month names and seasonal framework are established elsewhere. The exact local timing of migrations, breeding, spawning, and harvest-related animal behavior remains open. The following are broad expectations for a temperate agricultural region and should be adjusted to the campaign's actual weather.

- **Late winter and thaw:** animals seek accessible food; muddy tracks and swollen streams make signs easier to see but travel harder. Livestock are vulnerable if winter fodder runs short.
- **Spring:** nesting, breeding, lambing, calving, and new growth increase activity. Ditches and pools may be full. Young animals are vulnerable to dogs, foxes, and human disturbance.
- **Summer:** insects increase; water availability matters; shade and reliable watering places attract livestock and wildlife. Long daylight supports more work and travel.
- **Harvestfall:** grain, fruit, nuts, and other food become abundant in fields and woodland. Rodents and scavengers exploit stores and fallen crops. Hunting pressure may rise, but harvest labor competes for time.
- **Duskreach and deep winter:** food becomes scarcer; animals may move closer to settlements or rely on stored food and sheltered dens. Storms and cold can cause livestock losses and make carcasses a disease and scavenger concern.

Do not create a rigid migration timetable unless a particular species becomes important. Weather, local land use, and food availability should matter more than a universal month-by-month chart.

## 9. Tracks, signs, and field knowledge

Ordinary people do not need formal natural philosophy to recognize useful signs. Farmers, foresters, hunters, fishers, shepherds, and carters build practical knowledge through repeated work.

Useful sensory clues include:
- **Tracks:** shape, size, gait, direction, and whether prints overlap older mud or snow.
- **Droppings and feeding signs:** browsed shoots, gnawed grain, feathers, fish remains, burrows, and disturbed soil.
- **Calls:** alarm calls, mating calls, flock disturbance, and the sudden absence of ordinary noise.
- **Scent:** fox musk, wet dog, dung, rotting fish, carrion, smoke, and stagnant water.
- **Habitat disturbance:** trampled grass, broken branches, burrow spoil, scratched bark, disturbed nests, and displaced stones.
- **Season and time:** tracks and behavior mean different things during breeding, harvest, drought, flood, or deep winter.

A skilled local can often identify the likely animal but may still misjudge its size, number, age, or intent. A frightened witness is not a field guide.

## 10. Predation, carrion, and ecological balance

The candidate fauna suggests ordinary food webs rather than a wilderness full of apex predators:

- Small rodents and birds feed on grain, seeds, fruit, and insects.
- Foxes, owls, hawks, snakes, and other predators feed on smaller animals.
- Deer and other herbivores browse woodland margins and field edges.
- Boar and badgers forage broadly, including roots, invertebrates, carrion, and human food where available.
- Gulls, crows, ravens, foxes, dogs, and insects exploit carrion and refuse.
- Fish, birds, amphibians, and aquatic invertebrates are linked through the river and shore food webs.

These relationships are plausible ecological guidance, not confirmed local population data. Avoid claiming that the disappearance of one species will cause a specific regional crisis without evidence about numbers and dependencies.

## 11. Folklore and practical belief

No specific Tallowmere animal folklore is canonized by this catalogue. When local beliefs are needed, build them from occupation and lived experience rather than generic fantasy omens. Examples that may be proposed for a particular NPC or community, but are not established setting facts:

- A farmer may treat a fox repeatedly visiting a poultry yard as a warning to mend fences, not as a prophecy.
- A bargeman may distrust a familiar pool after heavy rain because the current changes beneath the surface.
- A charcoal burner may use bird calls to judge whether a clearing is disturbed, while admitting that wind and work noise make the signs unreliable.
- A fisher may avoid a cove during a particular wind because of dangerous surf, not because it is haunted.
- A stablehand may have remedies for minor bites, hoof injuries, and insect irritation, but such remedies are not guaranteed cures.

Folk remedies should be treated as beliefs or practical care, not automatically as effective medicine. Do not give ordinary animals supernatural knowledge or powers without a separate canon decision.

## 12. Disease, injury, and animal care

Animal husbandry is part of the region's economy. Common practical concerns include wounds, parasites, poor feed, hoof problems, birthing complications, spoiled fodder, infected bites, and animals weakened by hard work or winter. The exact diseases, veterinary knowledge, and treatment traditions remain to be developed.

Do not casually introduce a devastating livestock epidemic. Such an event would affect food supply, trade, rents, transport, military logistics, and household survival. It requires deliberate GM approval and a coherent timeline.

Carcasses are not simply scenery. They attract scavengers, create odor, may contaminate water, and require removal or burial near settlements. Disposal practices vary by wealth, custom, and local authority.

## 13. Supernatural boundary

Ordinary wildlife is not automatically a vehicle for supernatural clues. In particular:

- Bats are not evidence of vampires.
- Ravens and crows are not automatically omens or spies.
- Unusual animal behavior may have mundane causes: weather, hunger, injury, breeding, disturbance, or predators.
- Caves, quarry cuts, old trees, and abandoned clearings do not establish monster lairs.
- The planned Session One troll encounter is a specific prepared event, not evidence of a local troll population.
- No regional magical-beast population, undead ecology, dragon survival, or supernatural migration is established by this catalogue.

## 14. Open decisions and future development

The following should be decided only when they materially affect play:

1. Which candidate species are approved as ordinary regional fauna?
2. Which fish and shellfish support Tallowmere's markets and shore households?
3. Are beavers or similar water-engineering animals present, and if so, where are their dams tolerated or contested?
4. Are wild boar common enough to be a meaningful hunting or crop-protection issue?
5. Which game and fishing rights are held by lords, chartered communities, or customary users?
6. What animal diseases and practical veterinary traditions are locally recognized?
7. Are any local animal sayings, seasonal customs, or folk remedies worth recording?

Until those decisions are made, keep species occurrence and frequency unconfirmed. The goal is a believable countryside, not an encyclopedia that mistakes plausible for true.
