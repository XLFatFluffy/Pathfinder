# River Vale and Siltfen — Aquatic Ecology and Working Waters

**Status:** ESTABLISHED RESOURCE FRAMEWORK / PROPOSED SPECIES DETAILS  
**Snapshot:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY  
**Scope:** River Vale watershed, lower river, and Siltfen wetland  
**Authorities:** [Regional Geography and Points of Interest](../locations/starting_city/Regional_Geography_and_Points_of_Interest.md); [Wilderness Beyond Tallowmere](../locations/starting_city/Wilderness_Beyond_Tallowmere.md); [Tallowmere Regional Ecology](Tallowmere_Regional_Ecology.md)

## 1. Purpose and canon boundary

This record develops the practical ecology of the waters that support Tallowmere and its hinterland. It is intended to help portray fishing, river work, wetland gathering, food supply, travel, and the knowledge held by people who work around water.

The underlying resource framework is established:
- The River Vale is a navigable lower river with slower pools, gravel bars, mill reaches, seasonal streams, flood meadows, drainage channels, tributaries, barges, mills, ferries, fishing, and riverbank settlements.
- The river supports fishing hamlets and small landing places. Bellwater Pool is a known deep, slow pool where bargemen wait or turn when conditions permit and children fish.
- The Siltfen is a wetland south and southeast of the lower River Vale, with wet meadows, reed beds, shallow pools, muddy channels, alder stands, firmer ridges, and seasonally flooded ground.
- People use the Siltfen for reed cutting, fishing, waterfowl, useful plant gathering, and limited grazing on firmer ground. The wilderness record specifically names eels and frogs among its ordinary resources.
- Siltfen grazing, reed-cutting, and fishing rights overlap. Exact jurisdiction and the boundaries of individual rights remain unresolved.

These statements establish resource use and habitat, not a complete species inventory. No particular fish species, bird species, catch volume, fishery season, price, fishing method, or formal fishing institution is established by this document. Species examples below are proposals for portrayal, not canon.

## 2. Two connected but different waters

### The River Vale

The River Vale is a working river, not a wilderness stream. Its upper branches rise in the Western Uplands and join in a wooded valley west of Brackenholt. The river then travels east and southeast through the inhabited basin. Millhaven occupies a reliable mill reach; Fordmere lies farther upstream where the channel narrows and crossing is dependable. Below Millhaven, the river broadens, slows, and divides around low islands and gravel bars before reaching Tallowmere's harbor.

The lower river supports barges under ordinary conditions. Smaller, lightly loaded craft can travel farther upstream, though water level, towpaths, and local pilots matter. Mills, ferries, landing places, drainage works, and river traffic make the river a managed and contested resource.

The river's ecological character changes along its course:
- Faster, stonier upper reaches are not interchangeable with the slower lower river.
- Mill reaches have altered flow, banks, and access.
- Pools and backwaters offer calmer water than the main channel.
- Gravel bars and low islands shift the usable channels and create shallow margins.
- Flood meadows connect the river to fields and drainage channels during high water.

### The Siltfen

The Siltfen is a low wetland basin south and southeast of the lower River Vale, associated with a slow tributary system. It is not an endless or uniformly deep swamp. It contains shallow pools, muddy channels, reed beds, wet meadows, alder stands, firmer ridges, and patches of dry ground.

Its water levels and routes change with season and weather. Some areas are easier to cross on foot; others are more practical by small boat. Local knowledge matters because the shortest visible route may cross soft ground or a channel too deep to ford safely.

The River Vale and Siltfen are connected through the wider watershed, but their conditions differ. Do not assume that a fish, bird, or plant suited to a quiet wetland pool is equally common in a navigable river channel.

## 3. Aquatic life: what is established and what remains open

### Established resource categories

- **River Vale:** fishing is an established activity. The exact catch is not recorded.
- **Siltfen:** fishing is established, and eels and frogs are specifically named as ordinary resources in the wilderness framework.
- **Waterfowl:** harvesting waterfowl is an established Siltfen activity. No species or season is specified.
- **Wetland plants:** useful plant gathering is established. The exact species and gathering customs remain open.
- **Reeds:** reed cutting is established and supplies thatch, mats, and basketry according to the wilderness record.
- **Grazing:** limited seasonal or local grazing on firmer Siltfen ground is established; the precise stock, schedule, and rights are not.

### Candidate fish and other animals

The following are ecological candidates only. They may be useful when the GM needs ordinary background life, but should not be treated as established local populations until approved or observed.

- **Carp or other hardy fish:** plausible in slower pools, backwaters, and sheltered channels.
- **Pike or another ambush predator:** plausible in weedy margins and quiet water. If used, it is a food animal and part of the ordinary food web, not a monster.
- **Small schooling fish:** plausible forage for larger fish and fish-eating birds; local names and species are unknown.
- **Eels:** established as a Siltfen resource, but their precise species, abundance, migration, and capture practices are unknown.
- **Frogs:** established as a Siltfen resource category. Species, size, abundance, and whether they are eaten regularly or only gathered opportunistically are unresolved.
- **Other amphibians:** plausible in pools, ditches, and wet meadows, but not specifically established.
- **Waterfowl and wading birds:** established as a harvested resource category. Ducks, geese, herons, and other familiar wetland birds are candidate examples, not approved species records.
- **Fish-eating mammals such as otters:** possible along quieter connected waterways, but unconfirmed. Do not add them merely as decorative wildlife if their presence would create meaningful consequences for fishing households.
- **Aquatic invertebrates:** insects, larvae, molluscs, and small crustaceans would form part of a functioning wetland food web. Exact local species and uses are not established.

No crocodiles, giant frogs, giant insects, stirges, or other Pathfinder threats are ordinary Siltfen fauna by default. The wilderness record lists some as possible threats in suitable circumstances; it does not confirm their presence.

## 4. Fishing as work and household practice

Fishing should be portrayed as a varied livelihood rather than a single uniform profession.

Possible forms of participation include:
- A household member setting a line or small trap for food.
- A fisher working from a small boat or bank-side position.
- A crew using nets where the channel and local rights permit.
- A market seller buying catches from several independent fishers.
- A boat crew carrying fish with other cargo to a settlement or market.
- A specialist supplying inns, households, or a particular market stall.

These are general working possibilities, not confirmed Tallowmere occupational structures. No named River Vale fishers' guild, universal fishing license, fixed toll, or standard division of catch is established.

Fishing depends on access, water level, equipment, weather, skill, and rights. A net needs repair; a line and hook can be carried by an individual; traps require placement and checking; boats require maintenance and safe landings. The method should fit the reach and the fisher's resources. Do not assign precise catch rates or guaranteed daily income.

A fisher's useful knowledge may include:
- Where a channel is shallow, deep, fast, or obstructed.
- How a recent rain has changed the current.
- Which landing is usable at the present water level.
- Where nets are likely to snag.
- Whether a fish is fresh, damaged, or beginning to spoil.
- Which stretches are claimed, disputed, or customarily shared.

The exact local rights remain open. The Siltfen's overlapping rights are established, but the legal source, holders, boundaries, and remedies for disputes are not. River fishing rights elsewhere should not be assumed to follow one uniform rule.

## 5. Reed, wetland, and seasonal work

Reed cutting is an established Siltfen activity. Reeds are used for thatch, mats, and basketry. Cutting, drying, bundling, carrying, and storage require labor and suitable weather. The exact local cutting season and access rules are unresolved.

Wetland households may combine several forms of work rather than depend on one resource:
- Reed cutting and simple craft production.
- Fishing and waterfowl gathering.
- Gathering useful plants.
- Grazing animals on firmer ground.
- Small-scale farming or other work on nearby dry ground.
- Carrying goods by boat or along firm tracks where available.

The Siltfen should not be treated as a resource-rich wilderness that anyone can freely exploit. Rights overlap, access depends on conditions, and people who work there have practical reasons to protect familiar channels, cutting areas, landing places, and grazing patches.

Peat and clay are mentioned as possible limited resources in the wilderness framework. Their exact sites, scale of extraction, commercial importance, and ownership are not established. Do not turn them into major industries without a separate decision.

## 6. Food handling, preservation, and trade

Fresh fish is perishable. A catch intended for a distant market must be sold or prepared quickly. Depending on household means and local practice, fish may be eaten fresh, cooked soon after capture, salted, smoked, dried, or carried live where the species and journey allow. These are general preservation possibilities, not a confirmed Tallowmere recipe or regulated trade.

Preservation requires salt, fuel, time, suitable space, and reliable handling. Salt is available as a regional economic activity on the Eastern Shore, but this record does not establish that Siltfen fishers buy from a particular saltworks or that fish is a major export.

The lower River Vale gives Tallowmere a plausible route for moving perishable goods, but cargo capacity, travel time, and spoilage depend on weather, boat type, current, handling, and market demand. Do not invent a standard fish price or volume. The city's established market and commercial records govern actual trade practice.

A useful portrayal distinction:
- A household catch may be modest and consumed locally.
- A market catch must be sorted, carried, and sold while fresh.
- A preserved catch can travel farther but requires labor and materials.
- A poor catch affects a household first; a prolonged disruption may affect market supply more broadly.

These are practical economic relationships, not statements that Tallowmere currently faces a fish shortage or that any particular household is prosperous.

## 7. Water, flood, and health

Water is useful and dangerous. The River Vale can rise with spring melt and prolonged autumn rain. Flooding may affect low meadows, riverside stores, roads, crossings, and shallow wells. Floodwater can carry silt, debris, animal waste, and refuse. The Siltfen's channels and paths can change or become difficult to read after high water.

Standing water and wet ground create ordinary hazards:
- Insects and biting flies can make work miserable.
- Mud, hidden channels, and soft banks can cause injury or loss of equipment.
- Contaminated water can cause illness.
- Wet clothing, cold exposure, and prolonged damp can worsen ordinary sickness.
- Fish and other food can spoil if handled or stored poorly.

These are environmental risks, not proof of a local epidemic, curse, or supernatural contamination. The specific diseases present in the region are handled in the campaign's medicine and disease records. Do not diagnose an illness from a vague description of bad water without evidence.

Millers, fishers, farmers, ferrymen, and wetland workers may disagree over water use. A mill's flow, a blocked drainage channel, a damaged bank, a ferry landing, or a poorly maintained crossing can affect other users. The exact local legal remedies and responsible authorities must be drawn from the relevant charter and landholding records, not invented here.

## 8. What different people know

| Person | Practical knowledge likely to be useful | What they do not automatically know |
|---|---|---|
| River fisher | Local current, usable banks, familiar catches, net snags, spoilage, customary access | The full watershed, legal history of every right, distant species inventories |
| Siltfen reed cutter | Firm ground, safe routes, reed stands, water depth by season, drying and bundling | Every channel's ownership, hidden history, exact flood forecasts |
| Ferryman or bargeman | Navigable channels, landing conditions, current, cargo handling, weather signs | Every fishery boundary or wetland plant use |
| Miller | Mill flow, water levels, silt, damage to works, grain traffic | Fish populations beyond the mill reach |
| Farmer near the floodplain | Field drainage, flood marks, hay quality, livestock access, water contamination | The cause of every upstream change |
| Market seller or innkeeper | Quality, freshness, demand, storage, reliable suppliers | Where every catch was taken or whether a fisher's claim of origin is true |
| City official | Recorded charters, tolls, complaints, recognized jurisdictions | Informal customs that were never reported or recorded |

This is a guide to occupational knowledge, not a universal literacy or education rule. Individuals vary.

## 9. Sensory portrayal

### River Vale
- A slow reach may carry the smell of wet stone, mud, river weed, fish, and mill refuse.
- A barge pole strikes gravel or finds unexpected depth beside a familiar bank.
- Water moves quietly over a shallow bar while the deeper current pulls harder beneath it.
- Nets dry near a landing; rope, wet wood, tar, grain dust, and fish scales mingle around working boats.
- After rain, the river runs cloudier and carries branches, straw, and field debris.

### Siltfen
- Reeds move in separate bands as wind crosses shallow water.
- Frogs call from pools; insects gather above still margins.
- A footstep that seems firm may sink through a thin crust of vegetation into mud.
- Waterfowl lift suddenly from a pool, leaving ripples and broken reflections.
- Cut reeds lie in bundles on a firmer ridge, drying before transport.
- Mist can hide the far bank without making the wetland supernatural.

These are portrayal prompts, not claims that every scene contains every detail.

## 10. Deliberately unresolved

Do not fill these in without a reason:
- Exact fish and waterfowl species.
- Whether eels are abundant, seasonal, or a minor supplementary catch.
- Whether frogs are eaten regularly, gathered opportunistically, or simply recognized as part of the wetland's resources.
- Specific fishing methods, gear designs, and local craft terminology.
- The identity of fishing households, boat crews, and wetland gatherers not already named elsewhere.
- Exact fishing, reed-cutting, grazing, and landing rights; their holders and boundaries.
- Local catch volumes, prices, taxes, guild rules, and market shares.
- The timing of spawning, migration, and waterfowl harvest.
- The presence of otters, beavers, crocodiles, or any unusual aquatic animal.
- Any current fishery dispute, disease outbreak, flood disaster, or pollution event.

The purpose is not to make the river and wetland feel empty. Their ordinary work is already established. The purpose is to leave the unrecorded details open until the campaign has a reason to care about them.
