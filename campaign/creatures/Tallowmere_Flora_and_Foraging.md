# Tallowmere Region — Flora, Foraging, and Useful Plants

**Status:** PROPOSED GM natural-history reference; plant occurrences are not yet a canon species register  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY  
**Geographic authority:** `../locations/starting_city/Regional_Geography_and_Points_of_Interest.md`  
**Ecological baseline:** `Tallowmere_Regional_Ecology.md`  
**Related records:** `Regional_Wildlife_Catalogue.md`, `../world/Everyday_Life_and_Material_Culture.md`

This record develops the plant life of the Tallowmere region as a working landscape. Its purpose is to help describe farms, hedgerows, woodland, riverbanks, shore settlements, and the outer countryside, and to give foraging and gathering a believable place in ordinary life.

**Nothing in this catalogue confirms that every named plant grows locally.** Species and uses below are plausible candidates for the established temperate, agricultural, river-and-coast landscape. Treat them as GM proposals until approved or observed in play. Do not assign exact abundance, harvest rights, market prices, or magical properties from this document.

## 1. The cultivated landscape

Tallowmere's countryside is not divided into neat blocks of untouched nature and farmland. Fields, pasture, hedges, orchards, coppice, drainage ditches, roads, streams, and worked woodland meet and overlap.

The established regional economy supports the following broad plant products:

- Grain, flour, oats, and seasonal legumes from the Millhaven and Northfields agricultural district.
- Orchard produce and garden vegetables from farms and household plots.
- Timber, poles, charcoal wood, firewood, and gathered plants from the Brackenwood.
- Reeds and wetland plants from the Siltfen and lower river margins.
- Salt-tolerant shore vegetation and gathered coastal plants where conditions allow.
- Hay, straw, bedding, thatching material, and animal fodder from managed holdings.

The exact crop varieties, orchard species, planting dates, and yields remain open. The names of settlements and their economic roles do not establish a complete agricultural census.

## 2. Northfields — field margins, orchards, and pasture

The Northfields contain the most intensively managed plant communities in the immediate region. Their character comes as much from boundaries and neglected corners as from the crops themselves.

### Candidate hedgerow plants

- **Hawthorn:** dense thorny hedges, useful as livestock barriers and nesting cover. Flowering and fruiting can mark the passing season. Its branches are difficult to force through and make practical boundary material.
- **Blackthorn:** another thorny hedge shrub, often flowering before its leaves. Its dense growth can make old boundaries difficult to cross. Fruit may be gathered after ripening, but local food customs are not yet established.
- **Hazel:** suitable in hedges and coppiced margins. Nuts are useful food; straight shoots can serve as rods, handles, hurdles, and basket material.
- **Bramble:** common candidate along rough boundaries and abandoned corners. Blackberries can be eaten fresh or preserved; thorny growth catches clothing and can conceal holes or discarded objects.
- **Elder:** may grow near farms, lanes, and damp boundaries. Flowers and berries have culinary uses when properly prepared. Different parts of the plant are not interchangeable as food.
- **Nettles:** thrive on disturbed, fertile ground near yards, manure heaps, and neglected field edges. Young leaves can be cooked; mature plants are a nuisance to walk through and may be cut for practical household uses.
- **Plantain and dandelion-like weeds:** familiar plants of paths, yards, and trampled ground. Some leaves may be used as food or in household remedies, but a plant's reputation is not proof of medical effectiveness.

### Orchards and gardens

The existence of orchards is established; their exact varieties are not. Apples and pears are plausible candidates, with plums or similar stone fruit possible in suitable sheltered holdings. A prosperous manor may maintain grafted trees and a more deliberate orchard than a small tenant farm.

Orchard work includes pruning, grafting, guarding fruit, gathering fallen produce, drying, pressing, and storing. Windfall fruit is not necessarily free for passersby to take. Ownership, tenancy, and customary gleaning rights matter.

Household gardens may contain onions, leeks, cabbages, beans, peas, turnips, carrots or similar roots, culinary herbs, and a few fruit bushes. A small garden is often more important for reliable household variety than for large market sales.

### Field weeds and soil

Farmers know the plants that compete with crops, foul seed, poison livestock, or indicate poor drainage. They may not classify them formally, but they recognize which weeds are difficult to remove, which return after cutting, and which appear on neglected land.

A field's vegetation can reveal its use: trampled gateways, nettle-rich manure corners, rushy wet ground, compacted cart tracks, and scrub reclaiming an abandoned plot. These are useful visual details, not automatic clues to hidden events.

## 3. Brackenwood — coppice, mature trees, and gathered growth

Brackenwood is a managed woodland, not a uniform stand of trees. Coppice cycles, mature timber trees, charcoal clearings, paths, and older pockets create different conditions within a short walk.

### Candidate trees and woodland plants

- **Oak:** valuable for durable timber, beams, tools, and other heavy work. Mature trees are long-term assets; cutting one may require permission and a reason.
- **Ash:** useful straight timber for handles, shafts, and tools. Its presence in a hedge or woodlot may also be reflected in local place-names, though no special sacred status is established.
- **Alder:** a candidate for damp ground and stream margins. It tolerates wet conditions and can provide useful wood for certain local purposes.
- **Willow:** grows readily near water and can be cut for flexible rods, bindings, hurdles, and basketry.
- **Birch:** may occur in lighter or disturbed woodland. Its bark and small timber have practical uses, while young growth can colonize clearings.
- **Hazel and other coppice shrubs:** provide rods, stakes, nuts, and repeated small harvests without felling large timber trees.
- **Ferns, bracken, mosses, and woodland groundcover:** shape the forest floor and provide shelter for small animals. Bracken can be cut for bedding or other uses where local practice permits.
- **Mushrooms and other fungi:** appear in suitable woodland conditions, but identification is a specialist skill. Similar-looking species can differ sharply in safety.

### Woodland work and plant succession

Coppicing is a cycle of cutting selected stems near the base and allowing regrowth. It produces a changing mosaic: recently cut stools, dense young shoots, older poles, and mature standards left to grow. The cycle supports recurring supplies of small timber and rods while preserving selected larger trees.

Charcoal burning requires suitable wood, dry weather, labor, and a controlled burn. The work leaves earth-covered charcoal stacks, ash, disturbed soil, and temporary clearings. A recently worked clearing looks different from a mature stand and may attract different plants and animals.

Woodland gathering is governed by access. A person may have permission to collect fallen branches but not cut a living tree, gather nuts from a reserved stand, or take bark from a managed tree. Customary rights vary between holdings and should not be treated as one universal rule.

## 4. River Vale, tributaries, and wet ground

The River Vale and its tributaries create a shifting border between cultivated land and wet habitats. Flood meadows, ditches, mill channels, backwaters, and stream margins support plants that tolerate wet soil or periodic inundation.

### Candidate riverbank and wetland plants

- **Reeds:** useful for thatching, mats, screens, basketry, and light construction. Cutting at the wrong time or in the wrong place can damage future growth or conflict with local rights.
- **Rushes and sedges:** common candidates in wet meadows and ditch margins. They may be used for simple floor coverings, bindings, animal bedding, or thatching mixtures depending on the plant and local skill.
- **Willow and alder:** likely candidates on suitable banks and damp ground. Roots help bind soil, while branches and timber have practical uses.
- **Iris-like wetland flowers and flowering herbs:** possible in quiet margins and pools. Their visual presence can distinguish a healthy wet margin from a trampled or polluted one, but exact species remain open.
- **Watercress-like edible greens:** possible in clean, flowing water. Gathering from polluted or stagnant water is unsafe; the presence of a familiar edible plant does not prove the water is clean.
- **Marsh grasses and sedges:** support wetland food webs and provide grazing or cutting material in suitable areas.

Floods can flatten vegetation, deposit silt, expose roots, move seed, and alter the edge of a channel. A plant community may recover over time, but repeated flooding or drainage works can change it. The River Vale is a working waterway, so millers, fishers, farmers, and landholders may disagree about cutting, drainage, and access.

## 5. Greyback Ridges and quarry country

The Greybacks are low, stony ridges rather than alpine mountains. Exposed stone, thin soils, scrub, quarry spoil, and warmer sheltered pockets create a patchwork of plant life.

Candidate plants include tough grasses, thorny scrub, low flowering herbs, lichens, mosses, and hardy shrubs. Where soil is deeper, small trees and brush may take hold. Quarry faces and abandoned cuts can support plants in cracks and damp pockets, but disturbed stone is not automatically fertile ground.

Useful gathered materials may include:
- Tough grasses and fibers for simple bindings.
- Scrub wood for small fuel loads.
- Mosses for packing or limited household uses.
- Lichens and mineral-stained growths that may attract specialist curiosity, though no magical or alchemical value is established.
- Common clay-bearing or mineral soils used locally where suitable deposits are known.

Quarry activity removes vegetation, creates dust, alters drainage, and exposes bare ground. A worked face may remain largely barren, while older spoil heaps and abandoned cuts gradually acquire grasses, weeds, and shrubs.

## 6. Eastern Shore and salt-working places

The Eastern Shore is exposed to inland-sea storms and includes low bluffs, beaches, shingle coves, landing places, and salt-working sites. Salt spray, wind, thin soils, and shifting shingle limit what grows directly beside the water.

Candidate shore plants include tough grasses, low salt-tolerant herbs, sea-beet-like greens, and plants that colonize disturbed sand or shingle. Reeds and wetland vegetation may occur where freshwater meets the shore, but should not be assumed along every cove.

Salt production can alter nearby plant life through brine, heat, fuel gathering, and the movement of soil and waste. Saltwick's working landscape should show practical signs of this activity: cleared fuel areas, storage, drying places, paths to water, and vegetation disturbed by repeated use.

No rare coastal herb, magical seaweed, or unique alchemical plant is established. Such additions require a specific reason and GM approval.

## 7. The Siltfen and outer wilderness

The Siltfen's wet meadows, reed beds, shallow pools, alder stands, and muddy channels support a different gathering economy from the Northfields or Brackenwood.

Potential resources include reeds, rushes, wetland grasses, suitable edible greens, willow rods, fungi on damp wood, and plants used for animal bedding or simple craft. Seasonal water levels affect access. A familiar path may disappear beneath water or become soft enough to trap a loaded cart.

The Deep Bracken and Upper Vale offer more remote gathering opportunities: mushrooms, nuts, resins, bark, useful roots, and plant fibers. These resources are not automatically unclaimed. A gathering party may cross customary woodland, grazing, or estate rights even where no fence is visible.

## 8. Food, storage, and household use

Plant gathering supplements rather than replaces agriculture. Most households depend on cultivated grain, legumes, garden produce, dairy, meat, and fish. Foraged food is seasonal, variable, and labor-intensive.

Practical uses include:
- Fresh greens and shoots eaten in season.
- Berries and orchard fruit eaten fresh, dried, cooked, or preserved.
- Nuts stored where they can be kept dry and protected from pests.
- Mushrooms dried or cooked only when correctly identified.
- Reeds and rushes used for household goods and repair.
- Willow, hazel, and other flexible stems used in baskets, hurdles, bindings, and simple structures.
- Bark, resin, and plant oils used in craft or household work where local knowledge supports the use.
- Fodder, bedding, thatch, fuel, and composting material.

Preservation requires labor, containers, fuel, dry storage, and protection from vermin. A glut of fruit does not automatically become winter food; it must be gathered before spoilage, processed, and stored.

## 9. Medicine, poison, and the limits of herbal knowledge

People may recognize plants by taste, smell, shape, season, habitat, and the advice of experienced gatherers. That does not mean every household has reliable medical knowledge.

Distinguish:
- **Food plants:** eaten as ordinary food after suitable preparation.
- **Craft plants:** gathered for fiber, rods, dyes, fuel, tannins, resins, or other materials.
- **Household remedies:** plants used in customary preparations; effectiveness may be partial, uncertain, or misunderstood.
- **Dangerous plants:** species that irritate skin, cause illness, or are toxic if eaten.
- **Specialist materials:** plants sought by trained herbalists, physicians, alchemists, dyers, or craftspeople.

Do not assign mechanical healing effects, poison DCs, alchemical bonuses, or mandatory crafting components to ordinary plants without an explicit rules decision. A folk remedy may soothe a minor complaint without curing an infection or neutralizing a poison.

Plant identification matters. A novice may confuse edible and dangerous lookalikes. The GM should telegraph uncertainty through knowledge checks, local advice, and the consequences of careless gathering rather than arbitrarily punishing a player.

No unique magical herb, resurrection plant, universal antidote, or required magic-item ingredient is established in this record.

## 10. Gathering rights and local knowledge

The right to gather depends on where the plant grows and who controls the land. Relevant distinctions include:
- A household garden or orchard under private control.
- Tenant land where gleaning may be governed by custom or agreement.
- Managed woodland with cutting and gathering rights.
- Common pasture or village land with customary access.
- Riverbanks, fisheries, and wetland margins subject to overlapping rights.
- A lord's reserved land or a chartered resource area.
- Remote ground where authority is weak but ownership or claims may still exist.

A person who knows a plant may not have the right to harvest it. Conversely, customary gathering may be accepted even when the land is technically held by a lord or estate.

Experienced gatherers know where to look, when to collect, how to avoid damaging a stand, and which buyers value particular materials. Their knowledge is local and practical, not omniscient.

## 11. Seasonal field guide

The following is a broad temperate-region guide, not a fixed schedule. The calendar explicitly does not guarantee weather or biological timing.

| Period | Likely plant activity | Human work |
|---|---|---|
| Late winter / thaw | Dormant growth; early shoots may appear in sheltered places; wet ground limits access | Repair fences, prepare tools, prune where appropriate, assess winter damage |
| Spring | New growth, flowering, leafing, and seedling establishment | Planting, weeding, hedge repair, gathering selected greens, coppice work where locally timed |
| Summer | Dense growth, flowering, fruit development, hay and fodder growth | Haymaking, garden care, selective gathering, reed and craft work where suitable |
| Harvestfall | Grain and fruit gathering; nuts and berries may be available; seed dispersal | Harvest, gleaning where permitted, drying, pressing, storing, fuel preparation |
| Duskreach / Longnight | Dormancy increases; late fruit and roots may remain; wet and cold conditions constrain gathering | Protect stores, repair roofs and fences, manage fuel and fodder, avoid damaging saturated ground |

Exact flowering, fruiting, and harvest dates depend on local weather and species. Do not turn the table into a rigid almanac.

## 12. Field descriptions for play

Use plant life to make places distinct without turning every bush into a clue.

- **Northfields:** clipped hedges, trampled gateways, orchard rows, stubble, vegetable plots, drainage ditches, hay ricks, and manure-rich corners.
- **Brackenwood:** cut coppice stools, straight young shoots, mature standards, leaf litter, bramble tangles, charcoal clearings, and paths that shift with work.
- **River Vale:** reed margins, willow roots, wet grass, silt lines, flood debris, mill channels, and bankside gardens.
- **Greyback Ridges:** scrub clinging to thin soil, hardy grasses, lichen-stained stone, quarry spoil, and isolated sheltered pockets.
- **Eastern Shore:** wind-shaped grasses, salt-stressed scrub, shingle plants, drying racks, fuel-cleared ground, and vegetation bent away from exposed water.
- **Siltfen:** reed beds, rushes, shallow pools, soft banks, alder stands, and strips of firmer ground used as paths.
