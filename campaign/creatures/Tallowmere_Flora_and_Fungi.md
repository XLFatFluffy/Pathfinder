# Tallowmere Region — Proposed Flora, Useful Plants, and Fungi

**Snapshot:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Status:** PROPOSED / GM reference  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY  
**Geographic authority:** [Regional Geography, Routes, and Points of Interest](../locations/starting_city/Regional_Geography_and_Points_of_Interest.md)  
**Ecological baseline:** [Tallowmere Regional Ecology](Tallowmere_Regional_Ecology.md)

This record proposes a practical botanical foundation for the established Tallowmere landscape. It is not a definitive species list and does not establish that every named plant grows locally. Ordinary plants should support the texture of farming, fuel gathering, food, craft, medicine, building, and seasonal work. Magical or supernatural plants are deliberately excluded unless separately approved.

Names below use familiar common-language equivalents for ease of reference. The setting's exact local plant names, varieties, and botanical taxonomy remain open.

## 1. Botanical principles

- The Northfields are cultivated, not natural grassland. Crops, pasture, hedges, orchards, drainage ditches, and fallow ground dominate.
- Brackenwood is managed woodland. Coppicing, charcoal production, grazing, timber cutting, and gathering shape its plant communities.
- The River Vale has wet banks, flood meadows, gravel bars, mill reaches, and managed drainage.
- The Greyback Ridges are low stony uplands, not alpine mountains.
- The Eastern Shore has exposed bluffs, shingle, salt-working places, coves, and wind-shaped vegetation.
- Plant distribution varies with soil, water, shade, grazing, cultivation, and human management.
- A plant's traditional use is not proof that it works. Folk remedies, poison lore, and ritual use must be distinguished from reliable effects.
- No plant in this record is inherently magical, sentient, cursed, or supernatural.

## 2. Northfields — crops, pasture, orchards, and hedgerows

### Cultivated plants

Candidate staple crops include wheat, barley, oats, rye, peas, beans, turnips, onions, leeks, cabbages, and other hardy kitchen vegetables. Which crop dominates a particular manor depends on soil, custom, rents, market demand, and rotation practices. Do not assign acreage or yields without a specific economic need.

- **Wheat** is a desirable grain for finer bread and flour. It is more sensitive to poor conditions than hardier grains.
- **Barley** can supply food, animal feed, and malt for brewing.
- **Oats** are useful for porridge, coarse food, and horse fodder.
- **Rye** is a candidate for poorer or cooler soils and can produce darker, denser bread.
- **Peas and beans** provide stored food and help diversify the field system.
- **Turnips and other roots** can support winter food and livestock fodder.
- **Onions, leeks, cabbages, and hardy greens** are plausible kitchen-garden staples.

These are candidate crops, not a fixed agricultural schedule. The region's established farm economy supports cultivation, but exact rotations, seed varieties, yields, and prices remain open.

### Orchards and fruit

The Northfields include orchards. Apples and pears are strong candidates, with plums, cherries, and other hardy fruits possible where sheltered conditions permit. Fruit may be eaten fresh, dried, cooked, pressed, fermented, or stored. Fallen fruit attracts insects, birds, rodents, and scavengers.

An orchard is a long-term investment. Trees require pruning, protection, grafting knowledge, and patience. A damaged orchard cannot be replaced in one season. Manor orchards may be more carefully tended than small household plots, but ordinary households can keep a few fruit trees where land and custom permit.

### Hedgerows and field margins

Hedges may contain hawthorn, blackthorn, hazel, dog rose, elder, bramble, and mixed native shrubs. They mark fields, slow livestock, provide fuel and poles, and offer nesting and feeding habitat.

- **Hawthorn** forms dense boundaries and produces berries used by birds.
- **Blackthorn** produces sloes and thorny growth; its early blossom is a recognizable seasonal sign.
- **Hazel** supplies nuts, rods, hurdles, and coppice material.
- **Elder** provides flowers and berries used in household preparations, though parts of the plant require care in handling.
- **Bramble** produces blackberries and forms difficult thickets.
- **Dog rose** produces hips that may be gathered and dried.

Field margins are not wasted land. They provide access, fuel, gathering, nesting cover, and boundary definition. Cutting them at the wrong time may destroy nests or reduce later harvests.

## 3. Brackenwood — managed woodland plants

### Trees

Candidate trees include oak, beech, ash, birch, alder, willow, hazel, and smaller fruiting or flowering trees in clearings. Their exact distribution is not established.

- **Oak** supplies durable timber, fuel, acorns, and long-lived standards. Large oaks may be boundary markers or customary gathering points.
- **Beech** provides timber and mast in suitable ground.
- **Ash** is valued for workable wood and tool handles.
- **Birch** grows in lighter or disturbed ground and can provide poles, bark, and fuel.
- **Alder** favors damp ground and stream edges.
- **Willow** grows readily near water and can supply rods for baskets, hurdles, and bindings.
- **Hazel** is useful for coppice poles, hurdles, stakes, and nuts.

### Coppice and woodland work

Coppicing is a central feature of Brackenwood's managed character. Trees are cut near the base and allowed to regrow from stools. Rotating coppice plots produce a changing mosaic of open ground, young shoots, brush, and mature trees.

The work supplies:
- fuelwood and charcoal feedstock,
- poles and stakes,
- hurdle and basket material,
- tool handles and small craft timber,
- bark and other gathered materials.

Not every tree is available to every household. Woodland rights, marked plots, customary gathering, and Denvor oversight matter. Cutting a protected standard or taking wood from a reserved plot can be treated as theft even if fallen branches are commonly gathered.

### Understory and gathered plants

Candidate plants include bracken, ferns, nettles, wild garlic, sorrel, woodruff, primrose, violets, mushrooms, berries, and edible roots. Some may be gathered for food, bedding, animal fodder, dye, scent, or household use.

Gatherers need to distinguish edible species from toxic lookalikes. Knowledge is local and practical, often passed through families and work groups. A city visitor may know a plant's name but not the correct season, preparation, or safe part to use.

## 4. River Vale — wet meadows and water margins

Candidate riverside plants include reeds, rushes, sedges, willow, alder, water mint, nettles, meadow grasses, and flowering wetland plants.

- **Reeds and rushes** may be used for thatch, mats, screens, bedding, and simple craftwork.
- **Willow** supplies flexible rods for baskets, fish traps, and bindings.
- **Alder** tolerates wet ground and can provide useful timber.
- **Water mint and other aromatic herbs** may be gathered for household use, though their medicinal effects should not be assumed.
- **Sedges and meadow grasses** support grazing and can be cut for fodder or bedding.

Flood meadows are productive but unreliable. Floodwater can deposit silt and nutrients, damage hay, wash away fences, or leave contaminated debris. The timing of a hay cut matters: a field left too long may lose quality, while an early cut may reduce the amount available for winter fodder.

Riverbank plants also affect navigation and maintenance. Reeds can conceal shallow margins; roots stabilize banks; careless cutting can accelerate erosion. Mill owners, farmers, fishers, and river users may disagree about what should be cleared.

## 5. Greyback Ridges — scrub, exposed stone, and quarry margins

The low stony ridges support tougher vegetation than the sheltered fields. Candidate plants include gorse, broom, heather, hawthorn scrub, grasses, mosses, lichens, and hardy herbs. Trees become more scattered on exposed or shallow-soil slopes.

- **Gorse** provides dense thorny cover and can burn fiercely when dry.
- **Broom** grows in open scrub and may be used in simple sweeping bundles.
- **Heather** may occur in suitable poorer soils but should not be assumed to carpet the whole ridge system.
- **Mosses and lichens** occupy stone and damp surfaces; their presence can make steps slippery.
- **Hardy grasses and low herbs** support grazing on accessible slopes.

Quarrying exposes bare stone and creates disturbed ground where pioneer plants can establish. Dust and runoff may suppress vegetation immediately around active workings. Abandoned cuts may develop scrub, pooled water, and unstable plant-covered edges that conceal hazards.

## 6. Eastern Shore — salt, wind, and shingle

Candidate shore vegetation includes salt-tolerant grasses, sea beet or similar coastal greens, thrift, sea lavender, hardy scrub, and plants adapted to exposed bluffs. The exact local species remain open.

Salt-working places may also contain deliberately managed fuel, drying, and storage areas. Salt itself is a major practical resource, but the presence of salt pans does not establish a particular technical process or output.

The shore's plant cover is vulnerable to storms, trampling, grazing, and erosion. A path across a bluff may be stable in summer and dangerous after rain or winter storms. Coastal plants can provide useful local markers for experienced fishers and gatherers, but should not become magical signposts.

## 7. Household gardens and cultivated herbs

A household garden may contain a mixture of food, scent, dye, and practical plants. Candidate plants include:

- parsley, sage, thyme, mint, and savory for flavoring;
- rosemary or other hardy aromatic shrubs where climate permits;
- onions, garlic, leeks, and cabbages;
- beans, peas, and root vegetables;
- calendula-like flowers and other plants used for color or household preparations;
- flax or hemp where local cultivation and law permit.

A wealthy household may have a dedicated kitchen garden, orchard, herb beds, and gardeners. A poorer household may grow a few herbs in pots, a narrow yard, or a shared plot. Access to garden land is a class and property question, not merely a botanical one.

## 8. Fungi and decay

Mushrooms and other fungi are part of woodland and field ecology. They break down dead wood, leaf litter, and other organic matter; some form relationships with tree roots. They appear in different places according to moisture, season, host trees, and disturbance.

Potentially useful categories include:
- edible woodland mushrooms known to experienced gatherers,
- bracket fungi on dead or weakened trees,
- puffballs and field fungi in suitable grassland,
- molds and yeasts used in food and drink production,
- poisonous mushrooms that resemble edible species.

No named local mushroom is approved as a reliable cure, poison, intoxicant, or magical ingredient. If a particular fungus becomes important, create a dedicated record describing identification, preparation, risks, and evidence. A character's confident identification can still be wrong.

## 9. Useful plants and material culture

Plants support far more than food. Candidate practical uses include:

| Use | Candidate materials | Important limits |
|---|---|---|
| Fuel | Coppiced wood, deadwood, charcoal | Access rights and moisture matter. |
| Building and repair | Oak, ash, birch, willow, reeds, thatch grasses | Different woods have different strength and durability. |
| Baskets and hurdles | Willow, hazel, flexible shoots | Requires seasonal cutting and craft skill. |
| Rope, cloth, and cordage | Flax, hemp, nettle fiber, imported fibers | Local production is not yet established. |
| Food and preserves | Grain, fruit, berries, nuts, roots, herbs | Safe identification and storage matter. |
| Dye and color | Woad-like dye plants, madder-like roots, bark, berries | Exact local dye crops remain unresolved. |
| Tanning and craft | Oak bark and other tannin-rich materials | Processing is skilled, unpleasant, and resource-intensive. |
| Bedding and thatch | Straw, reeds, rushes, grasses | Quality and fire risk vary. |
| Household scent | Herbs, flowers, resins | Luxury access differs by class. |

These are possible uses, not proof that Tallowmere produces every material commercially. Imported goods may supply specialized dyes, fibers, resins, and spices.

## 10. Medicinal plants and folk practice

People may use herbs, poultices, washes, teas, oils, and salves for comfort, wound cleaning, digestion, sleep, or minor complaints. The existence of a traditional remedy does not mean it is medically effective. Some plants are harmful when misidentified, prepared incorrectly, or taken in excessive amounts.

Do not make a plant a reliable cure for infection, poison, serious wounds, disease, or supernatural conditions without explicit setting and rules approval. Pathfinder magic remains a separate matter. A healer's practical knowledge can be valuable without being equivalent to spellcasting.

Specific remedies, dosages, toxicology, and regional medical traditions belong in the medicine reference when that work is developed. This document does not establish them.

## 11. Seasonal gathering and work

- **Early spring:** pruning, coppice work, first greens, hedge repair, and preparation of gardens.
- **Late spring and summer:** haymaking, herb gathering, weeding, fruit development, and maintenance of field boundaries.
- **Harvestfall:** grain harvest, fruit gathering, nut collection, drying, pressing, and preparation of stores.
- **Duskreach:** root crops, late fruit, firewood, thatch repairs, and winter preparation.
- **Winter:** limited gathering, tool repair, woodland work where weather allows, and reliance on stored food and fuel.

These are broad working expectations, not a fixed agricultural calendar. Weather, labor, local custom, and the specific crop determine actual timing.

## 12. Knowledge and folklore boundaries

Different people know different things:
- Farmers recognize crop disease, soil, weeds, and the practical value of hedges.
- Foresters and charcoal burners know coppice cycles, timber, fuel, and woodland paths.
- Gatherers know safe identification and preparation of familiar plants.
- Fishers know river and shore plants that mark banks, shallows, and seasonal changes.
- Healers may know household preparations, but their training and reliability vary.
- Scholars may classify plants but lack the practical experience of local workers.

No universal herbalist guild, druidic monopoly, secret botanical order, or magical plant trade is established. Such institutions should be added only if useful and approved.

## 13. Frontier habitats — outer woodland, uplands, wetland, and shore

The outer frontier environments are described in [Wilderness Beyond Tallowmere](../locations/starting_city/Wilderness_Beyond_Tallowmere.md). The plants below are habitat-consistent candidates, not confirmed species occurrences or grants of gathering rights.

### Deep Bracken

Older woodland beyond the managed coppice may contain mature trees, fallen timber, deadwood, shaded understory, mosses, ferns, fungi, and less-disturbed clearings. The exact tree composition remains open. Gatherers may find useful materials, but older stands can be subject to different ownership, customary rights, and access restrictions from the Brackenwood.

### Upper Vale

Headwater valleys and upland pasture support a changing mix of streamside growth, meadow grasses, rough pasture, scrub, and woodland pockets. Willow and alder are candidates along suitable watercourses; tougher grasses and low herbs may occupy exposed slopes. Do not assume alpine flora throughout the Upper Vale. Elevation, aspect, soil depth, and water availability must be established for a specific site.

### Greyback Heights

The higher and rougher country beyond the worked quarries may support scrub, grasses, mosses, lichens, and hardy plants adapted to shallow soil and exposed stone. The same broad candidates proposed for the lower Greyback Ridges may occur, but exact species and distribution are not established. Quarry spoil, old prospecting cuts, and unstable ground can create disturbed plant communities.

### Siltfen

The wetland's reeds, rushes, sedges, wet meadow grasses, alder, willow, and water-margin herbs are practical candidates. Their distribution depends on water depth, flow, seasonal flooding, grazing, and cutting. Reed beds may be harvested for thatch, mats, screens, and basketry; rights and customary access remain local questions. Peat and clay are mentioned as possible resources in the wilderness framework, not as universal or commercially important deposits.

### Outer Eastern Shore

Exposed coves and bluffs may support salt-tolerant grasses, low coastal herbs, hardy scrub, and plants adapted to wind, salt spray, and thin soils. Sheltered hollows may support different growth from exposed cliff edges. Coastal gathering must account for storm damage, erosion, and local rights.

No frontier plant in this record is a confirmed magical ingredient, reliable cure, or supernatural sign. Rare finds should be valuable because a buyer wants a particular quality or material, not because every unusual plant is secretly magical.
