# Tallowmere Regional Bestiary — Dangerous Creatures

**Status:** GM-approved regional creature expansion  
**Baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Visibility:** GM reference  
**Purpose:** Establish a small, ecologically coherent set of dangerous non-humanoid creatures for the starting region. This is not a list of every Pathfinder monster that could exist.

## 1. Philosophy

Most people in the Tallowmere region encounter animals, pests, livestock, and other people far more often than monsters.

Dangerous creatures exist, but they are not evenly distributed. A creature appearing in a Bestiary does not mean it is common.

A monster should have a habitat, food source, reason to avoid or approach people, and recognizable evidence.

The existence of a creature also does not mean ordinary people understand its Pathfinder statistics, magical abilities, or true nature.

## 2. Giant Spiders

**Regional status:** ESTABLISHED  
**Primary habitat:** Deep Bracken and isolated wooded frontier areas  
**Frequency:** UNCOMMON  
**Threat:** Serious to isolated travelers; usually avoidable with local knowledge

Giant spiders are among the more widely feared supernatural predators associated with the older woodland.

They are not found throughout every forest. Managed woodland, active charcoal grounds, heavily traveled roads, and busy farm edges are poor habitat for large populations. The Deep Bracken provides more suitable shelter.

### What ordinary people know

Most rural people know that some spiders in the deep woods grow large enough to kill a person.

Common warnings include:

- do not put your hand into an unseen hollow;
- do not camp beneath suspicious webs;
- do not follow prey animals into thick brush without checking the ground and branches;
- if several birds or small animals disappear from one patch of woodland, tell someone who knows the forest.

People often exaggerate their size and numbers.

### What experienced hunters know

Experienced woodsmen recognize:

- heavy webbing between trees;
- wrapped prey;
- disturbed leaf litter;
- silk anchored across narrow passages;
- unusual concentrations of flies;
- abandoned web sites where the spider has moved.

A hunter who finds a large web does not automatically know whether the spider is still nearby.

### GM truth

Giant spiders are predators, not supernatural guardians of ancient ruins.

They establish territories where prey and shelter support them. They may retreat from repeated human disturbance.

A spider encounter should therefore be preceded by ecological evidence unless circumstances justify a surprise.

## 3. Giant Frogs

**Regional status:** ESTABLISHED  
**Primary habitat:** Siltfen and isolated wetland pools  
**Frequency:** UNCOMMON  
**Threat:** Dangerous near water; generally localized

Large predatory frogs occur in portions of the Siltfen and other poorly drained wetland pockets.

They are not intelligent monsters and do not organize into societies.

### What ordinary people know

Wetland workers know that some frogs are big enough to take birds, rats, fish, and occasionally small livestock.

Children are warned not to approach deep reed pools alone.

Most people do not distinguish ordinary frogs from unusually large specimens until they see one.

### What experienced marsh workers know

Large frogs favor:

- quiet pools;
- reed margins;
- warm shallows;
- concealed banks;
- places with abundant small prey.

A sudden lack of smaller animals around a pool can indicate a large predator.

### GM truth

Giant frogs are opportunistic predators.

They do not deliberately hunt people as their preferred food. A person becomes a target because they approach too closely, resemble prey at the edge of the water, or disturb a feeding or nesting site.

## 4. Stirges

**Regional status:** ESTABLISHED  
**Primary habitat:** Wet woodland, caves, abandoned structures, marsh margins  
**Frequency:** RARE locally  
**Threat:** Dangerous in groups; minor individually

Stirges are small flying parasites feared by people who work near caves, wetlands, and abandoned structures.

Their existence is more widely known than their actual appearance.

### What ordinary people know

People describe them as blood-drinking flying pests.

Common advice includes:

- cover exposed skin when entering suspicious caves;
- do not disturb nests;
- leave a place where large numbers of bats or other animals suddenly flee without explanation;
- seek help after multiple bites or significant blood loss.

Folk stories often exaggerate their size.

### What specialists know

Hunters, healers, and soldiers recognize that stirges are attracted to living creatures and can become dangerous in numbers.

A single stirge is unpleasant.

A swarm can become a serious emergency.

### GM truth

Stirge populations are localized and tied to suitable shelter and prey.

They should not appear simply because the party enters a cave.

## 5. Dire Animals

**Regional status:** ESTABLISHED AS OCCASIONAL INDIVIDUALS  
**Primary habitat:** Deep Bracken, Greyback margins, remote frontier  
**Frequency:** RARE  
**Threat:** Serious

Occasionally an unusually large and dangerous animal appears in the deeper wilderness.

The term "dire" is a practical description rather than a scientific classification used consistently by local people.

A farmer may call an enormous wolf a dire wolf. A hunter may simply call it a monster.

### What ordinary people know

People know that some animals become frighteningly large.

They generally do not understand why.

Stories may attribute unusual size to:

- old age;
- starvation;
- cursed ground;
- witchcraft;
- divine punishment;
- eating too much human flesh;
- simply being "born wrong."

Most of these explanations are unreliable.

### GM truth

Do not treat every unusually large animal as supernatural.

A rare giant specimen can exist without establishing a broader magical mutation system.

## 6. Trolls

**Regional status:** ESTABLISHED EXISTENCE; LOCAL POPULATION UNCONFIRMED  
**Primary habitat:** Unknown  
**Frequency:** RARE  
**Threat:** Severe

Trolls are real.

They are not ordinary Tallowmere wildlife.

Most residents know them through stories, old military accounts, travelers' tales, and occasional physical evidence. A small number of people may have encountered one and survived.

### What ordinary people know

A troll is generally understood to be a large, dangerous creature that can kill several people and is difficult to stop.

Details vary wildly.

Some people claim trolls live under bridges. Others describe caves, mountains, forests, or ruins.

The popular association with bridges is folklore, not a confirmed ecological rule.

### What experienced fighters and scholars know

Those with actual experience know that ordinary weapons may not be enough and that trolls possess unusual resilience.

Specific weaknesses should not be assumed to be common knowledge merely because they appear in a story.

### GM truth

The prepared Selwick & Company troll encounter remains an available scenario, not evidence that Tallowmere contains a resident troll population.

Do not use the encounter to retroactively establish a troll ecology unless the campaign actually produces evidence.

## 7. What commoners believe about monsters

Most commoners do not maintain a precise monster taxonomy.

A person may distinguish:

- wolf;
- unusually large wolf;
- monster wolf;

without knowing whether those categories correspond to different species.

Knowledge is local.

A charcoal burner may know more about giant spiders than a city merchant. A ferryman may recognize dangerous river conditions that a noble scholar has never encountered. A priest may know stories about undead that a farmer has never heard.

This means an NPC's ignorance is not stupidity.

It reflects their experience.

## 8. Evidence before encounters

Whenever practical, dangerous creatures should leave evidence.

Examples:

- tracks;
- scat;
- webs;
- damaged vegetation;
- missing livestock;
- blood;
- unusual sounds;
- nesting material;
- disturbed burrows;
- carcasses;
- frightened or displaced prey.

Not every encounter requires a warning.

But unexplained monster appearances should be the exception rather than the normal rhythm of play.

## 9. Magical rarity

The existence of monsters does not make magic commonplace.

A farmer can believe that trolls exist while never having seen a spell.

A sailor can know stories about sea monsters without knowing a single wizard.

A village may possess an old sword said to have been blessed by a priest without understanding whether the blessing involved actual divine magic.

The supernatural exists within the world.

It is not everyday technology.

## 10. Expansion rule

Future regional creatures should be added only when one of the following is true:

1. The creature fills a genuine ecological or adventure need.
2. The GM deliberately establishes it.
3. The PCs encounter and document it.
4. Existing canon requires it.

Do not fill empty wilderness with monsters merely because the Pathfinder Bestiary contains them.


## 11. Field notes: what an encounter looks like before initiative

These notes add table-facing evidence and behavior without establishing new lairs, breeding sites, population sizes, or migration routes. Creature-specific ecology remains partly unknown.

### Giant spiders — the web-maker in the old wood

**Local descriptions:** "web-hunter", "tree spider", or simply "the big spider". These are descriptive folk labels, not formal species names.

**Likely prey and feeding evidence:** small and medium animals caught in webs; wrapped carcasses; remains beneath feeding sites; insect activity around old web structures. The exact prey range of local individuals is not recorded.

**Territory and shelter:** web sites are associated with sheltered woodland pockets, tree gaps, hollows, and quiet passages. Do not assume every web is occupied or that a spider's territory has a fixed radius.

**Useful warning signs:** silk across a narrow trail at face height; anchored lines between branches; prey wrapped above the ground; repeated absence of small birds in a confined patch; silk disturbed by something large moving through it.

**False or exaggerated beliefs:** every old web is a giant spider's trap; spiders deliberately collect human bodies; a web across a path proves the spider is watching it. These claims are not established.

**GM use:** A web site can be a warning, a hazard, an abandoned feeding place, or evidence of a nearby predator. The spider need not attack immediately. It may remain still, retreat, or wait for prey to become trapped. Avoid making giant spiders guardians of ruins or intelligent ambushers unless a particular individual is deliberately established that way.

### Giant frogs — the still pool predator

**Local descriptions:** "pool-jaw", "reed frog", or "big marsh frog". These are local descriptions, not confirmed species names.

**Likely prey and feeding evidence:** fish, amphibians, insects, small birds, rodents, and other animals within reach are plausible prey. A giant frog may leave disturbed shallows, flattened vegetation, tracks or impressions in mud, and partially consumed prey. Specific local diet has not been documented.

**Territory and shelter:** quiet pools, reed margins, warm shallows, concealed banks, and places with abundant prey. A pool can support one large animal without supporting a population.

**Useful warning signs:** a sudden splash from an otherwise quiet pool; large movement beneath the surface; repeated disturbance at one bank; missing small animals around a particular pool. None of these signs uniquely identifies a giant frog.

**False or exaggerated beliefs:** giant frogs pull people under from any depth; all deep pools contain them; they are poisonous; they deliberately hunt livestock. None of these claims is established.

**GM use:** The creature is dangerous because water, mud, and vegetation restrict movement and visibility. Its attack is an opportunistic feeding or defensive response, not evidence of malice or intelligence. Give wetland workers knowledge of dangerous pools through experience, not universal monster lore.

### Stirges — the blood-feeders

**Local descriptions:** "blood flies", "needle-wings", or "blood-suckers". These terms may also be used inaccurately for ordinary biting insects.

**Feeding evidence:** small puncture wounds, blood loss, disturbed roost sites, and attacks concentrated on exposed living creatures. A single unexplained bite is not proof of stirges.

**Shelter:** suitable caves, sheltered cavities, abandoned structures, damp woodland sites, and marsh margins. Exact roost locations and colony structure in Tallowmere are unknown.

**Useful warning signs:** unusual numbers of small flying shapes in a dark shelter; animals avoiding a cavity; remains of small creatures; multiple unexplained puncture wounds after entering a confined site.

**False or exaggerated beliefs:** every bat is a stirge; stirges can smell blood from miles away; one bite inevitably kills; they can pass through walls. These are not established.

**GM use:** Stirges become dangerous through numbers, surprise, confinement, and the difficulty of removing attached parasites. They should have a reason to occupy a site and a reason to feed there. A cave with no suitable shelter or prey is not automatically a stirge nest.

### Dire animals — the exceptional specimen

**Local descriptions:** "great wolf", "old tusker", "monster boar", or "devil-beast". The language depends on the animal and the witness.

**Evidence:** unusually large tracks, heavy browsing or rooting, carcasses, damage to fences, and prey animals avoiding a particular area. Size estimates from frightened witnesses are unreliable.

**Ecology:** occasional individuals only. Do not invent a breeding population, magical contamination zone, or universal reason for unusual size. A dire animal may be old, unusually large, displaced, injured, or simply an exceptional specimen; which explanation applies is case-specific.

**False or exaggerated beliefs:** every dire animal is cursed; eating human flesh makes an animal grow; a dire animal cannot be killed by ordinary weapons. None is a general setting rule.

**GM use:** Treat the animal as an individual with ordinary animal needs and instincts unless the specific creature is established otherwise. It may avoid people, defend young, protect a feeding site, or become dangerous because it is wounded or cornered.

### Trolls — a known danger, not a local species record

**Local descriptions:** "troll" is a recognized broad term, but stories differ about size, habitat, intelligence, and weaknesses.

**Evidence:** the prepared Selwick encounter is not yet an observed event. No local tracks, feeding site, lair, breeding population, or travel corridor is confirmed.

**Ecology:** UNKNOWN. Do not decide that the troll lives under a bridge, in a cave, or in the Deep Bracken unless the encounter's eventual evidence supports that conclusion.

**False or exaggerated beliefs:** every troll lives under a bridge; every troll can be killed by fire alone; trolls cannot reason; trolls are all part of one local clan. These claims are not established.

**GM use:** Preserve the difference between a prepared encounter and a world fact. If the PCs encounter the troll, record what they actually learn. A single encounter can establish that a troll was at that location on that date; it does not automatically establish its origin or population.

## 12. Turning an encounter into canon

After a creature encounter, record only what the PCs or reliable witnesses actually establish:

- date and location;
- creature identification and confidence;
- tracks, remains, wounds, or other evidence;
- observed behavior;
- whether the creature was alone or accompanied;
- whether it fled, defended territory, fed, hunted, negotiated, or pursued;
- what happened to the habitat or nearby people;
- what local authorities and communities learned;
- what remains uncertain.

Do not fill gaps with assumptions simply to make the record look complete. Use UNKNOWN where the evidence ends.

A creature can become part of the world's history without becoming a recurring encounter. A killed predator may leave a vacant territory, a frightened community, a disputed bounty, a damaged livelihood, or a new local story. Those consequences are often more valuable than adding another monster to the list.
