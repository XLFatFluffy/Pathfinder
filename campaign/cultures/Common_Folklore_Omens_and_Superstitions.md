# Common Folklore, Omens, and Superstitions

**Status:** GM-approved worldbuilding expansion  
**Baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Visibility:** GM reference; individual beliefs may be common knowledge, local custom, or personal superstition

This record establishes ordinary folklore without declaring supernatural explanations true.

The setting contains real gods, real divine magic, real monsters, and a history of real dragons. That does not make every omen meaningful.

Most people cannot reliably tell the difference between coincidence, natural causes, deliberate deception, and genuine supernatural events.

## 1. General principle

People use superstition to manage uncertainty.

Common subjects include:

- weather;
- travel;
- childbirth;
- illness;
- death;
- harvests;
- ships;
- strangers;
- dreams;
- animals;
- unusual lights;
- abandoned places;
- eclipses and unusual celestial events;
- magic.

A belief can be widespread without being universally accepted.

A person can follow a custom "just in case" without believing it has supernatural power.

## 2. Harvest and agriculture

Farm communities may observe small rituals before important agricultural work.

Examples include:

- leaving the first portion of a harvest untouched until thanks have been given;
- keeping a useful tool from the previous harvest year;
- avoiding unnecessary arguments on the first major harvest day;
- saving seed from a particularly successful crop;
- treating damaged grain as a practical warning rather than immediately calling it a curse.

The established Harvest Home tradition of the First Table is broader and more important than these local superstitions.

## 3. Weather

Weather signs are often treated as practical knowledge first and superstition second.

People may believe:

- unusual bird behavior predicts storms;
- a certain cloud shape means rain;
- sudden silence in animals means something is wrong;
- a red sunset predicts particular weather;
- unusual wind changes are warnings.

Some of these observations may have practical value.

Others are folk explanations attached to real patterns.

## 4. Travel

Travelers commonly carry small protective objects.

These might include:

- a religious token;
- a family charm;
- a piece of worked metal;
- a carved piece of wood;
- a written blessing;
- a keepsake from home.

The object may be religious, sentimental, superstitious, or all three.

A traveler may touch the object before crossing a dangerous bridge without claiming that the object actually protects them.

## 5. Ships and sailors

Maritime superstition varies heavily between crews.

A sailor may:

- avoid saying a particular word before departure;
- knock on wood or touch a mast;
- keep a charm near the helm;
- leave a small offering before a difficult voyage;
- distrust an unusually silent harbor;
- interpret certain birds as signs.

Crew members may mock one another's beliefs while still following the ritual.

This is normal.

Humans are perfectly capable of believing something is nonsense and doing it anyway.

## 6. Death

Death creates some of the strongest folk practices.

Families may:

- keep a lamp burning during the first night;
- place a personal object near the dead;
- speak the dead person's name during remembrance;
- avoid certain household tasks until burial;
- preserve written records;
- visit graves or memorials at particular times.

These practices vary by religion and region.

They should not be treated as universal funerary law.

## 7. Dreams

Dreams are frequently interpreted.

A dream may be considered:

- ordinary;
- a reflection of worry;
- a message from a dead relative;
- a divine sign;
- a warning;
- meaningless.

Different people can interpret the same dream differently.

A priest may offer religious guidance without claiming certainty.

A scholar may discuss dream theory without believing the dream supernatural.

A frightened commoner may treat a disturbing dream as an omen.

All three responses are plausible.

## 8. Animals

Animals attract folklore because they are familiar and unpredictable.

Common beliefs may concern:

- black or unusually pale animals;
- owls near houses;
- crows gathering;
- dogs refusing to enter a building;
- horses reacting to empty spaces;
- rats appearing before storms;
- spiders appearing in unusual numbers.

Most such events have ordinary explanations.

Some may not.

The GM should avoid making every animal omen supernatural.

## 9. Magic

Because magic is rare, ordinary people often have inaccurate beliefs about it.

Common misconceptions include:

- wizards can always tell when someone is lying;
- every magical item is dangerous;
- divine spellcasters can heal any injury;
- curses are obvious;
- all magic leaves visible signs;
- an educated scholar must know magic;
- anyone who studies books about magic can become a wizard.

These beliefs are often wrong.

Specialists know better.

Even specialists can disagree about unusual phenomena.

## 10. Elves

Common human folklore about elves may include:

- elves never forget an insult;
- an elf can always recognize a lie;
- elves sleep differently from humans;
- elves can hear conversations from impossibly far away;
- old elven objects carry luck or misfortune.

Some beliefs contain a grain of truth.

None should be treated as universal rules.

## 11. Dwarves

Common folklore about dwarves may include:

- dwarves always keep promises;
- dwarven metalwork cannot break;
- dwarves can identify valuable stone by touch;
- dwarves remember every debt;
- a dwarf who insults someone's craft is making a serious accusation.

These beliefs are exaggerated versions of real cultural tendencies.

A dwarf can still make a bad tool.

## 12. Ratfolk

Surface folklore about ratfolk is particularly vulnerable to prejudice.

False or harmful beliefs may include:

- ratfolk spread disease by nature;
- ratfolk steal everything;
- ratfolk live only underground;
- ratfolk cannot be trusted;
- ratfolk always know every sewer route;
- a ratfolk presence means criminals are nearby.

These are prejudices, not setting truths.

Ratfolk communities are diverse.

## 13. Monsters

When monsters exist, folklore about them becomes complicated.

A village may know that giant spiders exist without knowing why one appeared near a particular road.

People may exaggerate:

- size;
- intelligence;
- numbers;
- powers;
- territorial range;
- motives.

A frightened witness is often a poor natural historian.

Specialists distinguish evidence from rumor.

## 14. The dead and undead

The existence of Pharasma and the reality of divine magic make death culturally serious.

Ordinary people may still believe:

- a restless spirit remains near unfinished business;
- a corpse should never be disturbed after burial;
- certain places are "wrong" because too many people died there;
- speaking the dead person's name protects their memory;
- certain dreams mean the dead are nearby.

These are beliefs.

The existence of actual undead means some people have good reason to take certain fears seriously.

That does not make every ghost story true.

## 15. Children’s folklore

Children create their own explanations.

Common children's warnings might concern:

- abandoned wells;
- dark woods;
- old ruins;
- empty houses;
- strangers;
- deep water;
- particular animals.

A child may insist:

**"Never whistle after sunset or the forest hears you."**

Adults may laugh.

The child may still refuse to do it.

## 16. Household taboos

Small household customs can include:

- not leaving bread upside down;
- not stepping over someone working with a blade;
- not giving an empty cup as a gift;
- not sleeping with certain doors open;
- not counting money aloud at night;
- not praising a newborn too loudly;
- not announcing a journey too early.

These vary by family.

They are useful because they reveal background without requiring exposition.

## 17. Common sayings

Local sayings should often reflect practical experience.

Examples:

**"A full barn still needs a sound roof."**

Used when someone celebrates success before securing it.

**"Do not bargain with the tide."**

Used when someone is trying to control something beyond their power.

**"Stone remembers the hammer."**

A Kharadorn-influenced expression about consequences.

**"The road hears every promise."**

A central and traveler-oriented expression about reputation.

**"A warm hearth is worth two speeches."**

A northern-influenced saying about practical help.

**"Do not spend tomorrow's wind."**

A maritime expression about planning with uncertain resources.

These are sayings, not universal proverbs.

## 18. When superstition becomes dangerous

Superstition becomes socially dangerous when it causes people to:

- accuse neighbors;
- attack outsiders;
- refuse medical care;
- destroy property;
- violate graves;
- panic during unusual weather;
- blame minorities;
- interfere with legitimate religious practice;
- provoke violence.

This is especially important in a world where some supernatural dangers are real.

A false accusation can be more dangerous than the original superstition.

## 19. GM use

When introducing an unusual event, give NPCs multiple interpretations.

For example, if birds suddenly leave an area:

- one farmer says a storm is coming;
- a child says a ghost frightened them;
- a priest wonders whether something disturbed the dead;
- a ranger looks for a predator;
- a scholar asks what species left and when;
- a frightened merchant wants to leave immediately.

The event itself does not need an answer immediately.

The disagreement is part of the world.

## 20. Core principle

The setting should contain superstition without making the world stupid.

People have accumulated practical knowledge for generations.

Some beliefs are wrong.

Some are useful.

Some are religious.

Some are prejudiced.

Some are harmless habits.

And occasionally, something genuinely supernatural happens.

The difficulty is knowing which is which.
