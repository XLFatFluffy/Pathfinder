# Kharadorn Dwarven Culture and Khar-Dum

**Status:** GM-approved worldbuilding expansion  
**Baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Scope:** Kharadorn dwarven society, with emphasis on Khar-Dum and the political distinction between the ancestral homeland and Dragonstone.

## 1. The meaning of Khar-Dum

Khar-Dum is the ancestral homeland of the Kharadorn dwarves. Dragonstone is a conquered former Targaryen stronghold and the seat from which the Kharadorn ruler governs the dwarven clans and the territories around the central inland sea. These places are not interchangeable.

Khar-Dum represents continuity, ancestry, and the deep foundations of dwarven society. Dragonstone represents conquest, political authority, and the difficult work of ruling beyond the traditional homeland.

A Kharadorn may serve at Dragonstone while considering Khar-Dum the place that makes their people who they are.

## 2. The shape of the homeland

Khar-Dum lies in the northeastern mountains. Its settlements are shaped by elevation, geology, water, access to ore, and the need to move goods through difficult terrain.

Dwarven settlements are not all underground. They may include:

- deep halls and chambers;
- surface gates and fortified entrances;
- terraces and mountain farms;
- workshops near water or ventilation;
- roads cut into slopes;
- bridges and lifts;
- outlying mines;
- smelting and refining sites;
- storage halls;
- shrines and memorial chambers.

The mountain is a source of shelter and material, but it is also a hazard. Rockfall, flooding, poor air, fire, structural failure, and blocked routes are practical concerns.

## 3. Architecture

Dwarven architecture prioritizes structural reliability, controlled access, ventilation, drainage, and maintainability.

A well-built hall is not simply a grand chamber. It is an engineered environment.

Its builders consider:

- load-bearing stone;
- water channels;
- air movement;
- smoke removal;
- emergency exits;
- storage;
- defensive chokepoints;
- movement of ore and finished goods;
- long-term repair.

Ornament often records the people who built, repaired, defended, or funded a structure. A carved lintel may be both decoration and a public record of obligation.

## 4. The ordinary household

Dwarven domestic life varies by settlement and occupation.

A household may include close family, elderly kin, apprentices, or people temporarily attached through work and obligation. Multi-generational living is common in established communities, but not compulsory.

Homes often include a central room, sleeping spaces, storage, and access to shared kitchens or communal facilities where buildings are dense.

A miner's household, a merchant's townhouse, and a master craftsperson's residence can be very different.

## 5. Clan and kinship

Clan is a major source of identity, but it does not erase personal choice.

Clan records preserve ancestry, marriages, adoptions, service, disputes, debts, and notable work. A person's standing may depend partly on the reputation of their relatives, but an individual can improve or damage that reputation through their own actions.

Adoption and incorporation into a clan are meaningful social acts. Their precise legal forms vary among clans and should be recorded when a specific clan becomes important in play.

## 6. Oaths, contracts, and reputation

Dwarven culture places high value on keeping one's word, especially when the promise concerns craft, payment, protection, or clan obligation.

A broken promise is not automatically treated as unforgivable. Circumstances matter. A person who admits failure, makes restitution, and explains the cause may preserve some standing.

Concealing a defect in a commissioned weapon or structure is especially shameful because it places other lives at risk.

Contracts are often witnessed and preserved. The record of an agreement can matter as much as the agreement itself.

## 7. Craft and work

Craftsmanship is a source of identity and public standing.

Respected work includes:

- stonecutting;
- masonry;
- metalworking;
- engineering;
- mining;
- surveying;
- toolmaking;
- armor and weapon manufacture;
- jewelry;
- brewing;
- record keeping;
- transport and logistics.

Not every dwarf is a smith. Dwarven society needs cooks, guards, healers, traders, farmers, teachers, porters, and administrators.

A master craftsperson is judged by durability, function, honesty, and the ability to explain how the work was made.

## 8. Food and drink

Dwarven food depends on trade, surface agriculture, livestock, preserved goods, and what can be produced within mountain settlements.

Common foods may include:

- dense breads;
- root vegetables;
- beans;
- mushrooms;
- smoked or salted meat;
- hard cheeses;
- pickled vegetables;
- river or lake fish;
- stews;
- nuts and dried fruit.

Brewing is culturally important in many communities, but dwarves do not all drink heavily. Water, broth, small beer, and other drinks are ordinary.

Feasts are often tied to clan gatherings, completed works, remembrance, and oath ceremonies.

## 9. Education

Children learn practical skills and clan history.

Formal instruction may include reading, arithmetic, measurement, engineering principles, law, genealogy, religious practice, and craft apprenticeship.

A young dwarf may be expected to understand why a bridge is safe, how a tool should be maintained, and what a family record means before being entrusted with major responsibilities.

## 10. Religion and Torag

Torag is especially prominent among Kharadorn dwarves and craftspeople. Worship may be expressed through temple rites, household shrines, work blessings, memorial practices, and oaths.

The Kharadorn claim of descent from Torag is an important dynastic tradition. Dwarves do not universally interpret it as proof of literal divine ancestry, and disagreement about what the claim means can itself become politically sensitive.

## 11. Death and remembrance

Memorials often preserve a person's name, clan, work, service, and obligations. A structure repaired by a family may carry the names of successive generations of repairers.

The dead are not remembered only for status. A reliable miner, healer, cook, or bridge inspector may be honored because their work sustained the community.

Pharasma's rites remain important, alongside Torag-centered customs and local clan practice.

## 12. Military life

Kharadorn military culture values discipline, equipment, defensive engineering, and unit reliability.

Dwarven forces are associated with:

- shield formations;
- heavy infantry;
- engineers;
- sappers;
- siegecraft;
- fortified positions;
- tunnel and gate defense;
- disciplined logistics.

These are cultural and institutional tendencies, not a claim that every dwarf is a soldier or that dwarven forces cannot adapt.

## 13. Khar-Dum and Dragonstone

The relationship between the ancestral homeland and the conquered seat is a persistent political tension.

Some dwarves view Dragonstone as proof of Kharadorn strength and rightful imperial standing.

Others regard it as a useful but foreign seat, carrying the memory of the dynasty displaced there.

Khar-Dum's clans may support the ruler while expecting ancestral customs and clan autonomy to be respected. Dragonstone's court must govern a wider and more politically mixed population.

## 14. Internal tensions

- **Clan authority vs. central rule:** how much authority should the ruler exercise over clan affairs?
- **Ancestral custom vs. imperial necessity:** should old practices change to govern conquered territories?
- **Craft integrity vs. commercial demand:** should output increase if quality or working conditions suffer?
- **Khar-Dum vs. Dragonstone:** where does the heart of dwarven political identity reside?
- **Tradition vs. younger ambition:** who has the right to reinterpret inherited obligations?

## 15. GM portrayal

Do not portray dwarves as interchangeable gruff miners or comic drinkers. A Kharadorn may be a patient diplomat, a brilliant engineer, a poor craftsperson, a devout priest, a restless traveler, or a merchant who dislikes clan politics.

The useful cultural themes are continuity, workmanship, recorded obligation, and the tension between ancestral identity and imperial power.

**Core cultural idea:** A thing, a promise, or a community is judged by whether it can bear the weight placed upon it.
