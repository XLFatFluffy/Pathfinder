# Regional Lived Culture Field Guide

**Status:** GM-approved worldbuilding expansion  
**Baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Scope:** Practical sensory and social portrayal of established continental cultures

This file turns the broad cultural framework into table-usable examples. These are recurring patterns, not mandatory behavior. A household, village, city, or individual can differ.

No new borders, settlements, routes, population figures, or political jurisdictions are established here.

## 1. How to use this guide

When a PC enters an unfamiliar region, do not explain the entire culture.

Show three or four details:

- what people are doing;
- what they are eating or carrying;
- what they consider rude or admirable;
- what they worry about;
- what they remember.

The goal is for players to notice that the world changes before anyone tells them it has changed.

---

# 2. Edarth Heartlands

## The ordinary morning

A farm household begins with practical work rather than ceremony.

Someone checks animals.

Someone checks the weather.

Someone checks stores.

Someone begins preparing food.

Children old enough to help are expected to contribute before leisure.

A household that wastes usable food, leaves tools exposed to weather, or neglects livestock is likely to be criticized more harshly than one that lacks wealth.

## Homes

Farmhouses vary by wealth, but durability matters.

Common homes have:

- a central cooking and living space;
- storage for grain and preserved food;
- tools kept where they can be reached quickly;
- simple furniture repaired repeatedly rather than replaced casually;
- family or household items kept because they are useful or carry history.

A wealthy estate may be much more elaborate, but practical storage and agricultural work remain visible.

## Food

Common foods include:

- grain porridge;
- bread;
- root vegetables;
- beans and pulses;
- preserved meat;
- cheese;
- eggs;
- seasonal fruit;
- ale or other ordinary drink.

A harvest feast is different from ordinary eating because abundance is displayed deliberately.

## What earns respect

Competence.

A person who can repair a fence, settle a dispute, organize workers, protect a household, or keep an oath is valuable.

Courtly polish is useful but secondary.

## What causes friction

Land and customary rights.

Arguments may concern:

- pasture;
- woodland use;
- water access;
- damaged fences;
- livestock trespass;
- inheritance;
- labor obligations.

A dispute that looks trivial to an outsider may represent a custom a family has defended for generations.

## Common social lesson

**Do not promise what you cannot carry.**

# 3. Veyr Northern Territories

## The ordinary morning

People check the conditions before deciding what the day will permit.

Cold, snow, wind, damaged roads, animal health, stores, and frontier warnings matter.

A northern household may have a routine for checking fuel, food, tools, and weather before work begins.

Preparedness is not paranoia.

It is ordinary competence.

## Homes

Homes emphasize insulation, storage, repair, and efficient use of fuel.

Common features include:

- thick walls or substantial insulation;
- covered storage;
- drying areas for clothing;
- protected fuel stores;
- practical workspaces;
- sleeping arrangements that keep warmth concentrated.

A household may have fewer decorative possessions but take considerable pride in maintaining useful ones.

## Food

Preservation is central.

Common foods may include:

- stored grains;
- dried meat;
- smoked fish;
- preserved vegetables;
- hard cheeses;
- soups and stews;
- root crops;
- fermented or stored foods.

Fresh food is valued when available.

Wasting preserved food is particularly offensive because someone had to prepare it for difficult months.

## Children

Children learn practical survival skills early.

They may learn:

- how to dress for severe weather;
- how to identify safe firewood;
- how to carry messages;
- how to help with animals;
- how to recognize dangerous conditions;
- where household emergency supplies are stored.

This does not mean every northern child is a miniature soldier.

Most are simply children who have been taught that mistakes can have consequences.

## Death

The dead are remembered through names, stories, records, and obligations to the living.

Funerals may be restrained.

The emotional emphasis is often on continuity:

**Someone died. The household remains. Their duties must continue.**

## Common social lesson

**If you can prepare for a problem, prepare before it becomes a problem.**

# 4. Vaelorian Highlands and Elven Communities

## The ordinary morning

The rhythm of work can appear slower to human outsiders.

That does not mean less work is being done.

A craftsperson may spend considerable time inspecting materials before beginning.

A household may maintain objects for decades or generations.

A merchant may review records that a human counterpart would consider ancient.

## Homes

Vaelorian homes vary enormously by wealth and locality.

Established patterns include:

- careful integration with the surrounding landscape;
- durable materials;
- deliberate craftsmanship;
- spaces designed for both practical use and long-term maintenance;
- gardens or managed natural spaces where conditions permit;
- objects chosen for quality rather than novelty.

Older estates may contain rooms or objects whose original purpose has changed over generations.

## Food

Meals emphasize quality and preparation when resources allow.

Common foods remain ordinary:

- grains;
- vegetables;
- fruit;
- meat;
- fish;
- cheese;
- breads;
- preserved foods.

The cultural difference lies more in preparation, presentation, and patience than in magical ingredients or impossible cuisine.

A wealthy Vaelorian meal may contain dishes whose preparation took much longer than a human guest expected.

## Time

One of the largest cultural differences is temporal expectation.

An elf may say:

**"We can settle this next year."**

and mean that the matter is being handled promptly.

A human may hear:

**"I do not intend to settle this."**

Both interpretations can create friction.

## Memory

Vaelorians may remember:

- an insult;
- a contract;
- a favor;
- an architectural change;
- a family connection;

for far longer than humans expect.

This does not mean every elf remembers everything.

It means time does not automatically erase significance.

## Common social lesson

**If something matters, make a record of it.**

# 5. Kharadorn Dwarven Communities

## The ordinary morning

Work begins with tools.

A craftsperson checks equipment before using it.

A miner checks safety conditions.

A smith checks fuel and metal.

A household may have inherited tools that are older than several human generations.

## Homes

Dwarven construction emphasizes:

- durability;
- load-bearing reliability;
- repair access;
- storage;
- controlled heat;
- craftsmanship.

A wall is not merely a wall.

Its construction may be a point of pride.

A poorly repaired structure can be considered embarrassing even if it remains standing.

## Apprenticeship

Skill is normally demonstrated through work.

A young apprentice is expected to learn:

1. how to maintain tools;
2. how to perform basic work safely;
3. why the work is done a particular way;
4. how to recognize unacceptable work;
5. how their craft connects to clan reputation.

The point is not simply to produce workers.

It is to preserve reliable knowledge.

## Food

Dwarven food is practical and durable.

Common foods may include:

- breads;
- grains;
- roots;
- mushrooms where locally available;
- preserved meats;
- smoked foods;
- cheese;
- hearty stews;
- fermented drinks.

Not every dwarf eats enormous quantities or spends every meal drinking.

That stereotype is useful for jokes and poor for worldbuilding.

## Reputation

A person's work can become part of their family reputation.

A badly made tool may be remembered.

A masterwork may be remembered longer.

A person who admits a mistake and repairs it may preserve their reputation better than someone who hides a flaw.

## Common social lesson

**A promise is a thing you build.**

# 6. Merrowen Maritime Communities

## The ordinary morning

The day begins with conditions.

People check:

- wind;
- weather;
- tides;
- harbor conditions;
- cargo;
- ropes;
- hulls;
- tools;
- schedules.

A sailor who ignores the weather because the plan says to leave is considered foolish, not courageous.

## Homes and ports

Maritime settlements contain a mixture of permanent residents and temporary visitors.

A street may include:

- sailors;
- merchants;
- shipwrights;
- fishermen;
- pilots;
- innkeepers;
- dock laborers;
- foreign traders;
- priests;
- craftsmen;
- officials.

This produces a culture comfortable with strangers without automatically trusting them.

## Food

Common maritime foods include:

- fish;
- shellfish where available;
- bread;
- grains;
- preserved vegetables;
- dried or salted foods;
- stews;
- cheese;
- imported foods when trade permits.

Fresh food aboard a ship is limited by voyage length.

A sailor may therefore value ordinary fresh fruit or vegetables more than a wealthy landowner expects.

## Hospitality

Storms change etiquette.

A person caught in dangerous weather is more likely to receive practical assistance.

This does not mean everyone becomes generous.

It means refusing reasonable help during a genuine emergency can damage a person's reputation badly.

## Sailor superstitions

Individual sailors may maintain private rituals involving:

- touching a charm before departure;
- avoiding particular words;
- observing unusual weather signs;
- leaving a small offering;
- refusing to sail on a personally unlucky date.

These customs vary between crews.

A sailor can be devout, skeptical, superstitious, or all three.

## Common social lesson

**Respect the conditions, because the sea does not care about your plans.**

# 7. Crownlands and Central River Communities

## The ordinary morning

Life is shaped by administration as much as agriculture.

People encounter:

- tolls;
- charters;
- market rules;
- written contracts;
- royal officers;
- merchants;
- travelers;
- bridge and road maintenance.

A person may complain about bureaucracy while relying on the same system to enforce a contract.

## Written records

In central communities, written documents are especially useful.

People may keep:

- receipts;
- contracts;
- debt records;
- property documents;
- letters;
- guild records;
- family records.

A person who cannot read may still understand the importance of having a trustworthy scribe.

## Food

Central markets have greater variety because goods travel through them.

Local staples remain important, but travelers can encounter food from distant regions.

This makes central markets culturally mixed as well as commercially useful.

## Common social lesson

**Get it written down.**

# 8. Southern Reaches

The Southern Reaches are too politically fragmented for one uniform culture.

## Southwestern Marches

Water is a practical concern.

People respect:

- wells;
- cisterns;
- water rights;
- caravan agreements;
- shade;
- reliable guides.

A stranger who wastes drinking water may be judged more harshly than someone who wastes food.

## Southern Principalities

Local identity is often stronger than continental identity.

A person may identify first with:

- a city;
- a valley;
- an island;
- a ruling family;
- a profession.

The same title may carry different social expectations in different principalities.

## Southern Islands

Ships and captains may hold considerable social influence.

A captain's reputation can affect:

- employment;
- trade;
- marriage prospects;
- credit;
- access to ships.

Islanders may regard mainland officials as distant outsiders even when technically subject to them.

# 9. Ratfolk Communities

Ratfolk culture must never be treated as one standardized society.

## Household life

Mutual aid is often practical.

People may share:

- food;
- tools;
- information;
- childcare;
- safe routes;
- repair skills;
- emergency shelter.

This does not eliminate arguments.

It creates a culture in which refusing reasonable help may have social consequences.

## Surface interaction

A ratfolk person may have learned to read surface behavior carefully.

They may know:

- which shopkeepers are fair;
- which streets are hostile;
- which officials are trustworthy;
- which businesses refuse ratfolk customers;
- which rumors are dangerous.

This knowledge is local and personal.

It should not be assumed that every ratfolk knows the same information.

## Names and identity

Different ratfolk communities may use different naming practices.

A descriptive name, occupational nickname, family name, inherited name, or personal name can all exist.

No single naming convention should be imposed on all ratfolk.

## Common social lesson

**Know who will help when the door closes.**

# 10. Cross-cultural misunderstandings

The most useful cultural conflicts are often small.

An Edarth farmer may consider a Vaelorian's careful deliberation evasive.

A Vaelorian may consider the farmer's immediate demand for an answer reckless.

A Kharadorn may consider a human's casual promise meaningless.

A human may think the dwarf is being excessively formal.

A Veyr traveler may see a Merrowen sailor's superstition as frivolous.

The sailor may consider the northerner's refusal to acknowledge luck dangerously arrogant.

None of these interpretations is objectively correct.

## 11. What travelers learn quickly

Travelers who pay attention eventually notice:

- food changes before accents do;
- clothing changes before political banners do;
- greetings change before laws do;
- hospitality changes before architecture does;
- what people fear reveals more than what they claim to value.

This gives PCs a way to learn the world naturally.

# 12. Sensory shorthand for the GM

When portraying a region, use recurring sensory details.

### Edarth

- grain;
- horse sweat;
- wood smoke;
- leather;
- freshly turned soil;
- bells or farm sounds;
- large shared meals during festivals.

### Veyr

- cold air;
- smoke;
- dried meat;
- wool;
- wet leather;
- heavy doors;
- stacked fuel;
- quiet attention to weather.

### Vaelorian

- worked wood or stone;
- gardens;
- ink;
- parchment;
- polished metal;
- carefully maintained textiles;
- deliberate speech;
- old objects still in use.

### Kharadorn

- forge heat;
- stone dust;
- metal;
- oil;
- leather;
- worked timber;
- controlled echoes;
- tools being maintained.

### Merrowen

- salt;
- tar;
- rope;
- wet timber;
- fish;
- spices from trade;
- harbor noise;
- changing crowds.

### Crownlands

- roads;
- horses;
- market noise;
- paper;
- bells;
- mixed accents;
- carts;
- public notices.

### Southern Reaches

- region-dependent;
- dry stone and dust in the marches;
- river and market smells in fertile principalities;
- salt and wind in island communities.

# 13. The important exception

Do not use this guide to announce what an NPC believes.

Use it to decide what the NPC might have learned.

A Veyr farmer can love poetry.

A Vaelorian can be impatient.

A Kharadorn can hate smithing.

A Merrowen sailor can be terrified of boats.

An Edarth noble can dislike military life.

A ratfolk merchant can be wealthy and socially confident.

Culture creates pressures.

It does not replace personality.

---

# 14. The table test

If a player asks:

**"How do I know this person is from Veyr?"**

Do not answer with a paragraph.

Give them something they can observe:

- the way the traveler checks the weather;
- the way they protect their supplies;
- the way they speak about preparedness;
- the condition of their clothing;
- the practical design of their equipment.

If they ask:

**"How do I know this person is Vaelorian?"**

Show them:

- careful workmanship;
- an old object still maintained;
- deliberate speech;
- a concern with precedent;
- a piece of craftsmanship whose quality is obvious.

The setting should be discoverable.

Players should be able to learn it by paying attention.
