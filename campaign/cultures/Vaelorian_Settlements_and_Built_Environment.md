# Vaelorian Settlements and Built Environment Field Guide

**Status:** GM-approved worldbuilding expansion  
**Baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Scope:** The established Vaelorian settlement network and the physical environments in which its people live.  
**Canon rule:** This guide adds character and lived detail without establishing exact borders, coordinates, travel times, road courses, or surveyed geography.

## 1. What Vaelorian settlement feels like

A traveler entering Vaelorian territory does not suddenly find a single elven city repeated across the mountains.

The established landscape contains high peaks, forested slopes, sheltered valleys, mines, farms, estates, passes, roads, and commercial communities. Settlement therefore follows opportunity and terrain.

A typical settled valley may contain several layers at once:

- an old elven core or estate;
- newer houses and workshops;
- farms, orchards, or terraces;
- a road or bridge;
- woodland managed for timber, hunting, or protection;
- a shrine or small religious site;
- warehouses or merchant yards if trade is important;
- older walls, foundations, or boundary markers whose original purpose may no longer be obvious.

The important visual distinction is not that everything is ancient. It is that old things are commonly incorporated into newer life rather than automatically demolished.

A building may have a foundation centuries older than its roof. A road may have been widened without abandoning its older alignment. A family house may contain a modern workshop inside an inherited structure.

## 2. The ordinary Vaelorian house

The ordinary established household should look lived-in rather than palatial.

A common house may be built around a compact courtyard, a central room, or a narrow garden depending on available land.

Typical features include:

- stone or masonry lower walls;
- timber-framed or plastered upper sections;
- slate or tile roofing;
- deep eaves where mountain weather demands them;
- fitted shutters;
- storage niches;
- hooks and racks for tools;
- a hearth or stove;
- a small work area;
- carefully maintained drainage;
- a cellar, cool room, loft, or other food-storage space where construction permits.

Windows are not automatically enormous. Good glazing is expensive, and mountain weather rewards practical design.

An established household may own furniture that looks older than the people currently using it. That is not necessarily a sign of poverty. A well-maintained inherited table can be a respectable household possession.

## 3. Poor and working households

Long elven lifespans do not eliminate poverty.

Miners, laborers, servants, small farmers, apprentices, porters, and other workers may live in:

- rented rooms;
- crowded townhouses;
- worker housing near mines or workshops;
- subdivided older buildings;
- small cottages;
- mixed-ancestry neighborhoods.

A poor Vaelorian home may contain old objects precisely because replacing them is unaffordable.

The difference between wealthy and poor households is therefore often visible in space, privacy, repair quality, food security, heating, clothing, and access to skilled labor rather than in whether the furniture is old.

## 4. Estates and old families

An old elven estate is not necessarily a giant castle.

Many estates are better understood as productive household complexes containing some combination of:

- a principal residence;
- gardens;
- orchards;
- workshops;
- barns and animal shelters;
- guest accommodation;
- storage buildings;
- servants' quarters;
- walls or hedges;
- old family memorials;
- woodland rights;
- tenant or dependent households.

The age of an estate can make its boundaries socially complicated. A wall, tree line, spring, bridge, or old marker may have legal or customary significance even when its original construction is forgotten.

Old families may therefore care intensely about maintenance of seemingly insignificant features.

## 5. Terraces, orchards, and valley agriculture

The Green Valleys support productive land where terrain allows.

Agricultural landscapes may include:

- terraced fields;
- orchards;
- vegetable gardens;
- grazing land;
- vineyards or other locally suitable plantings;
- managed woodland;
- stone retaining walls;
- irrigation or drainage channels.

Terracing is expensive to build and maintain. A neglected terrace can collapse, lose soil, or become difficult to cultivate.

This makes land maintenance a visible measure of competence. A wealthy family with beautiful halls but collapsing retaining walls may be mocked as wasteful.

## 6. Mining communities

Silverfall is established as a precious-metal and gemstone mining center.

A mining settlement is practical and noisy compared with the romantic image of an elven mountain town.

Expect:

- ore storage;
- workshops;
- timber yards;
- pack animals;
- tool repair;
- assay and weighing facilities;
- food sellers;
- taverns or boarding houses;
- workers' housing;
- merchants;
- guards;
- engineers and skilled craftsmen;
- waste rock and disturbed ground.

The surrounding landscape bears evidence of extraction.

Mine wealth does not remain underground. It creates roads, demand for food and timber, skilled employment, disputes over rights, and opportunities for smuggling or theft.

## 7. Thornvale

Thornvale is established as a forested valley with estates and agriculture.

Its identity should feel different from Silverfall.

The landscape is likely to be quieter and more enclosed, with:

- woodland edges;
- orchards and cultivated clearings;
- old estate properties;
- smaller farms;
- maintained paths;
- streams and drainage works;
- hunting or forestry activity;
- workshops serving agricultural households.

Thornvale is a useful example of the tension between old landholding customs and newer commercial interests.

A local dispute may concern a road, timber right, orchard boundary, inheritance, hunting privilege, or access to water rather than something dramatic enough to interest a king.

That is precisely why it matters.

## 8. Goldmere

Goldmere is established as a commercial crossroads.

Its character is therefore more mixed than a secluded elven settlement.

Travelers may encounter:

- inns;
- warehouses;
- merchant offices;
- workshops;
- stables;
- pack animals;
- carts;
- traders from outside Vaelorian territory;
- human and dwarven visitors;
- local landholders;
- scribes and brokers;
- guards concerned with commercial security.

Goldmere demonstrates the cosmopolitan side of Vaelorian society.

An elf from an old estate may find the town noisy, crowded, and socially exhausting. A young merchant may regard the same town as one of the most interesting places in the world.

Neither reaction is unusual.

## 9. Aurelith

Aurelith is the established political and commercial center of House Vaelorian.

It should not be treated as merely a larger version of Goldmere.

Aurelith contains the institutions that allow Vaelorian wealth and political influence to function:

- House administration;
- elite residences;
- commercial offices;
- financial activity;
- skilled workshops;
- religious institutions;
- archives and records;
- diplomatic and legal activity;
- markets serving both local residents and travelers;
- military and household facilities appropriate to a Great House seat.

The city should visibly contain multiple ages of construction.

An older elven quarter may preserve narrow streets, stone foundations, gardens, courtyards, and family buildings. Newer commercial districts may be more mixed in construction and population.

The richest buildings are not necessarily the tallest. A great house may prefer a broad, carefully maintained compound to a monumental tower.

## 10. Public buildings and civic maintenance

Vaelorian communities tend to take infrastructure personally.

A bridge, fountain, retaining wall, public stair, drainage channel, or road surface may be associated with a family, guild, temple, or civic endowment.

Maintenance therefore has social meaning.

A damaged public structure can produce questions such as:

- Who is responsible for repairing it?
- Who originally funded it?
- Who has the right to alter it?
- Who benefits from it?
- Which records prove the obligation?
- Has the responsibility been neglected for years?

This can turn something as boring as a broken bridge rail into a legitimate political dispute. Civilization is apparently incapable of surviving without paperwork.

## 11. Water

Mountain settlement depends heavily on reliable water management.

Communities may rely on:

- springs;
- streams;
- wells where groundwater permits;
- cisterns;
- channels;
- carefully maintained drainage.

Water systems are practical infrastructure, not magical conveniences.

A household may have a spring-fed basin, shared public source, rain collection, or purchased access depending on settlement and wealth.

Polluting a water source can therefore be treated as a serious offense even where no written law explicitly describes the exact act.

## 12. Roads and travel

Established Vaelorian roads are shaped by mountains.

They favor:

- maintained passes;
- bridges;
- switchbacks where necessary;
- retaining walls;
- sheltered stopping places;
- defensible approaches;
- known water sources.

This guide does **not** establish exact road courses or travel times.

A road shown on an eventual atlas should be treated as canonical only after it has been reconciled with the authoritative map record.

## 13. The visible marks of elven longevity

The strongest visual sign of Vaelorian longevity is not giant statues or impossibly ancient ruins.

It is continuity.

A traveler might see:

- a doorway repaired several times without being replaced;
- a family garden whose layout has remained recognizable for generations;
- a public inscription whose original language is now archaic;
- a bridge whose foundations have been reused;
- a workshop still operated by descendants of the original craft family;
- old portraits hanging beside newer ones;
- an estate whose household records have outlived several human dynasties.

This makes Vaelorian history feel present rather than archaeological.

## 14. Mixed populations

Vaelorian towns are not exclusively elven.

Humans, dwarves, half-elves, halflings, ratfolk, and other peoples may live, work, trade, or travel there depending on the settlement.

Population balance varies.

A remote old estate may be overwhelmingly elven. A mining center or commercial crossroads may be substantially more mixed.

Outsiders should not assume that an elven city means every resident has the same customs.

## 15. What outsiders often misunderstand

Humans sometimes interpret Vaelorian maintenance as vanity.

Elves sometimes interpret human rebuilding as carelessness.

Both judgments can be wrong.

A human merchant may replace a building because it is no longer economically useful. An elf may preserve it because the building contains legal, familial, or cultural significance.

Conversely, an old elven structure may be preserved simply because replacing it would be expensive.

The real distinction is often not age.

It is what the people using the building believe they owe to the past.

## 16. Player-facing knowledge

A character familiar with the wider realm would reasonably know:

- Vaelorian lands are mountainous and heavily forested in places.
- The region is known for mines, craftsmanship, estates, and trade.
- Aurelith is House Vaelorian's seat.
- Silverfall is associated with precious metals and gemstones.
- Thornvale is associated with forests, estates, and agriculture.
- Goldmere is an important commercial crossroads.
- Elven communities often preserve old buildings and family traditions.
- House Vaelorian is wealthy and commercially powerful.

A character would not automatically know:

- exact estate boundaries;
- the history of a particular building;
- who owns a specific mine;
- the obligations attached to an old bridge or road;
- which old families hold which customary rights;
- private political disputes inside Aurelith;
- hidden passages, ruins, or secret chambers.

## 17. GM sensory shorthand

When the party enters Vaelorian territory, useful details include:

**Sound:** water over stone, distant hammering, cart wheels, wind through high forests, bells or chimes, careful conversation in courtyards.

**Smell:** woodsmoke, damp stone, bread, fruit, worked metal, pine or other forest scents, animal feed, mountain air.

**Sight:** stone retaining walls, slate roofs, carved wood, terraces, orchards, old foundations, carefully maintained tools, mixed old and new construction.

**Social feeling:** people notice workmanship, manners, names, affiliations, and whether visitors respect established spaces.

## 18. GM rule for Vaelorian settlement design

Do not turn every Vaelorian location into an elven palace.

Most people live in houses, apartments, workshops, farms, worker quarters, inns, and ordinary rented rooms.

The distinctive Vaelorian quality comes from:

- long-term maintenance;
- accumulated history;
- strong craftsmanship;
- old property relationships;
- mountain adaptation;
- commercial wealth;
- coexistence between ancient customs and newer trade.

The result should feel like a functioning society that has been continuously inhabited, not a theme park built by immortal architects.
