# Veyr Settlements and Frontier Infrastructure Field Guide

**Status:** GM-approved worldbuilding expansion  
**Baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Scope:** The physical environment, settlement infrastructure, and ordinary built life of the Veyr Northern Territories.  
**Canon rule:** This guide expands established northern culture without creating new settlements, borders, coordinates, routes, travel times, or unconfirmed fortifications.

## 1. What Veyr territory feels like

Veyr country is defined by distance and adaptation.

The north contains forests, exposed uplands, agricultural settlements, roads, scattered communities, fortified sites, and the infrastructure needed to support the Ancient Barrier.

The landscape should not be portrayed as an endless frozen wasteland.

People live ordinary lives there.

Fields are worked. Animals are raised. Timber is cut. Goods are traded. Children grow up. Inns serve travelers. Smiths repair tools. Families argue about inheritance. Someone is always complaining about the weather.

The difference is that northern communities build ordinary life around the possibility that conditions may become dangerous.

## 2. Settlement placement

Established northern settlements tend to gather around practical necessities:

- reliable water;
- defensible ground;
- timber;
- grazing;
- workable farmland;
- roads and crossings;
- access to trade;
- communication with neighboring communities;
- proximity to frontier responsibilities where relevant.

A settlement may therefore look less aesthetically planned than a wealthy southern city.

Its location makes sense first.

## 3. The ordinary northern house

A typical northern house is designed around heat, storage, weather protection, and repair.

Common features may include:

- stone or packed-earth foundations;
- timber framing;
- thick or insulated walls;
- steep or strongly pitched roofs where snowfall requires them;
- shutters;
- storm doors;
- central hearths;
- enclosed storage;
- protected firewood;
- drying space for clothing;
- sheltered animal areas.

A household with limited resources may have fewer rooms rather than poorer-quality principles.

The family may simply make every available space serve several purposes.

A table can be used for meals, mending, accounting, and children's work.

A sleeping room may also store winter clothing.

A shed may contain tools, fuel, and feed.

## 4. Winter changes the interior

Northern buildings are organized around keeping useful warmth where people actually live.

During difficult weather:

- doors are kept closed;
- wet clothing is dried carefully;
- boots are positioned near but not dangerously close to heat;
- fuel is rationed;
- animals may be brought into sheltered spaces;
- food preparation becomes concentrated around the hearth;
- work moves indoors when possible.

A house can therefore feel more crowded during severe weather without having gained a single new resident.

## 5. Firewood and fuel

Fuel is infrastructure.

A household may maintain:

- stacked firewood;
- kindling;
- charcoal for specialized work;
- peat or other locally appropriate fuel where established;
- covered fuel stores.

Fuel theft is serious because it can become a survival issue.

A household that has food but no means to cook it or heat itself is still in danger.

Northern communities therefore notice fuel shortages quickly.

## 6. Barns, sheds, and animal shelter

The main house is only one part of a northern property.

A working household may maintain:

- barns;
- sheds;
- animal pens;
- hay storage;
- grain storage;
- tool shelters;
- fenced yards;
- drying racks;
- workshops.

These structures may be less attractive than the dwelling and more important to the household's survival.

A wealthy estate can therefore look impressive while still containing a great deal of plain agricultural infrastructure.

## 7. Roads and winter travel

Northern roads are not simply southern roads covered in snow.

Their maintenance is a continuing labor problem.

Communities may need to:

- clear fallen timber;
- repair bridges;
- stabilize muddy sections;
- clear snow;
- mark dangerous stretches;
- maintain drainage;
- repair ruts;
- restore damaged retaining works.

A route that is reliable in one season may become slow or dangerous in another.

Do not assume that a road remains equally usable throughout the year.

## 8. Inns and stopping places

A northern inn serves practical needs as much as social ones.

A useful stopping place may provide:

- hot food;
- warmth;
- dry sleeping space;
- stable room;
- water;
- feed for animals;
- a place to dry equipment;
- local information.

A good innkeeper knows the condition of nearby roads and dangerous stretches.

A traveler who ignores local warnings may be judged foolish rather than adventurous.

## 9. Markets

Northern markets carry a mixture of local production and imported necessities.

Local trade may include:

- grain;
- livestock;
- hides;
- wool;
- timber;
- firewood;
- preserved food;
- tools;
- leather goods;
- clothing;
- household items.

Imported goods may include items that northern communities cannot easily produce themselves.

The existence of a market does not imply abundance.

A market can be busy because people are searching for something scarce.

## 10. Administrative buildings

A local seat of authority does not need to be a grand castle.

It may consist of:

- a hall;
- offices;
- a storehouse;
- a guard post;
- a stable;
- a meeting room;
- records storage;
- a small residence for an official.

Written orders may take time to arrive from distant authorities.

This makes local knowledge and local relationships unusually important.

A reeve who knows which bridge is failing can sometimes be more useful than a distant official with a perfectly written decree.

## 11. The Ancient Barrier as infrastructure

The Ancient Barrier is not merely a wall or military monument.

Its operation creates an entire support network.

That network requires:

- roads;
- supply depots;
- workshops;
- food stores;
- animal transport;
- timber;
- stone;
- metal;
- tools;
- communications;
- medical support;
- trained personnel;
- administrative records.

People who never serve at the Barrier may still spend part of their lives supporting it.

A farmer may sell grain to a supply contractor.

A blacksmith may repair equipment intended for frontier service.

A teamster may haul supplies.

A mason may repair a structure.

A child may grow up knowing that a particular family member is away on Barrier service.

## 12. Barrier communities

Settlements supporting the Barrier may contain a mixture of military and civilian life.

A community could include:

- soldiers;
- veterans;
- farmers;
- merchants;
- smiths;
- carpenters;
- masons;
- teamsters;
- cooks;
- healers;
- clergy;
- families;
- travelers;
- government personnel.

This produces tension.

Military priorities may demand supplies that civilians also need.

A garrison may want timber for repairs.

A village may need the same timber for winter fuel.

Neither side has to be evil for the conflict to become serious.

## 13. Fortifications

Northern defensive structures should show maintenance rather than fantasy perfection.

A fortified site may have:

- walls;
- gates;
- towers;
- ditches;
- stockades;
- barracks;
- storehouses;
- workshops;
- signal positions.

Age matters.

A structure maintained for generations may contain repairs from different periods.

Some sections may be newer than others.

A wall can be formidable without being pristine.

## 14. Watch posts and communication

Distance makes information valuable.

Northern communities may use:

- mounted messengers;
- runners;
- signal fires where appropriate;
- bells;
- horns;
- flags;
- written dispatches;
- trusted travelers.

No single communication system should be assumed to cover the entire north.

Different settlements use whatever is practical for their terrain and political importance.

## 15. Agriculture in a difficult climate

Northern agriculture is not necessarily primitive.

It is adapted.

Farmers may emphasize:

- hardy grains;
- root vegetables;
- legumes;
- hay;
- livestock;
- protected gardens;
- preservation;
- careful storage.

The exact crops vary with local conditions.

A productive northern farm is therefore often judged by reliability rather than spectacular abundance.

## 16. Food storage

Storage is a visible part of northern architecture.

A household may maintain:

- grain bins;
- barrels;
- root cellars;
- hanging meat;
- smoked foods;
- dried produce;
- preserved fish;
- hard cheeses;
- sealed containers.

Storage rooms are not decorative.

A household may inspect them repeatedly for:

- damp;
- pests;
- rot;
- theft;
- spoilage.

A missing sack of grain can matter more than a missing luxury object.

## 17. Clothing and repair

Northern clothing is built around layers.

Common materials include:

- wool;
- leather;
- fur where available;
- linen;
- heavy woven cloth.

Clothing is repaired repeatedly.

A patched coat is not automatically shameful.

A coat that has been repaired intelligently may be considered evidence of competence.

People notice whether boots are sound, gloves are intact, and outer clothing is appropriate for the weather.

## 18. Social meaning of preparedness

Preparedness is visible.

People notice:

- whether a traveler carries enough clothing;
- whether a horse is properly equipped;
- whether a household has fuel;
- whether a merchant protects cargo from weather;
- whether a guest wastes food;
- whether a person understands local conditions.

A stranger who arrives badly equipped may receive help.

They may also become the subject of local gossip.

## 19. Ordinary life versus the frontier

Not every northern person thinks about the Barrier every day.

For a farmer far from the frontier, life may revolve around:

- crops;
- livestock;
- family;
- local disputes;
- weather;
- markets;
- taxes;
- religion;
- marriage;
- work.

The Barrier becomes important when:

- supplies are requested;
- relatives serve there;
- a veteran returns;
- rumors arrive;
- patrols pass through;
- an official announces new obligations.

This distinction keeps the north from becoming a military theme park.

## 20. Player-facing knowledge

A character familiar with the realm would reasonably know:

- Veyr territory is northern, colder, and more sparsely settled than the central regions.
- Northern communities emphasize preparation and practical cooperation.
- The Ancient Barrier is a major institution and defense responsibility.
- The north relies heavily on stored food, fuel, durable clothing, and maintained roads.
- Travelers should take weather and local warnings seriously.
- Veyr's people are not uniformly hostile, grim, or impoverished.

A character would not automatically know:

- the exact condition of a particular road;
- current Barrier staffing;
- private supply shortages;
- local disputes;
- the defenses of a particular fortification;
- which families are secretly unhappy with House Veyr;
- what dangers lie beyond an established frontier location.

## 21. GM sensory shorthand

**Sound:** axe blows, saws, livestock, boots on packed ground, wind through timber, wagon wheels, hammering metal, distant horns.

**Smell:** woodsmoke, wool, leather, wet earth, animal feed, stew, pine or other local forest scents, cold air.

**Sight:** steep roofs, stacked firewood, heavy doors, repaired tools, barns, fenced yards, smoke from chimneys, worn roads, supply carts.

**Social feeling:** people notice preparedness. A competent stranger earns respect quickly. A careless one may become memorable for all the wrong reasons.

## 22. GM rule for Veyr

Do not portray the north as uniformly bleak.

The defining feature is not misery.

It is **consequence**.

People understand that preparation, cooperation, and maintenance matter because failures can become dangerous.

That principle should appear in houses, roads, markets, family relationships, military service, and ordinary conversation.
