# Continental Economic Network

**Status:** GM-approved worldbuilding framework  
**Baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Purpose:** Explain the continent's established production, trade, finance, and resource dependencies as context for GM decisions.

## 1. Economic principles

The continent's economy is primarily agricultural, feudal, maritime, and regional.

The world does not use a modern national economy. Production, taxation, tolls, credit, household obligations, guild privileges, and local markets overlap.

Economic changes should normally appear first as:

- local price pressure;
- delayed deliveries;
- shortages;
- altered tolls;
- disputes over rights;
- merchant substitution;
- debt;
- reduced tax receipts;
- labor pressure;
- political lobbying.

A failed harvest does not instantly produce continent-wide famine. Effects travel through trade, reserves, transport capacity, and political decisions.

## 2. Major economic systems

### Agriculture

The major agricultural base is concentrated in:

- Edarth's Western Heartlands;
- Calderan's central Crownlands;
- the fertile parts of the Southern Principalities;
- local agricultural estates throughout the northern and eastern territories.

Core outputs include grain, livestock, dairy, wool, hides, and orchard produce.

**Primary dependency:** Veyr, Kharadorn, Merrowen, and major cities depend on reliable grain movement from productive regions.

### Timber and woodland products

Major woodland resources are associated with:

- Veyr's Pine Marches and northern forests;
- Vaelorian's western forests and highland estates;
- local managed woodlands throughout the continent.

Outputs include timber, charcoal, pitch, poles, fuel, and game.

**Primary dependency:** shipbuilding, construction, mining support, heating, and military logistics.

### Metals and stone

Kharadorn is the continent's most important established source of dwarven metalwork, engineering, and specialist manufactured goods.

Vaelorian territory contains important mineral resources and mining wealth.

Edarth and other agricultural regions require imported metal goods for tools, arms, fittings, and infrastructure.

Stonehollow and other regional quarries provide ordinary building stone; local quarrying does not imply access to dwarven metallurgy.

### Maritime trade

Merrowen's power rests heavily on shipping, ports, maritime knowledge, and naval capability.

The Central Inland Sea provides a second major maritime system under Kharadorn influence.

The two maritime systems interact with inland roads rather than replacing them.

### Finance and credit

Vaelorian commercial power includes lending, finance, trade connections, and access to capital.

Credit is politically important because noble houses, merchants, and the Crown may require money before taxes, harvests, or trade revenues are available.

Credit relationships should be treated as leverage, not as automatic ownership.

## 4. Continental trade corridors

The established major corridors are:

### Northern Road
Moves grain, manufactured goods, livestock, military supplies, timber, and northern resources between the frontier and central markets.

**Strategic importance:** Very high.

### Western Mountain Road
Connects western highland resources, mining communities, and inland markets.

**Strategic importance:** High.

### King's Road
Connects the central Crownlands with major political and commercial centers.

**Strategic importance:** Very high.

### Inland Sea Route
Carries bulk goods, metalwork, food, timber, and high-value cargo across the Central Inland Sea.

**Strategic importance:** Very high.

### Eastern Coastal Road
Links coastal markets and ports.

**Strategic importance:** High.

### Southern Trade Road
Connects the fragmented Southern Reaches with larger markets.

**Strategic importance:** Medium to high, depending on season and local security.

Exact road courses remain subject to cartographic tracing. The economic relationship exists independently of final map geometry.

## 6. Structural economic dependencies

These relationships shape the choices available to houses, merchants, towns, and households. They are structural conditions, not automatic crises or scheduled events.

### Grain dependency
Northern and dwarven populations require reliable food imports.

Possible consequences of disruption:

1. higher local prices;
2. merchant hoarding;
3. political pressure;
4. substitution with livestock or stored grain;
5. increased Crown or noble requisitions;
6. localized unrest if prolonged.

### Timber dependency
Shipbuilding, construction, mining support, and winter fuel require timber.

Possible consequences:

- increased prices;
- disputes over woodland rights;
- accelerated cutting;
- conflict with customary users;
- reduced future production.

### Metal dependency
Agricultural regions require tools, weapons, fittings, and manufactured metal.

Possible consequences:

- reduced tool availability;
- higher repair costs;
- delayed construction;
- military procurement pressure.

### Maritime dependency
Ports depend on inland food and raw materials while inland regions depend on maritime transport for some goods.

Possible consequences of maritime disruption:

- shortages in coastal markets;
- rerouting through roads;
- higher insurance and shipping costs;
- political pressure on naval powers.

### Credit dependency
The Crown and noble houses may depend on lenders and merchant credit.

Possible consequences:

- delayed payments;
- political concessions;
- debt restructuring;
- loss of commercial confidence;
- factional influence.

## 7. Understanding economic disruption

Economic relationships do not produce automatic outcomes. When a shortage, transport problem, price dispute, or loss of production becomes relevant to play, the GM should consider the specific circumstances:

- Which producers and households are directly affected?
- What goods are actually missing, delayed, damaged, or more expensive?
- Which roads, rivers, ports, mills, or markets are involved?
- What stores, credit, substitute suppliers, or alternative routes are available?
- Who has the means to absorb the loss, and who cannot?
- Which rights, contracts, customs, or political interests complicate a response?
- What would the people involved reasonably know, attempt, and risk?

A delayed barge, a poor harvest on one estate, and a prolonged interruption of a major trade corridor are different problems. Their consequences depend on duration, scale, alternatives, and the choices of affected people. Use these relationships as context for GM judgment, not as a prescribed event chain.

## 8. Economic actors

The most important categories are:

- Great Houses;
- minor houses;
- Crown officials;
- merchants;
- guilds;
- shipowners;
- lenders;
- farmers and estate managers;
- miners;
- craftsmen;
- transport operators;
- harbor authorities;
- religious institutions;
- independent southern city governments.

No category automatically acts as a unified faction.

## 9. Economic rights

The following must remain distinct:

- ownership;
- tenancy;
- customary use;
- chartered market rights;
- toll rights;
- ferry rights;
- harbor rights;
- mineral rights;
- woodland rights;
- tax authority;
- military protection.

A lord possessing one right does not automatically possess all others.

## 10. Tallowmere's place in the continental network

Tallowmere is a regional node rather than a continent-scale power.

Its importance comes from:

- the River Vale;
- access to the Central Inland Sea;
- local agriculture;
- quarrying;
- timber and charcoal;
- fishing and shore trade;
- its relationship with House Denvor;
- its chartered relationship with House Kharadorn.

Tallowmere's local disputes can therefore produce wider effects when they disrupt:

- grain movement;
- river traffic;
- quarry supply;
- timber;
- harbor activity;
- road access.

## 11. Economic reference limits

Do not assign precise production quantities, continental prices, tax totals, or market volumes without an established source. Existing records describe broad strengths, dependencies, routes, and rights. They do not constitute a complete census of production or a numerical economic model.

When a specific price or quantity matters at the table, establish it for the relevant place, date, quality, and circumstances. Avoid presenting a local estimate as a universal continental rate.

## 12. Current baseline

- No continent-wide trade collapse is established.
- No major port blockade is established.
- No major road is declared closed.
- No general currency crisis is established.

- The structural economic dependencies above are available as context for GM decisions.
