# Markets, Merchants, Guilds, and Commercial Contracts

**Snapshot:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Status:** GM reference; established economic structure with clearly marked working practices  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY  
**Related records:** [Continental Economic Network](Continental_Economic_Network.md), [Everyday Life and Material Culture](../world/Everyday_Life_and_Material_Culture.md), [Agriculture, Food, and Seasonal Work](../world/Agriculture_Food_and_Seasonal_Work.md), [Law, Justice, and Crime](../world/Law_Justice_and_Crime.md), [Travel and Communication](../world/Travel_and_Communication.md)

This record explains how goods pass between producers, carriers, merchants, workshops, markets, ports, and customers. It is intended to make commercial scenes credible and give the GM a practical way to think about disputes, delays, and economic influence.

The continent has no single uniform commercial code or market authority. Rights vary by town charter, lordship, port, guild privilege, custom, and contract. The working practices below are plausible tools for portrayal, not a declaration that every settlement uses the same system.

## 1. The commercial chain

A finished good may pass through several hands before reaching a buyer. A sack of grain might be grown by a tenant household, collected as rent or sold to a factor, moved by cart to a mill, ground by a miller, transported by barge, sold by a wholesaler, baked by a baker, and finally purchased by a household.

Each stage adds cost and risk:
- labor and handling;
- storage and spoilage;
- transport and animal upkeep;
- tolls, dues, and market fees;
- losses, theft, and damage;
- financing and delayed payment;
- the profit required by each participant.

A high market price does not necessarily mean the farmer received a high price. A low farm-gate price does not guarantee cheap bread in a city. The distance, number of intermediaries, market privileges, transport conditions, and bargaining power all matter.

Not every commodity follows the same chain. Fresh fish moves quickly; salt fish can travel farther. Grain can be stored but is bulky. Fine cloth is valuable relative to its weight. Timber is difficult to move overland. Metal goods can be expensive to produce but durable to transport.

## 2. The people who make commerce work

### Producers

Farmers, fishers, foresters, miners, quarry workers, and craftspeople produce raw materials or finished goods. Their position varies. Some sell directly; others owe rents, dues, shares, or deliveries to a landholder or chartered institution.

### Factors and agents

A factor acts for a merchant, estate, lender, or distant house. They may buy goods, arrange transport, inspect quality, collect debts, negotiate contracts, and report market conditions. A factor's authority is limited by the instructions and funds entrusted to them.

### Carriers

Carters, drovers, boatmen, sailors, porters, and caravan leaders move goods. They may own their animals or vessels, work for a carrier, or be hired for a particular journey. The carrier is responsible for some risks but not automatically every loss that occurs during transport.

### Wholesalers and merchants

Merchants buy in quantity, connect markets, finance shipments, hold stock, and accept risks that smaller producers cannot. A merchant may specialize in one commodity or trade across several. Wealth does not mean a merchant controls every stage of a trade.

### Retailers and shopkeepers

Retailers sell in smaller quantities to households and travelers. They depend on stock, cash flow, reliable suppliers, suitable premises, and permission to trade. A shop may be family-run, attached to a workshop, leased, or operated under a charter or guild privilege.

### Craftspeople and workshops

A craft workshop transforms materials into useful goods. Apprentices, journeymen, masters, household workers, and hired labor may contribute. The exact organization differs by trade and town. Not every craft is controlled by a formal guild.

### Brokers and money handlers

Brokers connect buyers and sellers, arrange introductions, compare offers, and sometimes negotiate on commission. Money changers, lenders, and account keepers help merchants handle different coinages, debts, deposits, and long-distance payments.

### Officials and inspectors

Market officers, toll collectors, harbor authorities, magistrates, weighers, and charter officials enforce particular rights. Their authority should be tied to a named office, charter, or local custom rather than assumed from their presence in a market.

## 3. The market as a place

A market is more than a collection of stalls. It is a regulated place where people gather to buy, sell, exchange news, arrange labor, settle accounts, and observe one another.

A larger market may include:
- open stalls for food and ordinary wares;
- permanent shops and workshops;
- covered spaces for rain-sensitive goods;
- livestock pens or a separate animal market;
- weighing and measuring points;
- storage rooms and warehouses;
- spaces for carts and pack animals;
- official notice boards or announcement points;
- guards or officers responsible for order;
- inns, eating houses, money handlers, and carriers nearby.

The exact arrangement is local. A port market differs from an inland grain market; a small village market may be little more than a customary gathering place.

### Market days and ordinary trade

A town may have designated market days, but daily retail trade can continue in permanent shops and food stalls. A fair is different from an ordinary market: it may attract distant merchants, specialist sellers, entertainers, lenders, and large temporary crowds under special privileges.

Do not assume every settlement has a weekly market, the same market day, or the same opening hours. Those details should be established for named places when needed.

### Haggling and posted prices

Bargaining is likely in many transactions, especially for livestock, bulk goods, used equipment, and specialist wares. Small everyday purchases may use customary or posted prices. A merchant may quote different terms to a trusted buyer, a stranger, a bulk purchaser, or someone paying immediately.

A buyer is not always entitled to the lowest price. Quality, urgency, reputation, credit, delivery, and the risk of the transaction shape the offer.

## 4. Guilds, craft associations, and privileges

The term guild should not be used as a universal label for every group of tradespeople. Commercial organization may take several forms:
- a formally chartered guild with recognized rights;
- a craft fraternity or association;
- a merchant company or partnership;
- a household workshop with apprentices;
- an informal network of trusted practitioners;
- a town-authorized group responsible for a particular service.

Where a guild exists, it may regulate entry to a trade, apprenticeship, standards, inspection, disputes among members, mutual aid, religious observance, or access to market privileges. Its powers depend on its charter and local authority.

Guilds are not automatically benevolent protectors of quality. They may defend standards and members while also restricting competition, favoring established families, excluding outsiders, or lobbying for privileges. A guild may be divided between wealthy masters, younger practitioners, and members with different commercial interests.

A guild does not automatically control all commerce in a town. Independent merchants, household production, rural craftspeople, foreign traders, and unchartered workshops may operate alongside formal associations, subject to local law.

### Apprenticeship

An apprenticeship is a relationship of training and service, usually governed by an agreement or custom. The apprentice learns a trade while performing useful work. The household or master may provide instruction, food, lodging, tools, or other support under agreed terms.

Apprenticeship can create opportunity, but it also creates dependence. Poor treatment, broken agreements, family pressure, and disputes over skill or completion are plausible sources of conflict. Exact ages, terms, and legal protections vary and are not established continent-wide.

## 5. Quality, measures, and honest dealing

Commercial trust depends on buyers believing that goods are what sellers claim they are and that quantity is measured fairly.

Common points of dispute include:
- watered or spoiled wine and ale;
- adulterated flour, spices, medicines, or dyes;
- damp or contaminated grain;
- underweight bundles or sacks;
- false claims about origin or quality;
- inferior metal or poorly tempered tools;
- cloth sold as a finer weave or dye than it is;
- animals misrepresented as healthy, trained, or sound;
- fish or meat sold after unsafe storage.

Towns may use recognized weights, measures, seals, inspectors, or sworn officers. The standards may differ between jurisdictions, creating problems for long-distance merchants. No single continental system of weights, measures, inspection, or product grading is established.

A credible commercial dispute asks: who set the measure, who witnessed it, who inspected the goods, what was promised, and which authority can hear the complaint?

## 6. Contracts and agreements

Commercial agreements may be spoken, written, witnessed, sealed, entered into account books, or confirmed through a combination of these methods. The form depends on the value, distance, parties, literacy, local custom, and legal risk.

A useful contract record identifies:
1. the parties and the authority of anyone acting as an agent;
2. the goods or service;
3. quantity and quality;
4. price and currency or other consideration;
5. payment timing and method;
6. delivery place and deadline;
7. who bears transport, storage, and loss risks;
8. inspection and acceptance conditions;
9. penalties, remedies, or dispute procedures;
10. witnesses, seals, or records.

Not every agreement needs all ten points. A household buying bread does not need a formal parchment. A merchant financing a cargo that crosses the inland sea probably wants more than a handshake.

### Deposits and advance payment

A buyer may pay a deposit to reserve goods or demonstrate commitment. A producer may require advance payment to purchase materials or hire labor. The agreement should establish whether the deposit is refundable and what happens if either party fails to perform.

### Credit and debt

Credit allows trade to occur before all parties have cash in hand. A merchant may extend payment terms to a known customer; a lender may finance a shipment; a noble household may borrow against expected rents or revenues.

Credit creates relationships and leverage. A lender's influence depends on the amount, collateral, alternatives, political protection, and the debtor's reputation. Debt does not automatically transfer ownership of land, a company, or a political office.

### Partnerships and shared ventures

Two or more parties may pool capital, cargo space, animals, contacts, or expertise. A partnership needs some understanding of contributions, authority, profit, loss, and responsibility. Disputes often arise when one partner believes another exceeded their authority or concealed a risk.

## 7. Transport, cargo, and risk

Transport changes the price and reliability of goods. A route that is shorter on a map may be more expensive if it is rough, dangerous, seasonal, or subject to several tolls.

### Overland cargo

Carts are useful for bulky goods where roads permit. Pack animals can travel on narrower or rougher routes but carry less. Drovers move livestock; caravans combine animals, guards, and handlers. Road conditions, bridges, ferries, weather, banditry, animal illness, and local tolls all affect cost.

### River cargo

River transport can move heavy goods more efficiently than carts, but it depends on navigable water, suitable vessels, landing places, currents, river levels, bridges, and local rights. Upstream and downstream journeys may differ greatly in time and effort.

### Maritime cargo

Ships carry large quantities, but cargo must be loaded, stowed, protected, unloaded, and moved through ports. Delays can arise from weather, harbor congestion, inspection, pilotage, repairs, crew availability, or political restrictions. A ship's arrival does not mean its cargo is immediately available in the market.

### Loss and responsibility

Cargo can be lost through storm, fire, theft, accident, spoilage, infestation, damage, seizure, or delay. Who bears the loss depends on the contract, custom, negligence, and circumstances. A carrier may be responsible for careless handling but not necessarily for every storm. A merchant may insure or spread risk through partnership, but no universal insurance system is established.

### Security

Armed escorts may deter robbery but increase cost and may create political complications. A lord's protection, a chartered safe-conduct, a port's authority, and a merchant's private guards are different forms of security. A merchant does not automatically have the right to bring armed men through every town or lordship.

## 8. Ports, customs, and harbor business

Ports are workplaces with their own rhythms and competing interests. Cargo handling may involve shipowners, captains, pilots, dock laborers, warehouse keepers, merchants, customs officials, harbor authorities, chandlers, repairers, and local guards.

A port may collect dues or fees for anchorage, cargo handling, storage, inspection, pilotage, wharf use, or other chartered services. The exact rights and charges vary. Do not invent a universal harbor tariff.

Commercial ports also depend on:
- safe anchorage and maintained access;
- pilots and local navigational knowledge;
- warehouses and dry storage;
- fresh water, food, rope, sailcloth, timber, and repair materials;
- laborers and suitable carts;
- reliable records and dispute resolution;
- protection against theft, fire, and damage.

A port can be wealthy while many dockworkers remain poor. Commercial success is not distributed evenly.

### Tallowmere

Tallowmere's established economic role is that of a regional port and River Vale trade hub. Its importance comes from river access, the Central Inland Sea, local agriculture, quarrying, timber and charcoal, fishing, shore trade, and its chartered relationship with House Kharadorn.

Tallowmere's harbor-defense review is a local charter review, not an occupation or naval mobilization. Do not infer new Kharadorn rights from the existence of the review. The exact fees, cargo inspections, harbor offices, and division of authority should follow the established Tallowmere civic and charter records.

## 9. Merchant reputation and information

Reputation is commercial capital. A merchant known to pay on time, deliver the promised quality, honor agreements, and communicate delays can obtain better terms. A merchant known for false weights or disappearing when debts fall due may find doors closing.

Information is uneven. A merchant may know that a bridge is damaged before a distant lord receives a report. A factor may know a shipment is late but not why. A ship captain may hear rumors of a port dispute that have not been verified. A town official may know the law but not the private arrangements between merchants.

News travels through letters, messengers, ships, caravans, market talk, and personal networks. Rumor can move quickly; proof often moves slowly.

Commercial intelligence may concern:
- expected harvests and prices;
- road, bridge, and river conditions;
- port closures or delays;
- reliable suppliers and dishonest agents;
- political tensions and new toll demands;
- availability of ships, animals, labor, and warehouse space;
- the creditworthiness of buyers and lenders.

No merchant network has perfect information or automatic access to private government knowledge.

## 10. Fraud, smuggling, and commercial crime

Commercial crime often hides inside ordinary transactions. Examples include:
- false weights and measures;
- counterfeit coin or forged seals;
- substitution of inferior goods;
- false cargo manifests;
- concealed ownership;
- theft by employees or carriers;
- deliberate insolvency or evasion of debt;
- bribery of inspectors;
- illegal export or import;
- smuggling to avoid tolls or restrictions;
- sale of stolen goods;
- fraudulent partnerships and forged letters.

Smuggling is not automatically organized crime. It may involve a large merchant network, a small boat crew, a corrupt official, or a household trying to avoid a burdensome toll. Its seriousness depends on what is moved, which law is broken, and who is harmed.

A lord may tolerate some evasion while prosecuting a rival. A town may defend its charter against outside merchants. A guild may call an outsider's trade unlawful while local consumers welcome the lower prices. Legal status and public opinion need not align.

## 11. Commercial influence and noble power

Merchants can influence political decisions without becoming rulers. They may:
- lend money;
- supply armies or households;
- control access to scarce goods;
- employ large numbers of workers;
- provide ships, carts, or warehouses;
- possess useful information;
- negotiate tolls and privileges;
- organize petitions or collective refusals;
- redirect trade to a competing market.

Nobles can influence merchants through charters, protection, taxation, courts, tolls, land rights, monopolies, and the threat or use of force. Neither side is all-powerful.

Vaelorian finance is a major source of commercial influence, but it does not mean every merchant owes Vaelorian money or that the house controls all credit. Merrowen's maritime strength does not make every eastern port a Merrowen possession. Kharadorn's influence over the Central Inland Sea does not erase local port charters or inland trade. Crown authority does not automatically override every established local privilege.

## 12. Portraying a commercial dispute

When a trade dispute enters play, establish the transaction before deciding who is right.

Ask:
- What was promised, and by whom?
- Was the agreement spoken, written, sealed, or witnessed?
- Who had authority to make it?
- What was delivered, and what was missing or defective?
- Who inspected or accepted the goods?
- Who paid, and what remains owed?
- Who bore the risk during transport or storage?
- Which charter, custom, guild privilege, or court applies?
- What evidence exists, and who controls it?
- What does each party want besides a legal victory?

Commercial disputes may end in payment, replacement, compromise, arbitration, litigation, public disgrace, retaliation, or a negotiated change in future terms. Not every dispute needs a villain. Honest people can disagree about quality, weather, responsibility, and what an agreement meant.

## 13. Deliberately unresolved

The following are not established as universal facts:
- one continental guild structure or standard guild charter;
- universal market days or trading hours;
- a common system of weights and measures;
- standardized market inspection and product grading;
- universal contract forms or commercial courts;
- continent-wide insurance practices;
- standard port dues, tolls, or customs tariffs;
- fixed apprenticeship terms and ages;
- universal merchant licenses;
- exact prices, wages, commissions, interest rates, or freight charges.

Create specific rules when a named market, merchant, guild, contract, or dispute requires them. A few well-defined local practices are more useful than pretending the entire continent runs on one invisible commercial code.
