# Regional Legal Customs and Dispute Practices

**Status:** GM-approved framework expansion  
**Baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Scope:** How ordinary disputes are commonly handled across established regions

This record supplements the formal legal framework. It does not create new borders, courts, jurisdictions, or universal statutes.

The same dispute can be treated differently depending on location, social status, charter, lordship, guild custom, faith, and the seriousness of the harm.

## 1. General principle

Most people do not begin with formal litigation.

For ordinary disputes, people commonly try:

1. direct settlement;
2. mediation by a respected person;
3. payment or replacement;
4. testimony by witnesses;
5. local authority;
6. formal court action when necessary.

The exact sequence varies.

A wealthy or politically connected person may bypass steps available to ordinary people.

A person with no reputation or connections may have difficulty getting a dispute heard.

## 2. Property disputes

Common disputes involve:

- damaged fences;
- livestock trespass;
- stolen tools;
- unpaid debts;
- disputed ownership;
- damaged cargo;
- boundary markers;
- timber use;
- water access;
- inheritance.

The first question is often not "What does the law say?"

It is:

**"Who can prove what happened?"**

Witnesses, written records, physical evidence, reputation, and established custom all matter.

## 3. Edarth practice

Edarth communities place substantial weight on:

- personal testimony;
- oaths;
- family reputation;
- customary land use;
- witness accounts;
- obligations between lord and tenant.

A dispute may be settled through compensation or a binding agreement rather than punishment.

Publicly breaking a sworn settlement can damage a person's reputation severely.

A land dispute may become politically important if it involves an old custom or noble obligation.

## 4. Veyr practice

Northern communities favor practical resolution.

Questions include:

- What happened?
- Who is responsible?
- What damage resulted?
- How can it be repaired?
- Will the solution prevent recurrence?

During dangerous conditions, immediate survival can take precedence over ordinary procedural concerns.

A person who steals fuel during a severe winter may be judged differently from someone who steals luxury goods.

This does not make theft legal.

It affects how communities understand necessity and punishment.

## 5. Vaelorian practice

Vaelorian communities place significant value on:

- records;
- precedent;
- contracts;
- genealogy;
- witnesses;
- carefully defined obligations.

Disputes may take longer because parties want the exact terms established.

A Vaelorian may regard a vague promise as dangerously incomplete.

A human outsider may consider the same demand excessive.

## 6. Kharadorn practice

Dwarven disputes commonly emphasize:

- clan responsibility;
- testimony;
- workmanship;
- precedent;
- compensation;
- oath;
- repair.

A craft dispute may be judged partly by whether the work met the agreed standard.

A person who knowingly delivered defective work may damage both personal and clan reputation.

Not every dispute requires punishment.

Restoring what was damaged can be considered a stronger resolution.

## 7. Merrowen practice

Maritime disputes often focus on:

- cargo;
- contracts;
- crew obligations;
- pilotage;
- ship ownership;
- wages;
- damage caused by negligence;
- harbor customs.

Written records are particularly valuable.

A ship's log, cargo record, witness statement, or merchant contract may settle an argument that would otherwise become a prolonged dispute.

Harbor communities also depend heavily on reputation because people repeatedly do business with one another.

## 8. Crownlands practice

Central communities make comparatively frequent use of:

- written contracts;
- royal or local officials;
- chartered privileges;
- recognized procedures;
- clerks and witnesses.

This does not mean the central regions have perfect justice.

It means administration is more visible.

A person may complain about bureaucracy while deliberately seeking a written ruling because the ruling can be enforced later.

## 9. Southern practice

The Southern Reaches vary too widely for one system.

A small principality may rely heavily on noble authority.

A city may rely on merchant institutions.

An island community may rely heavily on maritime custom.

A caravan settlement may prioritize water rights and travel agreements.

Local legitimacy matters more than uniformity.

## 10. Nobles and commoners

Formal equality before law should not be assumed.

A noble possesses:

- wealth;
- witnesses;
- servants;
- political relationships;
- access to officials;
- family reputation.

An ordinary laborer may possess none of these.

This does not mean every noble wins every dispute.

It means the social cost of making an accusation can be very different depending on who is involved.

## 11. Guild disputes

Where guild structures exist, craftsmen may first seek internal resolution.

Typical questions include:

- Was the work properly performed?
- Was the agreed price honored?
- Was guild custom violated?
- Did another craftsman damage the reputation of the trade?
- Was an apprentice treated according to established expectations?

Guilds do not automatically possess criminal jurisdiction.

Their influence depends on region and charter.

## 12. Merchant disputes

Merchants value reliable settlement because prolonged conflict costs money.

Common resolutions include:

- replacement;
- partial refund;
- compensation;
- revised contract;
- third-party mediation;
- temporary refusal of credit.

A merchant who repeatedly cheats customers may become effectively isolated from useful trade even without a dramatic court case.

## 13. Debt

Debt is socially significant.

A person who cannot pay may attempt:

- renegotiation;
- additional work;
- selling property;
- finding a guarantor;
- obtaining help from family;
- seeking merchant mediation.

Failure to pay does not automatically make someone a criminal.

Fraud, theft, deliberate evasion, and breach of specific obligations can create different consequences.

## 14. Violence

People distinguish between:

- accidental injury;
- self-defense;
- fighting;
- assault;
- armed robbery;
- deliberate killing.

The circumstances matter.

A person who starts a tavern fight may face consequences that differ from a person who kills an attacker while defending a family member.

Local authorities determine the legal outcome according to applicable law and custom.

## 15. Oaths

Oaths are culturally important but not magical guarantees.

An oath may be religious, social, legal, or personal.

Breaking one can result in:

- loss of trust;
- family pressure;
- political damage;
- legal consequences;
- religious condemnation.

There is no assumption that the gods automatically punish every broken oath with supernatural force.

## 16. Evidence

Useful evidence includes:

- witnesses;
- written documents;
- physical damage;
- tools;
- cargo records;
- seals;
- signatures or marks;
- testimony from respected specialists;
- consistent timelines.

Magic can complicate evidence because magic exists, but magical investigation is not routine.

Most disputes are settled without spells.

## 17. False accusations

False accusations can themselves create harm.

A person accused of theft may lose customers.

A servant accused of disloyalty may lose employment.

A ratfolk accused of criminal activity may face prejudice before any evidence is considered.

A noble accused publicly of treason may respond politically even if the accusation is false.

The social consequences may begin before a formal investigation ends.

## 18. Missing persons

A missing person creates different responses depending on status and location.

A family may:

- search nearby;
- ask neighbors;
- question travelers;
- notify local authorities;
- ask religious institutions for assistance;
- seek professional trackers;
- circulate descriptions.

A wealthy family can mobilize more resources.

A poor family may rely on neighbors and informal networks.

A person who disappears while traveling may leave very little evidence.

## 19. Local authority and the Watch

The Watch is not a universal solution.

Officers must decide:

- whether the matter falls within their authority;
- whether immediate danger exists;
- whether evidence can be gathered;
- whether another institution has jurisdiction;
- whether the dispute is primarily civil;
- whether the parties can be separated safely.

A Watch officer may tell two merchants to settle a minor contract dispute rather than treating the matter as a criminal investigation.

## 20. Religious mediation

Temples may provide:

- witnesses;
- records;
- mediation;
- charity;
- sanctuary according to local custom or law;
- funerary records;
- moral guidance.

Religious institutions do not automatically possess secular jurisdiction.

A priest can help two families reconcile without possessing legal authority to decide the dispute.

## 21. Reputation as informal law

In small communities, reputation can matter as much as formal procedure.

A person who:

- pays fairly;
- keeps agreements;
- helps neighbors;
- repairs damage;
- tells the truth when inconvenient;

may receive considerable informal trust.

A person who:

- cheats;
- lies;
- threatens witnesses;
- refuses reasonable settlement;

may find doors closing even when they have not been convicted of anything.

## 22. GM use

When a PC becomes involved in a dispute, ask:

1. What actually happened?
2. What does each party claim?
3. What evidence exists?
4. Who has social power?
5. What local custom applies?
6. What would a practical settlement look like?
7. Who benefits from escalation?
8. What will the community remember afterward?

Do not turn every dispute into a courtroom scene.

Often the most interesting outcome is the negotiation before formal judgment.

## 23. Core principle

The law is not only the written rule.

It is also:

- who knows the rule;
- who can enforce it;
- who can afford to invoke it;
- what local custom expects;
- what people believe is fair;
- what happens when those things disagree.

That tension is part of ordinary life.
