# Brackenholt: Woodland Work, Charcoal, and Customary Rights

**Status:** Established settlement framework with practical GM portrayal guidance  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY  
**Snapshot:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Related records:** [Surrounding Settlements and Landholders](Regional_Settlements_and_Landholders.md), [Tallowmere Regional Ecology](../../creatures/Tallowmere_Regional_Ecology.md), [Deep Bracken Common Fauna](../../creatures/Deep_Bracken_Common_Fauna.md), [Household Comfort and Winter Preparation](Household_Comfort_Fuel_and_Winter_Preparation.md)

This dossier develops Brackenholt as a working settlement rather than a label on the regional map. It explains how woodland labor, charcoal production, customary access, and the trade with Tallowmere can shape daily life and local disputes.

The settlement's population, location, broad economy, authority, and principal concerns are established in the regional settlement record. The work practices below are practical portrayal guidance. They do not establish exact production methods, woodland boundaries, prices, quotas, or a complete local code of rights.

## 1. Brackenholt at a glance

Brackenholt is a woodland and charcoal settlement of approximately 300–400 people, about 14 miles west of Tallowmere beyond the lower river farms. It lies within the Denvor lordship, but customary woodland rights are shared among several households.

It is not a compact village built around a single street. Homes and working spaces are scattered among clearings and woodland edges. Some households live close to a work site; others are nearer a track, a loading place, or the Burners' Green. The settlement's shape follows access to woodland, water, usable ground, and routes for moving heavy loads.

Brackenholt supplies:
- charcoal;
- firewood;
- poles and rough timber;
- coppiced wood and other woodland products.

Some residents also hunt, trap, gather, cart goods, or take seasonal work in Tallowmere. Not every household burns charcoal, and not every worker owns the woodland or the equipment used in production.

### The Burners' Green

The Burners' Green is a broad clearing used for seasonal charcoal stacking, exchange, and local gatherings. It is an established meeting place, but not a formal market with a known schedule or a permanent civic hall.

It is a natural place for work crews to compare loads, arrange carts, discuss buyers, settle small disagreements, and hear what is happening elsewhere in the woodland. A gathering there may be busy without being a festival or official assembly.

## 2. Authority and customary access

House Denvor holds lordship authority. Several households possess customary woodland rights, and those rights are not identical or unlimited.

A Denvor forester records cutting rights and reports serious disputes. There is no single village reeve who commands all the burners. Household elders and work crews settle many ordinary matters themselves, while serious disputes may require the forester or the lordship's officers.

The Brackenwood Charcoal Fellowship represents working families but does not own the woodland as a unified body. It cannot grant rights that belong to the lordship or to individual customary holders. Its leverage comes from the practical importance of fuel supply and the ability of its members to coordinate their labor and sales.

Do not assume that a person may cut any tree simply because it stands near their home. Rights may differ between:
- cutting coppice within an assigned or customary area;
- taking poles or brush;
- collecting fallen wood;
- gathering plants, nuts, or fungi;
- hunting or trapping game;
- cutting mature timber or protected standards;
- carrying goods through a private or restricted working area.

These categories are useful for adjudication, not a complete established Brackenholt legal code. The exact rights attached to particular households, plots, and tracks remain unresolved.

### The forester's role

The Denvor forester is an officer who records cutting rights and reports serious disputes. The forester is not automatically a full-time forest guard, surveyor, judge, or expert on every household custom.

A forester may know which plots are assigned, which cutting has been authorized, and where a complaint has been made. They may not know every informal arrangement between neighboring households or every tree taken from an outer track.

## 3. The woodland is worked, not empty

The Brackenwood is managed woodland. Coppice, mature stands, clearings, charcoal burns, gathering areas, and older unmanaged pockets coexist. Human work changes the habitat.

Coppicing involves cutting selected trees near the base so that new shoots grow from the remaining stool. Repeated cutting creates a patchwork of regrowth, brush, open ground, and mature trees retained for timber or other purposes. The precise local rotation, species, plot boundaries, and cutting calendar are not established.

Woodland work can include:
- selecting and cutting coppice;
- trimming poles and small timber;
- clearing brush from working paths;
- gathering suitable deadwood;
- preparing wood for fuel or charcoal;
- repairing tracks and loading places;
- protecting young growth and retained trees;
- moving finished goods toward the settlement or Tallowmere.

A worked clearing is not necessarily abandoned. It may be resting between cuts, reserved for regrowth, used for grazing, or awaiting another stage of work.

### Brackenwood and Deep Bracken are not interchangeable

The Brackenwood is the managed woodland associated with Brackenholt and Tallowmere's settled hinterland. The Deep Bracken is the older, less-managed woodland beyond Brackenholt and the Brackenwood. Its canopy is denser, its paths less reliable, and Denvor customary rights weaken toward the outer forest. The exact boundary is not surveyed.

The five animals explicitly established as common in the Deep Bracken are deer, wild boar, foxes, badgers, and wolves. That does not establish the same common occurrence in the managed Brackenwood. Other wildlife records distinguish candidates from confirmed occurrences.

Do not turn a charcoal camp, coppice clearing, or woodland path into a monster lair merely because it lies near the forest. The ordinary dangers of fire, tools, weather, falling timber, difficult ground, and uncertain rights are sufficient to make woodland work consequential.

## 4. Charcoal production as work

Charcoal is made by heating wood with restricted access to air so that much of the wood's volatile material is driven off while a carbon-rich fuel remains. Traditional methods vary. A covered earth clamp or mound is one plausible high-medieval method, but the exact local design used at Brackenholt is not established.

A charcoal-burning operation may involve several stages:

1. **Wood selection:** suitable wood is cut and sorted. Different sizes and conditions may be used for different purposes.
2. **Seasoning and preparation:** wood may be dried or left to lose some moisture before burning. The exact period depends on the wood and weather.
3. **Stacking:** the wood is arranged into a controlled pile or clamp, with channels or a central space to help manage the burn.
4. **Covering:** brush, leaves, turf, earth, or other material restricts the air entering the stack.
5. **Burning and watching:** the burner manages vents and smoke, judging the process by its behavior. Too much air can consume the wood; too little can prevent proper carbonization.
6. **Cooling:** the finished charcoal must cool sufficiently before it is disturbed or exposed to air. Poor handling can cause flare-ups or destroy part of the batch.
7. **Sorting and transport:** charcoal is separated from unburned wood, ash, and unusable material, then loaded for sale or storage.

This sequence is a general portrayal model, not a fixed Brackenholt production specification. Do not assign a standard clamp size, burn duration, yield, number of active burns, or output per household.

Charcoal burning is skilled work. A burner must understand the material, weather, airflow, smoke, and the behavior of a particular stack. A mistake can waste days of labor and valuable wood. A good burner is not simply someone who lights a pile and walks away.

### Fire, smoke, and danger

Charcoal production creates heat, smoke, and dangerous fumes. Workers may suffer burns, smoke irritation, exhaustion, and injury while cutting or moving wood. A poorly watched burn can spread into surrounding vegetation or damage nearby property.

A charcoal camp needs working space, water or other means to manage fire, safe separation from vulnerable structures, and people who understand the burn. These are practical needs, not proof that Brackenholt has a formal fire service or standardized safety code.

A sudden change in wind or rain can ruin work or create danger. A worker who says a burn is lost may be protecting a week's labor, not exaggerating for effect.

## 5. Different products, different buyers

Charcoal, firewood, poles, and rough timber are related products but not interchangeable.

- **Charcoal** is useful where a hotter, more controllable fuel is needed, including smithing and other craft work.
- **Firewood** serves household heating and cooking where suitable hearths or stoves are available.
- **Poles** are useful for fences, supports, simple structures, and other practical work.
- **Rough timber** serves builders, repairers, carters, and other users who need material rather than fuel.
- **Coppiced wood and brush** may have value as fuel, bindings, small craft material, or other household resources depending on quality and local practice.

Brackenholt's goods supply Tallowmere's smiths, kitchens, builders, and ship repairers. The relationship is not a single contract between the settlement and the city. Individual households, work crews, factors, merchants, and buyers may have their own arrangements.

Deren Holt is an established charcoal factor active in the trade. His exact home and woodland contracts remain unspecified. He is one commercial intermediary, not the sole buyer or owner of Brackenholt's production.

A buyer who controls access to carts, storage, credit, or a reliable market can exert pressure even without owning the woodland. Conversely, a group of burners who coordinate sales can refuse terms that do not cover their labor and transport.

## 6. The Brackenwood Charcoal Fellowship

The Brackenwood Charcoal Fellowship is an established association of woodland laborers and charcoal producers based at Brackenholt and in the surrounding managed woodland.

Mara Bellwood is its senior charcoal burner and elected representative for several working families. She is blunt, observant, locally influential, and politically inexperienced. She prefers organizing workers before appealing to nobles.

The Fellowship's established interests are:
- preserving customary access to woodland resources;
- maintaining predictable cutting limits;
- securing fair charcoal prices;
- preventing outside buyers from monopolizing local production.

An internal difference already exists. Established households favor customary rights. Some younger burners favor formal contracts with Tallowmere merchants. Neither position is automatically foolish: customary arrangements can protect local control, while written contracts may offer predictable buyers and payment. Contracts can also bind households to terms they later regret.

The Fellowship's leverage is practical rather than sovereign. If its members coordinate, they can affect fuel supply to smiths, kitchens, kilns, and workshops. They cannot lawfully declare the woodland independent, erase Denvor authority, or grant themselves rights belonging to individual households.

## 7. Daily life and seasonal pressures

Brackenholt's work is not limited to the moment a charcoal stack is burning. Cutting, drying, carrying, tool maintenance, repairing carts, preparing loads, and negotiating sales all take time.

A household may combine several sources of income:
- charcoal burning;
- firewood or pole cutting within its rights;
- seasonal carting;
- hunting or trapping where permitted;
- gathering;
- repairs or craft work;
- paid work in Tallowmere.

This makes households vulnerable to several kinds of disruption. Rain may delay burning or make tracks difficult. A broken cart may strand a load. A disagreement over cutting rights may halt work in a particular plot. A poor price may make a long haul unprofitable. A worker injured by a falling tree may lose both wages and the ability to complete a seasonal task.

Harvestfall is a busy period across the region. It does not mean every woodland household is free to gather or trade at leisure. Work crews may be balancing city demand, household stores, repairs, and the next season's fuel.

Exact local seasonal schedules, work hours, wages, prices, and production quantities remain unresolved. Use the needs of the particular household and job rather than assigning a universal Brackenholt work calendar.

## 8. What different people know

| Person | Likely practical knowledge | Limits |
|---|---|---|
| Charcoal burner | Wood condition, burn behavior, smoke, work paths, buyers, cutting customs | Does not automatically know every household's rights or distant politics |
| Coppice worker | Regrowth, cutting boundaries, poles, mature standards, seasonal work | May not know commercial contracts or the forester's full record |
| Carter | Track conditions, loads, delivery arrangements, buyers, cart repairs | Does not know every woodland boundary or production method |
| Denvor forester | Recorded cutting rights, reported disputes, authorized work | Does not automatically know every informal custom or unreported taking |
| Mara Bellwood | Fellowship concerns, worker coordination, customary access, buyer pressure | Not a spokesman for every household or the entire Denvor lordship |
| Deren Holt | Charcoal buying and commercial arrangements in which he is involved | Not the sole buyer and not an authority over woodland rights |
| Tallowmere smith | Fuel quality, reliability, delivery, and price | May know little about how the charcoal was produced |

A city merchant may understand prices and contracts but know little about the labor that makes charcoal. A burner may understand woodland conditions but have limited interest in court politics. Knowledge follows work and relationships.

## 9. Using Brackenholt in play

Brackenholt can support scenes about labor, customary rights, local bargaining, resource use, and the costs of supplying a city. It does not need to be in crisis when the PCs arrive.

Useful ordinary situations include:
- a work crew sorting a load before a cart leaves for Tallowmere;
- a burner checking a stack after a change in weather;
- a disagreement over whether a tree was within a household's cutting area;
- a buyer pressing for a lower price while the Fellowship argues that transport and labor costs have risen;
- an older household resisting a written contract favored by younger workers;
- a forester comparing a complaint with the rights currently recorded;
- workers at the Burners' Green exchanging news while waiting for a load to be weighed.

Use Mara Bellwood, Deren Holt, and other already established figures only where their documented roles make sense. Background workers do not need names unless play makes them important.

Do not make every dispute a conspiracy or every resource shortage a political plot. A household can simply need a fair price, a sound cart, a dry week, or the right to cut the wood it has always been allowed to use.

## 10. Deliberately unresolved

The following details remain open:
- exact boundaries and maps of Brackenholt's customary woodland rights;
- which households hold which cutting, gathering, grazing, hunting, and trapping rights;
- the identity and personality of the Denvor forester;
- exact coppice species, rotation lengths, and protected-tree rules;
- the local charcoal clamp design, burn duration, output, and grading standards;
- the number of active charcoal burns and their seasonal distribution;
- exact wages, prices, cartage costs, contracts, and buyer shares;
- the Fellowship's membership, internal rules, and formal decision procedure;
- Deren Holt's home, suppliers, and individual contracts;
- the exact transition point between managed Brackenwood and less-managed Deep Bracken.

Do not resolve these questions simply to make Brackenholt look complete. The settlement already has enough structure to support meaningful play; add details when the PCs visit, negotiate, investigate, or depend on its people.
