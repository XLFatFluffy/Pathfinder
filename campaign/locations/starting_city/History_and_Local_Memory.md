# Tallowmere: History, Identity, and Local Memory

**Status:** Established local history framework; exact dates and several older details intentionally remain uncertain.  
**Last updated:** 2026-11-04

This record gives Tallowmere a history that predates the player characters and the current Denvor household. It establishes broad historical developments without pretending that every date, name, or surviving account is known. Where sources disagree, that disagreement is part of the city's history rather than an invitation to invent a single answer.

## 1. The City's Character

Tallowmere is a working port that became important by being useful. It did not begin as a royal foundation, a great fortress, or the seat of a powerful dynasty. Its advantages were practical: a sheltered harbor, a navigable river, a crossing point, and access to routes connecting the inland sea with productive land to the west and north.

Its civic identity is shaped by four recurring ideas:

- **The water brings opportunity.** Ships, barges, visitors, money, news, and danger arrive by water.
- **The city survives through maintenance.** Quays, walls, roads, bridges, warehouses, channels, and stores require constant labor and investment.
- **No one prospers alone.** The port depends on inland farmers, river workers, ship crews, craftspeople, merchants, and surrounding landholders.
- **Authority is negotiated in practice.** The Denvors rule, Kharadorn holds strategic rights, and commercial and neighborhood interests can influence whether decisions succeed.

Tallowmere is proud of its harbor and wary of anyone who treats it as a possession rather than a community.

## 2. The Name

**Tallowmere** is the established name. Its precise linguistic origin is not settled.

Three explanations are commonly repeated:
- An old local word for the pale, waxy sheen sometimes seen on sheltered water after certain seasonal blooms or frosts.
- A reference to the pale light of winter fog over the harbor, said to resemble rendered tallow.
- A corruption of an older river or settlement name that no longer survives in reliable records.

None is proven. Learned clerks may favor one explanation; dockside storytellers another. The city does not have a universally accepted older name.

The heron and silver water in House Denvor's arms are often associated with the city's identity, but the Denvor device is a family heraldic emblem, not an official city coat of arms.

## 3. Before the Walls

The earliest settlement was a small river landing and fishing community near the sheltered harbor. The river mouth provided access to inland produce and timber, while the natural anchorage offered protection to small vessels using the inland sea.

A crossing and market developed where travelers, boatmen, fishers, and farmers could exchange goods. The settlement likely grew in stages rather than according to a single founding plan. Early homes and workshops clustered around the landing, the river route, and the most reliable ground above seasonal flooding.

The first inhabitants were not a single people or one noble household. The settlement attracted families and traders from nearby river communities and the inland-sea shore. Its early importance was regional and practical.

**What is established:** Tallowmere grew from a useful river-and-harbor settlement rather than being founded as a royal capital.

**What remains uncertain:** The exact founding date, original settlement name, earliest ruling authority, and identity of the first major landholder are not established.

## 4. The Market and the First Defenses

As trade increased, the landing became a permanent market town. A regular market, storage buildings, workshops, and a more dependable crossing made it useful to merchants moving goods between the inland sea and the western uplands.

The town's first defenses were likely modest: timber barriers, controlled approaches, raised stores, and a small fortified residence or watch position. These protections were intended to deter raiders and opportunistic attacks, not withstand a great army.

The Old Stone Bridge represents the later permanence of this crossing system. Its present structure has been repaired and altered over time; it should not be assumed that every surviving stone belongs to the first bridge.

The earliest market customs helped establish a civic preference that remains recognizable: weights should be known, debts recorded, and access to the market negotiated openly enough that merchants continue to use it. This is an inherited civic ideal, not proof that the city has always been fair.

## 5. The Growth of the Port

Tallowmere's growth accelerated as inland-sea trade became more regular. The harbor developed from a natural anchorage into a managed port with quays, warehouses, repair yards, and designated cargo areas.

Old Quays grew around the original waterfront. Its lanes remain irregular because buildings, yards, and workshops expanded along older property lines. The district's crowded character is a physical record of growth without a single master plan.

As cargo volumes increased, the city extended its working waterfront. New Quays were built and expanded to handle larger shipments, bulk goods, and newer commercial concerns. Its broader roads and more regular yards reflect deliberate later construction.

The port's prosperity brought both wealth and vulnerability. A fire, blocked river, damaged bridge, unsafe harbor, or interruption to inland supply could affect the whole city. Tallowmere learned to treat storage, fire prevention, water access, and repair work as matters of civic survival.

## 6. The Great Rebellion and the Denvor Charter

During the Great Rebellion against the dragon-riding dynasty, Tallowmere's harbor and river connections made it strategically useful. The city was not one of the great seats of rebellion and did not determine the war's outcome. Its importance lay in ships, provisions, transport, repairs, and access to the central inland sea.

The precise campaigns fought near Tallowmere, if any, remain unestablished. The city should not be assigned a famous siege or decisive battle without further evidence.

After the rebellion, House Denvor's hereditary authority was confirmed under a charter recognized by House Kharadorn. The settlement of authority gave the Denvors responsibility for local government and the surrounding lordship while reserving strategic rights and obligations for Kharadorn.

The charter's practical obligations include:
- Annual tribute.
- Maintenance of harbor defenses.
- Provision of ships, supplies, or transport when lawfully called upon.
- Recognition of Kharadorn's strategic interests in the harbor and inland-sea defense.

The charter gave Tallowmere a durable local ruling house while tying its harbor to the wider balance of power created by the rebellion.

**Important distinction:** The Denvors' charter is not proof that House Denvor founded Tallowmere or ruled it from its earliest days. Their hereditary lordship is established in the post-rebellion political order.

The exact year of the charter, the identity of the Denvor who received or confirmed it, and the negotiations that produced it remain open.

## 7. Greywatch

Greywatch is the ancestral seat of House Denvor and stands on the high ground overlooking the harbor. Its present form reflects several periods of construction and repair.

The oldest defensible core predates some of the newer waterfront works, but the precise age of its oldest masonry is uncertain. Later Denvors expanded residential quarters, stores, defensive positions, and the garrison yard as the city's importance grew.

Greywatch serves several purposes:
- Noble residence and household administration.
- A visible statement of Denvor authority.
- A defensible position overlooking the harbor and approaches.
- Storage and command space for the lordship's local forces.

It is not a fortress capable of resisting a Great House army indefinitely. Its strength is local defense, deterrence, and command of the city during ordinary emergencies.

The Denvor heron motif appears in stonework and public decoration around the rise. Some carvings may be older than the current household; others are later commemorations. Individual carvings should not automatically be treated as coded records or hidden messages.

## 8. The Walls, Gates, and Civic Works

Tallowmere's walls were not built in one continuous project. Older sections enclose the historic settlement; later repairs and extensions incorporated expanding neighborhoods and the newer waterfront.

The wall line reflects successive needs:
- Protect the market and permanent settlement.
- Control the principal land approaches.
- Guard the harbor and cargo yards.
- Provide defensible positions during raids or local conflict.

The gates have been repaired, widened, narrowed, or rebuilt as traffic patterns changed. The current gate names and street approaches are recorded in the city's layout and street geography documents.

The Old Stone Bridge, Weigh House, Old Cistern, Reeve's Hall, Harbor Office, and Denvor Granary are civic structures whose present functions developed over time. Their current buildings need not be as old as the institutions they house.

The city has a long record of practical works rather than grand monuments: repairing the river banks, clearing channels, replacing quay timbers, improving fire breaks, maintaining wells and cisterns, and keeping roads passable.

## 9. The Underways and Ratfolk History

The Underways are not one ancient underground city built by a single people. They are a connected but incomplete patchwork of cellars, older foundations, disused service passages, water channels, natural cavities, storage spaces, and routes altered by generations of use.

Ratfolk families and communities have made homes and livelihoods in parts of this subterranean environment over a long period. Some routes and chambers predate living memory; others were adapted or created by residents responding to changes above ground.

The history of the Underways is not simply a story of hiding. Ratfolk have maintained water chambers, repaired foundations, moved goods, worked crafts, guided trusted travelers, and built neighborhood relationships. Surface authorities have relied on some of this labor while often failing to recognize it publicly.

Periods of prejudice, exclusion, and violence have pushed some families deeper underground or made them cautious about surface contact. Other ratfolk have maintained businesses and relationships above ground. The community's history contains different experiences and opinions; no single account speaks for all residents.

**Still unresolved:** The earliest ratfolk settlement beneath Tallowmere, the names of older neighborhoods, major migrations, and the extent of connections to the wider subterranean network.

## 10. Fires, Floods, and Other Hard Years

Tallowmere's history includes recurring hazards rather than one defining catastrophe.

- **Harbor fires:** Dense waterfront buildings, stored cargo, pitch, oil, rope, and timber make fire a recurring danger. Some fires have been accidental; the cause of the current recent incidents remains unknown.
- **River floods:** Seasonal high water can damage banks, mills, roads, lower stores, and bridges.
- **Storms and wrecks:** Vessels are lost on the inland sea. Salvage, missing crews, and disputed cargo claims are part of port life.
- **Bad harvests:** The city depends on the Northfields and wider hinterland. Shortages and price increases have occurred when harvests or transport fail.
- **Epidemic illness:** Crowded housing and visiting crews create risks. Specific historical outbreaks, dates, and mortality figures are not yet established.
- **Raids and local violence:** The walls and watch developed in response to real insecurity, but no particular famous sack or siege is established.

These recurring pressures shaped civic habits: maintain stores, keep fire access open, repair the river works, preserve harbor procedures, and avoid relying on one route or supplier.

## 11. Tallowmere's Place in the Wider World

Tallowmere is neither a Great House capital nor a remote provincial village. It is a useful regional port whose influence comes from traffic and reliability.

Its external relationships are layered:
- **House Kharadorn:** The city lies within Kharadorn's strategic sphere through the Denvor charter. Kharadorn's attention is strongest when harbor defense, tribute, shipping, or regional security is involved.
- **House Denvor:** The local ruling house depends on preserving its charter, maintaining the city, and balancing the needs of its people with obligations to Kharadorn.
- **The inland sea:** Trade connects Tallowmere to other ports, islands, noble territories, and independent communities. Its fortunes depend on the routes remaining useful and safe.
- **The hinterland:** Grain, timber, stone, livestock, charcoal, and labor come from surrounding settlements. The city cannot prosper if the countryside is neglected or hostile.
- **The wider realm:** Royal policy, Great House rivalries, wars, succession disputes, and changes in trade can affect Tallowmere even when no royal official is present.

Tallowmere's citizens tend to judge distant politics by what arrives at the harbor: new fees, changed flags, unfamiliar soldiers, delayed ships, unusual cargoes, and the price of necessities.

## 12. Local Memory and Civic Traditions

The city has no single official version of its past. Different groups remember different things.

- **Denvor household histories** emphasize lawful authority, defense, stewardship, and the charter.
- **Merchant families** remember market privileges, reliable measures, profitable voyages, fires, and debts.
- **River communities** remember floods, toll disputes, bridge repairs, and years when inland trade kept the city fed.
- **Dockworkers and sailors** remember wrecks, dangerous cargoes, lost crews, and the people who helped after disasters.
- **Ratfolk families** preserve neighborhood histories and memories of exclusion, mutual aid, hidden routes, and agreements with particular surface households.
- **Religious institutions** maintain burial records, memorials, charitable accounts, and stories of local devotion.

No annual festival, civic holiday, or formal founding day is established yet. The city may have local customs and commemorations, but their dates and forms should be developed deliberately rather than improvised as settled canon.

## 13. Historical Sites and Discoverable Layers

The following locations offer visible traces of the city's past. Their existence does not imply a hidden treasure, secret cult, or mandatory adventure.

- **Old Quays:** Irregular lanes, reused stonework, sealed doorways, old warehouse foundations, and buildings altered over generations.
- **Greywatch:** Older masonry incorporated into later defenses, family memorials, repaired walls, and household records.
- **Old Stone Bridge:** Repaired foundations and stonework from more than one construction period.
- **Old Cistern:** A public water structure with maintenance history and possible disused connections; it is not a map of the Underways.
- **Rivergate:** Former mill channels, altered riverbanks, old loading steps, and abandoned work spaces.
- **The Underways:** Routes and chambers with different ages, uses, and ownership histories. Access and knowledge are local and incomplete.
- **Harbor edge:** Reclaimed ground, buried mooring points, old quay lines, and foundations left by waterfront expansion.

Any specific hidden chamber, ruin, artifact, or secret historical event should be designed as a separate GM-facing discovery with a clear provenance and reason it survived.

## 14. Historical Questions Deliberately Left Open

- Exact founding date and the settlement's earliest name.
- The earliest known local authority before House Denvor.
- The original date and builder of the first bridge and harbor works.
- The date and full terms of the Denvor charter.
- The identity and actions of the Denvor who secured hereditary lordship.
- Whether Tallowmere saw a significant military action during the Great Rebellion.
- Specific historical fires, floods, epidemics, and raids.
- The oldest surviving structures in Greywatch and the walls.
- The earliest ratfolk neighborhoods and their relationship to older subterranean works.
- The origin and date of any civic customs, commemorations, and festivals.

Do not fill these gaps merely to make the timeline look complete. Uncertainty is appropriate where surviving records are incomplete or competing accounts have not been reconciled.
