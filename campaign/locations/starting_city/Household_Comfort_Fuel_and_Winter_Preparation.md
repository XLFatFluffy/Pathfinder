# Household Comfort, Fuel, Clothing, and Winter Preparation in Tallowmere

**Status:** ESTABLISHED SUPPLY CONTEXT / PRACTICAL PORTRAYAL GUIDANCE  
**Snapshot:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY  
**Scope:** Tallowmere and the immediate hinterland  
**Related records:** [Everyday Life and Material Culture](../../world/Everyday_Life_and_Material_Culture.md); [Agriculture, Food Supply, and Seasonal Work](../../world/Agriculture_Food_and_Seasonal_Work.md); [Foodways of Tallowmere and Its Hinterland](../../world/Foodways_of_Tallowmere_and_the_Hinterland.md); [Workforce and Daily Life](Workforce_and_Daily_Life.md)

## 1. The household is a working system

A household does not simply buy food and wait for the next adventure. It needs fuel, light, clean clothing, repaired tools, dry bedding, roof maintenance, and enough stored supplies to get through periods when work or transport is interrupted.

These tasks consume money and time. They also create relationships: a household buys fuel from a carter, shares a wash place, borrows a ladder, asks a neighbor to mend a coat, or owes a favor to the person who can repair a roof before rain.

This reference uses established local supply facts and practical high-medieval household patterns. It does not create new shops, fixed prices, guild privileges, or citywide service contracts.

## 2. Fuel: firewood and charcoal

Brackenholt is the established woodland and charcoal settlement west of Tallowmere. It supplies firewood, charcoal, poles, and rough timber. Fuel therefore moves from managed woodland and scattered clearings into the city through carts and local trade.

Firewood and charcoal are not interchangeable in every task:
- **Firewood** is a common household heating and cooking fuel where a suitable hearth or stove exists. It is bulky, must be kept reasonably dry, and takes storage space.
- **Charcoal** burns hotter and more consistently for many craft and workshop uses. It is valuable to smiths and other trades that need controlled heat, but it also serves other customers.
- **Poles and rough timber** are materials rather than fuel, though offcuts and damaged pieces may be reused where appropriate.

The exact retail price, delivery arrangement, household fuel allowance, and seasonal stock of Brackenholt goods are not established. Do not assign a standard monthly fuel bill without a specific household and circumstances.

Fuel supply is exposed to weather and transport. Wet woodland work, poor cart roads, labor shortages, or heavy demand can affect deliveries. The campaign has not established a current fuel shortage.

## 3. Heating and the cold season

A household's ability to stay warm depends on its building, fuel, clothing, bedding, and how many people share the space.

A modest dwelling may concentrate activity around one hearth or brazier. Rooms far from the heat source remain cold. Drafts around doors, shutters, roof joints, and poorly fitted windows matter. A wealthier home may have several fireplaces, better shutters, more bedding, and servants to keep fires tended, but it still needs fuel and maintenance.

Practical winter preparation includes:
- checking roofs, shutters, doors, and chimney structures before the worst weather;
- repairing gaps that let wind and rain into sleeping rooms;
- drying and storing firewood under cover;
- checking hearths, flues, and braziers for damage or dangerous buildup;
- airing bedding when weather permits;
- storing extra blankets and suitable clothing;
- protecting food, water, and animal feed from freezing or damp;
- arranging care for elderly, ill, very young, and mobility-impaired household members.

These are common-sense household preparations, not a universal civic inspection schedule. The exact construction and prevalence of chimneys, stoves, and dedicated kitchen hearths vary by property and remain unrecorded.

## 4. Light after sunset

Daylight governs much ordinary work. After dark, households rely on hearth light, candles, lamps, lanterns, and the light of public or commercial spaces where available.

The city's established shop inventories include hooded lanterns and oil. The exact local candle industry, lamp oil supply chain, and price of ordinary household lighting are not fully documented.

Lighting has a cost:
- A lamp needs fuel and maintenance.
- A candle is consumed as it burns.
- A lantern protects a flame from wind but requires a suitable source of light and careful handling.
- Bright, sustained lighting is more expensive than a single small flame.
- Poor households may finish tasks before dark, share light, or use the hearth rather than keep several lamps burning.

A shop, inn, temple, or wealthy household may remain lit later than a modest home. Streets are not uniformly illuminated. Do not assume modern street lighting or a continuous network of public lamps.

Open flames create risk, especially in crowded timber buildings, warehouses, stables, and workshops. Fire prevention is part of daily life, not merely a response after a blaze.

## 5. Clothing: wear, repair, and status

Clothing is expensive in labor and material. Most people do not replace a garment simply because it is worn. They mend, patch, alter, turn, layer, and pass clothing between family members where practical.

Common materials established in the wider setting include wool, linen, leather, and locally produced textiles. The quality, weave, dye, finish, and amount of cloth affect cost and appearance.

A working person's clothing may show:
- patched elbows and knees;
- reinforced seams;
- stains from fish, grain, soot, oil, mud, or workshop materials;
- worn soles and repaired straps;
- layers adjusted for weather and occupation;
- an apron, belt, hood, cap, gloves, or other practical workwear.

Merchants and craftspeople may afford better cloth and more varied dyes. Nobles can afford finer fabrics, embroidery, imported textiles, fur, jewelry, and specialized tailoring. Clothing communicates status, but it also communicates occupation and whether someone has time or money to maintain their appearance.

The exact local names of garments, regional textile patterns, and Tallowmere tailoring customs remain open. See [Clothing, Textiles, and Personal Adornment](../../world/Clothing_Textiles_and_Personal_Adornment.md) for the wider material framework and established local services.

## 6. Washing and laundry

Washing clothing requires water, fuel where hot water is needed, soap or other cleaning materials, suitable space, and time. Heavy garments are difficult to wash and dry, particularly in wet weather. Linen underlayers and work cloth may be washed more often than thick outer garments.

The Blue Heron offers a listed laundry service with next-day return. This is an established commercial service for travelers and customers able to pay, not evidence that all households use professional laundries.

Households may wash smaller items themselves, share washing arrangements, or pay someone for difficult or bulky loads. The exact number of laundresses, public wash places, soapmakers, and neighborhood laundry customs are not established.

Drying is a practical constraint. A damp garment is uncomfortable and may remain unusable in cold or rainy weather. A crowded home may have little room to hang bedding or clothing without making the interior damp.

## 7. Repair and replacement

Repair is often more economical than replacement. Households may:
- patch garments and reinforce seams;
- replace buttons, ties, buckles, and straps;
- resole shoes and repair boots;
- mend nets, ropes, sacks, and sails;
- rehandle tools and replace worn fittings;
- patch roof coverings and shutters;
- repair pots, baskets, and storage containers.

Tallowmere's working districts support many craftspeople, but no complete roster of tailors, cobblers, laundresses, candle-makers, or household repair specialists is established by this document. Use named businesses and NPCs only where they appear in the relevant city records.

A repair can be a small transaction or an ongoing relationship. A craftsperson may prioritize regular customers, charge more for urgent work, or refuse a repair that would be unsafe.

## 8. Seasonal preparation at the campaign opening

The campaign begins on 1 Harvestfall. The opening season is not automatically cold or stormy, and no specific weather event is established. It is nevertheless a sensible time for households to assess what they will need before winter.

A household with secure income may buy fuel, mend clothing, repair a roof, and increase stores in advance. A poor household may wait until wages arrive, buy supplies in small amounts, or rely on shared resources. A casual dockworker whose shifts are irregular may struggle to plan even when goods are available in the market.

Duskreach and Yearturn customs, harvest work, and winter preparation are covered in the calendar and agricultural records. This reference does not set a precise date when heating begins or a universal quantity of fuel required by household size.

## 9. Household differences

| Household | Likely strengths | Likely pressures |
|---|---|---|
| Wealthy merchant or noble household | Better storage, more rooms, servants, multiple sources of fuel and clothing | Large buildings cost more to heat and maintain; status creates expectations |
| Skilled craft household | Repair skills, work contacts, possible workshop offcuts and trade relationships | Work demands, apprentices, material costs, fire risk |
| Dock laborer's household | Access to quay work and informal exchange networks | Irregular wages, damp clothing, crowded rooms, injury, fuel bought in small quantities |
| Rural smallholder | May have access to some fuel, food stores, animals, and household production | Seasonal labor, weather, transport, limited cash, competing demands on land and time |
| Traveler or sailor | Can purchase provisions and lodging in the port | Must pay for laundry, dry clothing, storage, and replacement gear |
| Hospice resident | May receive temporary shelter, bedding, food, and practical support | Capacity is limited and assistance is not guaranteed |

These are broad patterns. Individual households can be resourceful, comfortable, improvident, generous, or unlucky in ways that do not fit a simple class label.

## 10. Sensory details for play

- A room is warm near the hearth and cold at the far wall.
- Damp wool smells of rain and smoke while it dries.
- A household keeps the best blanket folded away for a guest or a sick relative.
- Firewood is stacked under an eave, with the driest pieces kept for wet days.
- A lamp gives enough light for mending but not for fine work.
- A tailor or cobbler's workbench is crowded with scraps, thread, awls, leather, and half-finished repairs.
- A traveler pays for laundry because wet clothes are miserable to wear on the road.
- A family debates whether to spend coin on fuel now or wait for the next wage.
