# Tallowmere: Communication, Records, and the Movement of News

**Visibility:** GM-ONLY  
**Status:** Established local framework with portrayal guidance  
**Snapshot:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC

This reference applies the continental travel and communication framework to Tallowmere. It explains how information moves through the city, who is likely to know what, how written records function, and why the same event can produce several competing accounts.

It supplements, rather than replaces, `campaign/world/Travel_Communication_and_Information.md`, `campaign/world/Education_Literacy_and_Knowledge.md`, `campaign/locations/starting_city/Current_Situation_and_Rumors.md`, and the existing NPC information-network reference.

## 1. Tallowmere is well connected, but not instantly informed

Tallowmere's harbor, river connections, roads, merchants, religious institutions, civic offices, and mixed population make it an information-rich city. That does not mean everyone knows everything.

Information usually enters through a particular person or institution:

- a ship arrives with news from another port;
- a merchant receives a letter or account;
- a traveler brings a story from inland;
- a temple hears of a death, birth, voyage, or charitable need;
- a civic officer receives a report;
- a household receives private correspondence;
- a market seller repeats something heard from a customer;
- a laborer hears a story from a foreman or fellow worker.

The closer a person is to the source, the more likely they are to have useful detail. Distance, delay, literacy, secrecy, status, and personal motives all affect what survives transmission.

Tallowmere therefore has abundant **information** without having universal **knowledge**.

## 2. Who knows what?

### Ordinary residents

Most residents know what affects daily life: local prices, weather, work opportunities, notable accidents, public disputes, festivals, deaths in their neighborhood, changes at familiar businesses, and major civic announcements.

They may know that House Kharadorn is paying attention to the city without knowing the contents of private correspondence between Kharadorn officials and House Denvor.

### Workers and dockside residents

Dockworkers, sailors, warehouse laborers, shipwrights, porters, and food sellers often hear news before people farther inland because ships and crews are sources of information.

Their knowledge tends to be practical:

- which ships arrived;
- which ships are overdue;
- whether cargo is delayed;
- whether work is available;
- whether a captain is considered reliable;
- what crews claim to have seen;
- whether the harbor is unusually busy.

A sailor's account may be firsthand and still be incomplete.

### Merchants and commercial houses

Merchants may know prices, supply conditions, cargo movements, credit problems, business failures, and distant commercial news earlier than ordinary residents. They also have reasons to protect private information.

A merchant's information is not automatically objective. Commercial advantage can be a powerful reason to exaggerate scarcity, hide a competitor's success, or keep a valuable route secret.

### Civic officials

The Reeve, Harbor Master, magistrates, watch officers, castellans, and other officials have access to records and reports that ordinary residents do not.

Their knowledge is also compartmentalized. A harbor official may know the status of a ship without knowing why its owner is having financial problems. A magistrate may know a case exists without knowing the private history behind it.

Officials can therefore be authoritative about one question and uninformed about another.

### Religious institutions

Temples and shrines hear personal information because people bring births, deaths, marriages, illness, grief, travel concerns, disputes, and requests for assistance to religious institutions.

Pastoral knowledge is not automatically public information. A priest knowing something does not mean the temple's entire staff knows it, and private religious conversations should not become a convenient rumor pipeline without a reason.

### Ratfolk information networks

Ratfolk residents maintain their own household, neighborhood, work, and trade relationships. Surface contacts also provide information through markets, workshops, harbor work, and the city's ordinary institutions.

Do not treat the ratfolk community as one intelligence organization. Different people know different things, and disagreements, mistrust, family obligations, and competing interests exist within it.

The Underways can provide useful local knowledge about places and people that surface residents overlook, but access is personal and uneven.

## 3. Written communication

Written letters are more deliberate than spoken conversation and usually more expensive to produce or transport.

A letter may be carried by:

- a household servant;
- a hired courier;
- a merchant's agent;
- a ship's officer or crew member;
- a religious traveler;
- a trusted friend or relative;
- an official messenger.

There is no established universal postal service.

A letter's reliability depends on its chain of custody. It may be delayed, lost, damaged, opened, forged, copied, or deliberately withheld.

A seal can help identify a sender or reveal interference, but a seal does not prove that the contents are truthful.

### What makes a letter credible?

Recipients may consider:

- the sender's reputation;
- the seal or mark;
- handwriting or known clerk;
- the messenger;
- whether the message fits information already known;
- whether the sender has something to gain;
- whether the document contains specific names, dates, amounts, or other checkable details.

A letter can be authentic and still be wrong.

## 4. Official records

Tallowmere's administration depends on records because the city has property, contracts, harbor activity, taxes or dues, legal disputes, appointments, civic decisions, and other matters that cannot reliably be managed through memory alone.

Relevant records may include:

- council minutes;
- civic notices;
- harbor records;
- commercial ledgers;
- contracts;
- property and rent records;
- court or magistrate records;
- burial records;
- temple records;
- household accounts;
- guild or craft records;
- correspondence.

Records belong to the institution or person maintaining them unless local law or authority establishes otherwise. Do not assume that every citizen can freely inspect every document.

A record can also be incomplete. A clerk may not receive a report, a witness may omit a detail, a document may be filed under an unexpected name, or a dispute may remain unresolved.

## 5. Public announcements

People who cannot read still receive public information.

Tallowmere can communicate through:

- posted notices;
- market announcements;
- bells;
- public readings;
- town criers or officials;
- temple announcements;
- guild meetings;
- harbor instructions;
- word of mouth.

Public communication is especially useful for matters that affect large numbers of people, such as market rules, harbor closures, emergencies, civic meetings, or festival observances.

A public announcement is not necessarily a complete explanation. Authorities may announce what people need to do without explaining every reason behind the decision.

## 6. The rumor chain

When a rumor reaches a PC, determine three things:

**Source:** Who first knew or claimed it?

**Transmission:** Who carried it from that source to the current speaker?

**Distortion:** What changed along the way?

Example:

A sailor sees an unfamiliar vessel offshore.

The sailor tells a dockworker that the vessel appeared to be watching the harbor.

The dockworker tells a tavern patron that a strange ship has been lurking offshore.

The patron tells someone that an enemy ship has been spying on Tallowmere.

The final version may contain a real observation, an interpretation, and a conclusion that no one can currently prove.

This does not mean rumors are useless. A rumor can point toward a real event while being wrong about its cause.

## 7. Competing accounts are normal

Two intelligent people can honestly report different versions of the same event.

A shipowner remembers a loading dispute as a misunderstanding.

A dockworker remembers it as deliberate cheating.

A Harbor Office clerk records only that loading was delayed.

A merchant repeats that the ship was held for unpaid fees.

A sailor claims the cargo was damaged before it reached the quay.

All five accounts can exist without requiring a secret conspiracy.

When the PCs investigate, distinguish:

1. what happened;
2. what witnesses observed;
3. what witnesses inferred;
4. what they were told;
5. what they want the PCs to believe.

## 8. Information and social status

Access to information is itself a form of power.

A noble household can send a trusted servant with a sealed letter.

A merchant can pay a courier.

A guild member can ask a colleague.

A poor laborer may depend on whoever happens to know the right person.

A literate person can read a posted notice directly. Someone who cannot read may need another person to interpret it, creating another opportunity for misunderstanding or manipulation.

This does not mean poor people are ignorant. Practical knowledge can be extensive and highly specialized. The distinction is access to particular channels, not intelligence.

## 9. Tallowmere's current information climate

At the opening of the campaign, several subjects are already circulating through the city:

- harbor allocation and berth access;
- warehouse fire precautions;
- the Northfield harvest;
- ratfolk access to markets;
- House Denvor's succession and household influence;
- Kharadorn's strategic attention;
- rumors concerning the harbor and old quays.

The existing Current Situation and Rumors reference remains authoritative for the specific current rumors and their listed reliability.

Do not turn every rumor into a hidden plot. Some will prove false, some partially true, some misunderstood, and some simply become irrelevant.

## 10. GM adjudication: answering "Would they know?"

When a PC asks whether an NPC knows something, use this sequence:

1. **Is it public?** If yes, the NPC may know it unless there is a reason they were absent or uninformed.
2. **Is it local?** Residents are more likely to know it than travelers.
3. **Is it occupational?** Give relevant workers and specialists better access.
4. **Is it written?** Ask who created, received, stored, or could read the document.
5. **Is it private?** Require a plausible relationship or information channel.
6. **Is it recent?** Account for travel and communication delay.
7. **Is it disputed?** Decide what the NPC believes, not merely what is objectively true.
8. **Would they have a reason to care?** People notice information connected to work, family, money, faith, safety, reputation, or survival.

Do not use a skill check to replace ordinary world knowledge. A check is appropriate when the character is trying to recall specialist information, interpret incomplete evidence, identify a document, reconstruct a rumor chain, or notice something that is genuinely difficult to determine.

## 11. Canon boundaries

**Established:** Tallowmere has overlapping civic, commercial, religious, household, occupational, harbor, and informal information networks. Written communication exists but literacy is not universal. There is no universal postal service. Public announcements allow information to reach people who do not read. Rumors can travel faster than verified reports.

**Portrayal guidance:** The information-source distinctions, rumor-chain method, competing-account examples, and GM adjudication procedure are tools for consistent portrayal. They do not create a new intelligence organization, postal system, censorship office, or secret communications network.

**Unresolved:** Exact messenger fees, courier schedules, document-access procedures for each civic office, local letter-writing rates, regular courier routes, and the precise frequency of public readings remain undefined unless an established record resolves them.
