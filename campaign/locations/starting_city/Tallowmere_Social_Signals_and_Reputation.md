# Tallowmere Social Signals, Etiquette, and Reputation

**Status:** GM-approved worldbuilding expansion  
**Baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY reference  
**Scope:** Tallowmere social behavior, status signals, etiquette, reputation, and ordinary interpersonal expectations

This record fills a practical gap between Tallowmere's institutions and its ordinary-life records. It is intended to help portray what people notice about one another without reducing the city to a rigid etiquette system.

## 1. The basic rule

Tallowmere is a working port city. People constantly judge whether another person is safe, useful, trustworthy, respectable, dangerous, or simply difficult.

Most of these judgments are made quickly from incomplete information.

People notice:

- clothing and cleanliness;
- accent and speech;
- tools and equipment;
- who accompanies someone;
- where they entered the city;
- who vouches for them;
- how they treat servants and laborers;
- whether they pay promptly;
- whether they keep promises;
- whether they understand local customs;
- whether they cause trouble in public.

These observations are not always accurate. A person's reputation can be wrong, unfair, outdated, or deliberately manufactured.

## 2. Introductions matter

A stranger who arrives alone has less social context than someone introduced by a known resident.

A useful introduction may establish:

- name;
- occupation;
- place of origin;
- purpose in Tallowmere;
- who is responsible for them;
- whether they are expected to stay;
- whether they can be trusted with credit or work.

The person giving the introduction accepts some reputational risk.

A merchant introducing a sailor, for example, may be saying more than "I know this person." They may be saying, "If this person cheats you, you can come to me."

This does not create a universal legal guarantee.

## 3. Names and titles

People use titles according to circumstance.

A noble may be addressed formally in public but casually by close family.

A magistrate may be addressed by office during a hearing and by name in a private conversation.

A guild master may expect recognition of the office even if they possess no noble title.

A person with no title may still command substantial respect because of wealth, craft skill, family reputation, religious standing, or long service.

Using an inappropriate title can be accidental, insulting, or politically useful.

## 4. Clothing is information

People do not merely ask, "Is that person rich?"

They may ask:

- What kind of work do they do?
- Are those clothes practical or ceremonial?
- Are the boots maintained?
- Is the fabric locally made?
- Is the person wearing a house color or badge?
- Are the tools good enough for the claimed occupation?
- Does the clothing look borrowed?
- Has the traveler prepared for the weather?

A person dressed too finely for their claimed occupation may attract curiosity.

A person dressed too poorly for a claimed position may attract suspicion.

Neither conclusion is necessarily correct.

## 5. Cleanliness and presentation

Clean clothing, washed hands, trimmed hair, maintained boots, and reasonable grooming communicate that a person has time, water, and social expectations to meet.

But Tallowmere is a working city.

Fishers smell like fish.

Smiths smell of smoke and metal.

Tanners smell strongly.

Dockworkers may be dirty at the end of a shift.

A person who refuses ordinary cleanliness when circumstances permit may be judged differently from someone who has simply finished difficult work.

## 6. Eating and drinking in public

Eating is social information.

A person's choices may reveal:

- wealth;
- occupation;
- religious practice;
- origin;
- dietary restrictions;
- whether they are traveling or local.

Buying the cheapest food is not shameful by itself.

Buying expensive food and refusing to pay is.

Ordering more than one can afford is especially foolish because tavern and market staff remember repeat customers.

A person who buys food for others may be generous, attempting to build loyalty, showing off, or paying a debt.

The reason is often more important than the gesture.

## 7. Paying and credit

Tallowmere is a commercial city, so reputation around payment matters.

A person may pay with:

- coin;
- an established account;
- a written obligation;
- a known merchant's credit;
- a trusted intermediary.

A new arrival asking for substantial credit without references is likely to be refused or asked for security.

A respected regular may receive food or lodging on account because the proprietor expects payment later.

Repeated failure to pay can spread through merchant networks faster than a formal court case.

## 8. Hospitality

Hospitality is expected to be reasonable rather than limitless.

A host may provide:

- water;
- food;
- a seat;
- directions;
- temporary shelter;
- a place for an animal;
- useful local information.

A guest is expected to:

- behave respectfully;
- avoid unnecessary damage;
- observe household boundaries;
- not threaten servants or family;
- contribute when appropriate;
- leave when the hospitality is clearly finished.

Noble hospitality is more formal and can carry political meaning.

Among ordinary people, hospitality is often practical rather than ceremonial.

## 9. Gifts

Gifts can create obligations.

Small gifts may be ordinary courtesy.

Useful gifts can establish friendship or gratitude.

Expensive gifts may create suspicion if given without explanation.

A gift to a public official can be interpreted as bribery depending on circumstances.

A gift to a craftsman before a job may be interpreted as goodwill, payment in advance, or an attempt to obtain preferential treatment.

Context determines meaning.

## 10. Bargaining

Bargaining is normal in some markets and occupations and inappropriate in others.

A customer may reasonably negotiate over:

- bulk purchases;
- secondhand goods;
- certain services;
- uncertain-quality merchandise;
- direct trade with producers.

A customer should not assume every posted or customary price is an invitation to haggle.

Insulting the seller usually accomplishes little.

A person who bargains aggressively and then pays promptly may still be respected as a good customer.

A person who agrees to a price and later refuses payment is remembered much more negatively.

## 11. Public arguments

Tallowmere's streets are busy enough that public arguments attract witnesses.

People may watch without intervening.

They may intervene if:

- someone appears likely to be seriously injured;
- property is being destroyed;
- children are endangered;
- a fire begins;
- a weapon is drawn;
- the Watch arrives;
- the dispute threatens a business or public route.

Many people prefer not to become involved in another person's legal problem.

A witness may later tell the story to friends even if they refused to intervene.

## 12. Insults

Insults are not all equal.

Calling someone foolish may be ordinary anger.

Insulting a person's family, trade, faith, ancestry, or honor can be more serious.

Publicly accusing a merchant of cheating may damage their business.

Calling a noble's legitimacy into question may be politically dangerous.

Calling a craftsman's work unsafe can provoke a stronger reaction than insulting the craftsman personally.

The social meaning depends on what the insult threatens.

## 13. Reputation travels through networks

Tallowmere's reputation system is informal but powerful.

Information travels through:

- taverns;
- markets;
- docks;
- workshops;
- temples;
- merchants;
- guild contacts;
- household servants;
- sailors;
- magistrates;
- relatives;
- children;
- neighboring businesses.

A person does not need to be famous to become known.

A traveler who spends three weeks cheating dockworkers may be remembered by several people who have never formally met.

A person who pays fairly, protects a weaker worker, returns a lost item, or behaves reliably can also develop a positive reputation.

## 14. Reputation is local

Being respected in one part of the city does not guarantee respect elsewhere.

A successful merchant may be trusted by shopkeepers and distrusted by dockworkers.

A watch officer may be respected by magistrates and hated by people repeatedly arrested under their patrol.

A priest may be beloved by parishioners and considered meddlesome by merchants.

A ratfolk trader may be trusted within their network while facing prejudice from unrelated surface residents.

Reputation is therefore a collection of overlapping judgments, not one numerical score.

## 15. Ratfolk and unequal social treatment

Ratfolk residents experience the same ordinary social rules as everyone else, but prejudice changes how those rules are applied.

Some surface residents may:

- refuse service;
- demand higher prices;
- distrust unfamiliar ratfolk;
- assume criminal intent;
- deny lodging;
- refuse to touch goods handled by ratfolk;
- treat ratfolk testimony as less credible.

Other residents trade with ratfolk routinely and judge individuals by reputation.

The Underways themselves are not a single social world. Different communities and households have different relationships with surface residents.

This creates practical situations in which a ratfolk character may know a great deal about a neighborhood while still being treated as an outsider by some businesses.

## 16. Nobles in ordinary spaces

A noble entering a market or tavern changes the social temperature.

People may:

- make room;
- lower their voices;
- watch closely;
- attempt to approach;
- avoid eye contact;
- hope to attract patronage;
- worry that a dispute will become politically dangerous.

This does not mean everyone becomes silent or kneels.

Tallowmere is a commercial city. Merchants, sailors, craftsmen, and officials regularly interact with people of different status.

A noble who behaves casually may be liked.

A noble who treats ordinary workers badly may become a subject of gossip.

## 17. Servants and household staff

Servants often know more about a household than outsiders realize.

They may know:

- who visits;
- who argues;
- who drinks too much;
- which family member is favored;
- which debts are overdue;
- what deliveries arrive;
- which rooms are rarely used;
- which visitors are unwelcome.

This does not make servants omniscient.

They see only what their work gives them access to, and they may misunderstand what they observe.

A servant's loyalty may be to a person, household, wage, faith, family, or personal interest rather than to an abstract concept of service.

## 18. Children and social knowledge

Children are often underestimated sources of information.

They notice:

- new faces;
- unusual carts;
- changes in shop hours;
- arguments;
- damaged buildings;
- who visits whom;
- which adults are afraid;
- which alleys adults avoid.

They also misunderstand things.

Children repeat rumors without understanding them, exaggerate stories, and combine unrelated events.

A child saying "the baker is a spy" does not mean the baker is a spy.

It means someone told a child something interesting.

## 19. Strangers

A traveler is not automatically suspicious.

Tallowmere expects strangers because it is a port.

What matters is whether the stranger can explain themselves reasonably.

A traveler who says:

> "I am from upriver, looking for work at the docks."

is ordinary.

A traveler who refuses to say where they came from while asking questions about the watch, harbor defenses, noble residences, and warehouses may attract attention.

Suspicion is usually built from behavior rather than ancestry alone.

## 20. Common mistakes outsiders make

A newcomer may accidentally:

- assume every tavern serves the same clientele;
- mistake a commercial dispute for a personal feud;
- treat a guild custom as citywide law;
- assume a priest can cast spells;
- assume a noble owns everything around their residence;
- assume the Watch handles every dispute;
- assume the Underways are one organized faction;
- mistake a rumor for public fact;
- bargain where a fixed professional fee is customary;
- insult a family when intending to insult an individual;
- ignore a local funeral procession;
- enter a restricted work area because no sign is posted.

Most mistakes are recoverable.

Repeated mistakes become a reputation.

## 21. How Tallowmere remembers people

The city rarely remembers everyone equally.

People become memorable because they are:

- generous;
- dangerous;
- unusually skilled;
- wealthy;
- politically connected;
- involved in scandal;
- entertaining;
- reliable;
- strange;
- associated with a memorable event.

A person can become locally famous without ever becoming important to the wider world.

This is useful for player characters. If they spend several sessions in Tallowmere, people should begin recognizing them based on what they actually do.

## 22. Practical GM use

When deciding how an NPC reacts to a PC, consider:

1. Does the NPC know the PC?
2. If not, who introduced them?
3. What does the NPC see immediately?
4. What does the NPC already know?
5. What social class or occupation does the NPC belong to?
6. What does the PC's behavior imply?
7. Does the NPC have something to gain or lose?
8. Would the NPC risk acting publicly?
9. What would the NPC tell others afterward?

Do not roll for every social interaction.

Most ordinary interactions should simply follow established expectations.

Use checks when uncertainty matters, when the character is attempting something difficult, or when the NPC's response is genuinely unclear.

## 23. Common knowledge versus specialist knowledge

### Common knowledge

Most Tallowmere residents understand that:

- introductions matter;
- reputation matters;
- merchants remember bad debts;
- nobles possess social power;
- the Watch can become involved in public disorder;
- taverns and markets are major sources of news;
- ratfolk may face prejudice;
- strangers are normal in a port;
- public insults can have consequences.

### Local knowledge

A resident may additionally know:

- which businesses tolerate credit;
- which taverns attract sailors;
- which streets are safer at night;
- which officials are approachable;
- which families are feuding;
- which merchants are honest;
- where a particular trade gathers.

### Specialist knowledge

A merchant, dockworker, priest, Watch officer, magistrate, guild member, or ratfolk community member may know much more about their own network.

### GM-only truth

The GM should track the actual reasons behind important relationships and rumors separately from what people believe.

A reputation should never become objective truth merely because many NPCs repeat it.

## 24. Core social principle

Tallowmere is not a city where everyone knows everyone.

It is a city where **enough people know someone who knows someone** that behavior leaves traces.

That is why reputation matters.

And that is why a stranger can still disappear into the crowd if they give people no reason to remember them.
