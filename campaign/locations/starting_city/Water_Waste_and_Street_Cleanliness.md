# Water, Waste, and Street Cleanliness in Tallowmere

**Status:** ESTABLISHED INFRASTRUCTURE / PROPOSED DAILY PRACTICE  
**Snapshot:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY  
**Scope:** Tallowmere city  
**Related records:** [Residential Life and Housing](Residential_Life_and_Housing.md); [Medicine, Disease, and Healing](../../world/Medicine_Disease_and_Healing.md); [Medical Care in Tallowmere](Medical_Care_in_Tallowmere.md); [Streets, Landmarks, and Routes](Streets_Landmarks_and_Routes.md)

## 1. Why this matters

Water and waste are not background decoration. They affect where people live, what they pay for, how they cook, how they work, where disease risk increases, and which streets become unpleasant or dangerous after rain.

Tallowmere is a working port of roughly 18,000 people. Its sanitation is uneven. The city has public wells, pumps, cisterns, drains, privies, refuse arrangements, cellars, granaries, stables, and a working harbor. Wealthier homes may have private wells or cisterns and more room to store waste safely; crowded households rely more heavily on shared facilities and neighborhood arrangements.

This record does not establish a modern sewer system, a universal refuse service, a citywide clean-water guarantee, or a dedicated public-health department.

## 2. Water sources and access

### Public wells and pumps

Public wells and pumps are part of the city's established infrastructure. They are essential to households without private sources. Their condition, depth, reliability, and maintenance vary. A public water point is also a social space: people wait, exchange news, carry heavy vessels, and notice who is drawing water.

A well can be convenient without being safe. Runoff, nearby privies, animal waste, flooding, damaged linings, and careless storage can compromise water quality. No specific contaminated well or current outbreak is established.

### The Old Cistern

The Old Cistern is a covered public water cistern in the older city. It is maintained through civic labor and neighborhood arrangements and is especially useful during dry periods or fires. It is not connected to every water chamber in the Underways. Its exact capacity, refill source, inspection routine, and allocation rules remain open.

Do not assume the Old Cistern supplies the whole city. It is one important resource within a varied urban water system.

### Private wells and household storage

Wealthier households may have private wells or cisterns. Other residents draw water from public facilities and carry it home in buckets, jars, or casks. Storage vessels need cleaning and protection from dirt, insects, and animals. A clean source can be spoiled by dirty handling or storage.

The exact number and location of private wells, public pumps, and cisterns are not recorded. Do not invent a complete water map.

### The harbor and River Vale

The harbor and river are working waterways, not presumed drinking-water sources. People may use river water for washing, industrial tasks, animal needs, or other purposes where practical, but water near docks, mills, drains, or refuse should not automatically be treated as potable. The specific local rules for water abstraction and discharge remain unresolved.

## 3. Privies, drains, and refuse

Tallowmere has privies, drains, and refuse arrangements, but their coverage and management are uneven. The city is not uniformly clean, and there is no established universal collection schedule.

A household may use a private privy, a shared facility, or a neighborhood arrangement depending on property, space, and means. Waste must be removed, buried, stored, or otherwise managed somewhere; the exact service providers, disposal grounds, fees, and rules are not established.

Drains move water away from streets and buildings where they function. They can clog with silt, straw, ash, food scraps, animal waste, and other debris. A drain is not automatically a sewer carrying all household waste away. Do not assume that every drain is safe to enter or connected to a single underground network.

Refuse may include kitchen scraps, ash, broken pottery, spoiled material, sweepings, animal bedding, and workshop waste. Some material can be reused, fed to animals where safe, composted, or recovered; other refuse is simply a nuisance requiring removal. Specific scavenger organizations, collection contracts, and disposal sites have not been established.

## 4. District differences

These are broad conditions based on established district character, not a street-by-street sanitation survey.

### Greywatch Rise

The noble precinct and its surrounding houses have more space, security, servants, and resources for maintaining private facilities. The castle's stores, stables, and household operations still create waste and water demands. Better resources do not mean no hazards.

### High Ward

Comfortable residences and commercial offices may have private storage, wells, or better access to maintained public facilities. Household servants and property staff perform much of the daily cleaning. Exact arrangements vary by property.

### Old Quays

The older waterfront combines dense buildings, working boats, food handling, taverns, warehouses, and heavy foot traffic. Fish waste, bilge, cargo debris, animals, and refuse can make some locations unpleasant. The degree of harbor cleaning and enforcement is unresolved.

### New Quays

Bulk cargo, larger yards, warehouses, carts, and crews produce substantial refuse and animal waste. More open space may make some tasks easier, but heavy use, temporary storage, and uneven construction create their own problems. No specific waste contractor or quay-cleaning schedule is established.

### Market Ward

Food stalls, shops, bakeries, moneychangers, and dense foot traffic create daily sweepings, food scraps, ash, packaging, and animal waste. Cleanliness matters commercially: customers avoid foul stalls, and spoiled food can damage reputation. The exact authority responsible for market refuse and inspection remains open.

### Rivergate

Lower streets near the river can become muddy or flood-prone after heavy rain. Mills, cart yards, workshops, ferries, stables, and river warehouses create varied waste streams. Floodwater can spread mud, refuse, and animal waste into places that are ordinarily dry.

### Lantern Ward

Temple grounds, hospice gardens, burial spaces, charitable accommodation, and healers' residences create a quieter district but still require water, cleaning, and waste handling. The hospice and religious houses have finite resources; do not assume their grounds are pristine or that their charitable work includes citywide sanitation.

### The Underways

The Underways include inhabited chambers, old foundations, service passages, natural cavities, water chambers, and connected routes beneath several districts. Water and waste arrangements vary between neighborhoods and spaces. Some water chambers are locally maintained; they are not all connected to the Old Cistern or to a single city system.

Surface residents often misunderstand the Underways' infrastructure. Do not assume ratfolk communities share one water authority, one sanitation practice, or one standard of cleanliness.

## 5. Rain, flooding, and seasonal problems

Heavy rain can turn lower streets muddy, overwhelm local drainage, seep into cellars, and carry refuse into yards or water sources. Rivergate and lower waterfront streets are especially exposed to wet conditions. Floodwater may leave silt, debris, animal waste, and spoiled goods after it recedes.

Dry periods create different pressures. Public water points and cisterns become more important, stored water must be protected, and fire risk increases. The Old Cistern's usefulness during dry periods and fires is established; no current drought or water emergency is in effect at the campaign opening.

Seasonal conditions should be judged from the campaign's actual weather and events. Do not turn the first autumn rain into a citywide flood without a reason.

## 6. Work, responsibility, and disputes

Water and waste involve several kinds of labor:
- Households and servants draw, carry, store, and clean water.
- Property owners maintain private facilities and arrange household refuse handling.
- Civic labor and neighborhood arrangements maintain the Old Cistern.
- Drains require clearing and repair.
- Stables and workshops manage animal and craft waste.
- Markets and quays must keep working surfaces usable.
- Residents may complain when upstream or neighboring activity makes their own water or property unsafe.

These roles are practical categories, not a confirmed municipal department chart. The exact division of responsibility between the lordship, chartered council, property owners, guilds, neighborhoods, and private contractors is unresolved.

Potential disputes include a privy contaminating a nearby well, a blocked drain flooding a cellar, a workshop dumping waste near a public path, a stable creating a nuisance, or a property owner refusing to pay for shared maintenance. These are scenario possibilities, not current canon events.

## 7. Public health and ordinary behavior

People may know by experience that some wells taste foul, that water downstream of certain work sites is unpleasant, or that a drain becomes dangerous after a storm. Such local observations do not require modern germ theory.

Households may boil water for certain uses, let sediment settle, filter it through cloth, or prefer a known source. These are plausible practices, not universal city rules. Boiling requires fuel, time, and suitable vessels. Cloth filtering can remove visible debris but does not guarantee safe water.

People may also disagree about what makes water dangerous. Some blame bad air, curses, divine displeasure, or the habits of particular neighbors. A belief may shape behavior without being medically correct. Do not validate prejudice against ratfolk or poor districts as a biological fact.

The actual health consequences of contaminated water should use the campaign's medicine and disease framework. No named endemic waterborne disease, current contamination event, or epidemic is established.

## 8. Sensory details for play

- A public pump handle is polished by use; the stone around its base is worn and damp.
- Buckets knock against doorframes as residents carry water through narrow streets.
- A covered cistern is cool and shadowed, with the smell of wet stone.
- After rain, cart wheels cut dark tracks through mud near Rivergate.
- Market sweepings gather at the edges of busy lanes before someone clears them.
- A blocked drain leaves a shallow pool that smells worse as the day warms.
- Near the Old Quays, fish scales, wet rope, bilge, salt, and smoke mingle.
- A well-kept courtyard may smell of herbs and washed stone while a crowded lane nearby smells of damp timber, animals, and refuse.

Use these details selectively. Tallowmere is a functioning city, not one continuous sewer-themed attraction.

## 9. What remains unresolved

- The complete location and condition of public wells and pumps.
- The source, capacity, refill, inspection, and allocation practices of the Old Cistern.
- Whether any districts have formal water carriers or paid water sellers.
- The location and management of refuse dumps, night-soil removal, composting, and animal carcass disposal.
- The precise design and extent of drains, and which drains carry surface runoff versus other waste.
- Who is responsible for cleaning streets, drains, markets, quays, and shared privies.
- Whether public-health inspections, quarantine powers, or well closures exist under local law.
- Any active contamination, outbreak, drought, or flood emergency.

Do not add a citywide sanitation authority, universal sewer system, or public-health ordinance without a separate canon decision.
