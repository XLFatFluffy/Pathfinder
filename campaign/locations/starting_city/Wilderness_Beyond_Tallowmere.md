# Wilderness Beyond Tallowmere: Frontier Regions, Ruins, and Resources

**Status:** Initial wilderness framework established; individual lairs, encounter placements, and hidden histories remain unassigned.  
**Last updated:** 2026-11-04

This document defines the first dangerous country beyond Tallowmere's settled hinterland. It is intended to support exploration, travel, resource gathering, and later expansion. The wilderness is not uniformly hostile: there are worked woodlands, grazing routes, hunting grounds, abandoned structures, and places where people still travel. Danger increases where roads, patrols, and ordinary livelihoods thin out.

This is a regional GM reference. It does not establish a new rules subsystem, mandatory crafting ingredients, or a fixed sequence of encounters.

## 1. Wilderness Beyond the Settled Region

The settled region around Tallowmere gives way gradually to rougher country. Farms become scattered, maintained roads turn into tracks, and local authorities become less able to respond quickly.

The first wilderness belt consists of four connected environments:

1. **The Deep Bracken:** older and less-managed woodland beyond Brackenholt and the Brackenwood.
2. **The Upper Vale:** the River Vale's western headwaters, broken hills, ravines, and upland pasture.
3. **The Greyback Heights:** rougher ridges and high ground beyond Stonehollow's worked quarries.
4. **The Siltfen:** low wet ground and reed beds along a slow tributary basin south and southeast of the River Vale.

The Eastern Shore also becomes less settled beyond Saltwick, with exposed coves and isolated fishing camps. These areas are reachable from Tallowmere, but not all are safe for casual travel.

## 2. The Deep Bracken

**Character:** Older woodland, uneven ground, abandoned clearings, game trails, and intermittent charcoal camps.  
**Access:** Western Track and lesser woodland paths beyond Brackenholt.  
**Authority:** Denvor customary woodland rights weaken toward the outer forest; exact boundary is not surveyed.

The Deep Bracken is the less-managed continuation of the Brackenwood. Its trees are older, the canopy denser, and the paths less reliable. Some clearings show signs of earlier cutting or settlement. Seasonal workers know a few dependable routes, but no single map covers the whole wood.

**Ordinary uses**
- Hunting and trapping.
- Gathering medicinal and culinary plants.
- Coppice, poles, fallen timber, and limited charcoal production.
- Collecting mushrooms, resins, bark, and other mundane craft supplies.
- Finding lost livestock and recovering equipment.

**Risks**
- Poor visibility and disorientation away from marked paths.
- Sudden weather changes, fallen trees, and difficult stream crossings.
- Predators following game trails.
- Abandoned pits, old charcoal mounds, and unstable ground.
- Occasional conflict over cutting rights and unauthorized trapping.

**Wildlife and Pathfinder threats**
Common animals include deer, boar, foxes, badgers, and wolves. More dangerous encounters may involve dire wolves, giant spiders, ettercaps, owlbears, or other forest creatures appropriate to the specific site and party level. These are possible inhabitants of the wider woodland, not a claim that every listed creature lives near every trail.

## 3. The Upper Vale

**Character:** River headwaters, steep-sided valleys, stony streams, small caves, upland pasture, and scattered shepherd shelters.  
**Access:** River Vale Road beyond Fordmere, with smaller tracks branching into the western hills.  
**Authority:** Several local customary grazing and water rights; no single lordship has a complete presence throughout the upper valleys.

The Upper Vale is a working landscape in its lower reaches and increasingly remote country farther west. Small communities move livestock between pasture areas. River pilots and charcoal burners sometimes travel farther upstream than ordinary merchants.

**Ordinary uses**
- Seasonal grazing and dairy production.
- Fishing and freshwater gathering.
- Small-scale hunting.
- Collection of useful stone, clay, and mineral deposits where accessible.
- Water-powered craft at suitable streams.

**Risks**
- Flash floods and cold, fast water.
- Rockfalls and unstable slopes.
- Narrow trails with poor sightlines.
- Isolated homesteads that may be days from help.
- Predators and creatures using caves or ravines as shelter.

**Wildlife and Pathfinder threats**
Wolves, mountain cats, bears, giant eagles, and dangerous boars are plausible. More unusual threats may include harpies in remote cliffs, giant lizards in warmer ravines, or cave-dwelling creatures in isolated systems. Creature placement should follow terrain and established Pathfinder ecology rather than random monster distribution.

## 4. The Greyback Heights

**Character:** Broken ridges, exposed rock, abandoned prospecting cuts, narrow passes, and rough upland tracks.  
**Access:** Quarry Road beyond Stonehollow and lesser routes used by quarry workers and shepherds.  
**Authority:** Stonehollow's quarry charters cover defined worked areas only; the wider heights are not fully surveyed or uniformly claimed.

The Greyback Heights rise beyond the accessible quarry faces. The ground is rocky and difficult for carts. Old prospecting cuts and small caves are common, but most are unremarkable. Some contain unstable ceilings, standing water, or traces of earlier human work.

**Ordinary uses**
- Stone, gravel, and clay extraction.
- Prospecting for common minerals.
- Grazing on sparse high pasture.
- Hunting and gathering.
- Salvage from abandoned work sites, where ownership is not disputed.

**Risks**
- Loose scree, hidden shafts, and collapsing adits.
- Sudden storms and exposure.
- Difficult navigation in fog.
- Territorial disputes over newly discovered deposits.
- Creatures sheltering in caves or abandoned cuts.

**Wildlife and Pathfinder threats**
Mountain lions, bears, giant eagles, monstrous vermin, and cave-dwelling creatures may be encountered. Ankhegs or similar burrowing threats are possible in suitable soil at the lower edges of the ridges, but are not established as a local infestation. More powerful subterranean predators belong deeper in the hills and should be placed deliberately.

## 5. The Siltfen

**Character:** Wet meadows, reed beds, shallow pools, muddy channels, alder stands, and seasonally flooded ground.  
**Access:** South and southeast of the lower River Vale, reached by farm lanes and small boat landings.  
**Authority:** Local grazing, reed-cutting, and fishing rights overlap; exact jurisdiction remains open.

The Siltfen is not an endless swamp. It is a low, shifting wetland with firm ridges, seasonal pools, channels, and patches of dry ground. Local people harvest reeds, fish, waterfowl, and useful plants. Travel is possible with local knowledge, but a straight line on a map rarely gives the easiest route.

**Ordinary uses**
- Reed cutting for thatch, mats, and basketry.
- Fishing, eels, frogs, and waterfowl.
- Gathering suitable wetland plants.
- Seasonal grazing on firmer ground.
- Clay and peat in limited areas.

**Risks**
- Soft ground, hidden channels, and sudden changes in water depth.
- Insects, illness, and contaminated standing water.
- Fog and disorientation.
- Snakes and territorial wildlife.
- Flooding that erases familiar paths.

**Wildlife and Pathfinder threats**
Ordinary snakes, crocodiles only in suitable warmer waters, giant frogs, stirges, giant insects, and dangerous vermin may fit particular parts of the wetland. Undead or more overtly supernatural threats are not a default feature of the Siltfen; any such presence should be tied to a specific cause and established deliberately.

## 6. The Outer Eastern Shore

Beyond Saltwick, the shore becomes more exposed and less continuously settled. Small coves, shingle beaches, low cliffs, and isolated landing places provide shelter only in certain weather.

Fishing crews sometimes use temporary camps during good seasons. Wreck salvage can be valuable but is governed by local custom, ownership claims, and the danger of working in surf or unstable wreckage.

**Risks**
- Sudden squalls and rough water.
- Slippery rock shelves and unstable cliffs.
- Smugglers or opportunistic wreckers in isolated places.
- Predatory coastal wildlife.
- Limited fresh water and few reliable shelters.

The shore is not under one unified criminal group. Any illicit operation should be local, specific, and independently motivated.

## 7. Abandoned and Historical Sites

The following sites are established as physical locations or remains. Their deeper histories and any hidden contents are intentionally unresolved.

### Rook's Watch
**Type:** Ruined roadside watch post  
**Location:** A wooded rise west of the River Vale Road, beyond the more regularly patrolled farms.

Rook's Watch is a small stone watch post, now roofless and partly collapsed. Its position suggests it once watched a minor route through the western hills. It is too small to have been a major castle or seat of a powerful house.

The name is a local name; no reliable record identifies the original builder or the person called Rook. The structure contains no established magical feature or confirmed treasure.

### The Broken Mile
**Type:** Remnant of an older paved road  
**Location:** A stretch of raised stone road in the lower Greyback country, branching away from the modern Quarry Road.

The Broken Mile is a surviving section of old paving, partly swallowed by scrub and soil. It does not align perfectly with the present road. Local accounts disagree about whether it was a military road, a trade route, or a route to a settlement that no longer exists.

Its age, builders, and original destination are unresolved. It is not yet connected to the Great Rebellion or any named dynasty.

### Alder Chapel
**Type:** Abandoned rural chapel  
**Location:** On a low rise near the outer edge of the Siltfen.

Alder Chapel is a small stone religious building, abandoned after its surrounding hamlet declined. Its dedication is worn and cannot be read with certainty. The building has a simple nave, a collapsed side room, and a small burial ground.

The chapel is not established as cursed or haunted. Its former congregation, patron, deity, and reason for abandonment remain open.

### The Shepherd's Fold
**Type:** Ruined seasonal livestock enclosure  
**Location:** Upper Vale, near an old upland track.

A dry-stone enclosure and several roofless shelters mark a former seasonal grazing station. Shepherds still use the outer pasture in good weather, but few sleep inside the old structures. The site is useful as a landmark and occasional shelter, though it offers little protection from determined threats or severe weather.

### The Drowned Storehouse
**Type:** Partially submerged riverside structure  
**Location:** A backwater channel below Millhaven, accessible only when water levels are favorable.

The remains of a stone-and-timber storehouse sit beside a silted channel. Most of the lower floor is flooded or buried. Local stories say it was abandoned after repeated flooding, but no definitive record survives.

Its ownership and contents are unknown. It is dangerous to enter without assessing water depth, structural stability, and current.

## 8. Resource Gathering and Crafting Opportunities

The wilderness offers ordinary resources and occasional valuable finds. Availability depends on season, location, weather, local rights, and competition. These opportunities support the PCs' interests without changing Pathfinder's published crafting rules.

### Common resources
- Useful timber, poles, bark, resin, and charcoal.
- Medicinal and culinary plants.
- Mushrooms, fibers, reeds, and dyes.
- Clay, stone, gravel, and common mineral deposits.
- Animal hides, bone, horn, sinew, and other salvageable materials.
- Fish, game, and preserved food supplies.

### Less common finds
- Unusually pure mineral salts or pigments.
- Rare local plant varieties sought by specialist herbalists or alchemists.
- High-quality resins, waxes, and plant oils.
- Unusual animal products with legitimate craft or trade value.
- Old tools, glass, metal fittings, and other salvage from abandoned sites.

A rare find is not automatically a magic-item ingredient. It may be valuable because of its quality, scarcity, usefulness to a craft, or demand from a particular buyer.

### Magic item creation: rules boundary
- Pathfinder First Edition item creation rules remain authoritative.
- This document does not add mandatory ingredients, special checks, extra costs, or new prerequisites to published crafting rules.
- A PC who meets the published requirements can attempt the relevant crafting activity, subject to the actual availability of ordinary services, facilities, time, spells, and materials in the world.
- Rare materials may be sought for trade, research, experimentation, or a buyer's request, but do not become compulsory components unless a published rule or explicitly approved house rule says so.
- An unusual ingredient's value must be established through provenance, quality, scarcity, or a real buyer, not an invented universal price.

## 9. Threats and Encounter Scale

The two PCs begin at level 5. The following are broad GM planning bands, not a promise that every location is balanced or safe.

- **Settled fringe:** ordinary wildlife, isolated hazards, low-level vermin, and occasional small groups of dangerous creatures.
- **Outer woodland and wetlands:** threats broadly suitable for low- to mid-level adventurers, including predators, giant vermin, and isolated monstrous creatures.
- **Remote uplands and deeper caves:** potentially more dangerous creatures and environmental hazards; parties should have warning signs and meaningful choices about approach.
- **Specific lairs or ancient sites:** may contain threats well above the party's level, but their danger should be telegraphed through tracks, local reports, remains, behavior, or visible signs.

Use Pathfinder First Edition stat blocks and encounter rules. Do not alter monster statistics to force a desired story outcome. Avoid treating every random encounter as combat; tracks, warning signs, animal behavior, weather, negotiation, and retreat are all valid play.

## 10. Regional Principles for GM Use

1. Wilderness is dangerous because protection, information, and infrastructure diminish beyond settled areas, not because every square mile contains a monster.
2. Local people understand their own routes and seasons. Their knowledge may be incomplete, biased, or outdated, but should not be treated as useless.
3. Resources have owners, customary rights, labor costs, transport costs, and buyers. Gathering something does not automatically grant unrestricted commercial rights.
4. Abandoned structures are not automatically treasure dungeons. Some are simply old, damaged, and forgotten.
5. Supernatural horror remains a supporting element. It should be memorable when used, not routine background noise.
6. The world continues to function when the PCs are elsewhere. Wildlife moves, weather changes, workers harvest, structures decay, and local disputes develop independently.
7. The PCs may explore, harvest, trade, map, settle, hire guides, or ignore any site. No single path is required.

## 11. Unresolved Details

- Exact boundaries of the Deep Bracken, Upper Vale, Greyback Heights, and Siltfen.
- Exact locations and histories of the abandoned sites.
- Which local authorities claim particular hunting, grazing, quarrying, and gathering rights.
- Seasonal calendars for wetland harvest, upland grazing, and woodland cutting.
- Which rare plants, minerals, or animal products have established buyers and prices.
- Whether any site has a connection to the Great Rebellion, the fallen dynasty, or older regional powers.
- Which specific lairs and creatures inhabit each wilderness zone.
- Whether additional settlements exist farther beyond the first wilderness belt.

These details should be added when play or a deliberate worldbuilding stage requires them, not filled with arbitrary secrets in advance.
