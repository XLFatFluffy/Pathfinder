# Arcane Arts and Wizardry

**Status:** ESTABLISHED campaign lore  
**Visibility:** GM reference; reveal details to players through plausible character knowledge  
**Baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Ruleset:** Pathfinder First Edition

## 1. The place of magic in ordinary life

Magic is real, but genuine magic is almost absent from ordinary common life. Most commoners have never seen a spell cast and may never knowingly encounter a genuine magic item. They may know stories, rumors, religious claims, and travelers' accounts, but those are not the same as direct experience. A village can have active religious life without its residents witnessing divine spellcasting.

A visible spell can be startling, frightening, awe-inspiring, or treated as proof of a remarkable event. Reactions vary by region, faith, prior experience, and the spell's apparent purpose. Do not assume every commoner responds with the same fear or reverence.

## 2. Arcane practitioners

Wizards and other arcane spellcasters are exceptionally rare. Most recognized practitioners are learned, wealthy, and attached to a Great House or another powerful patron. A Great House may retain one or more practitioners, but the number is not standardized and must be established for each house rather than assumed. Some practitioners serve a royal office, a wealthy minor house, or a private patron; a small number remain independent.

A noble-house wizard generally serves two overlapping purposes:

1. **Arcane counsel and service:** research, identification, divination, magical protection, investigation, communication, specialist advice, and other services the individual can actually provide under Pathfinder rules.
2. **Magical craftsmanship:** producing commissioned or house-owned magical items when the wizard possesses the required feats, spells, caster level, skills, time, and resources.

A wizard is a strategic household specialist, not automatically a battlefield weapon. Their value may lie in rare knowledge, detection, protection, research, or the ability to solve a problem that ordinary officers cannot address. Their actual abilities must be supported by Pathfinder First Edition rules; no plot-only powers are assumed.

## 3. How arcane knowledge is learned

There are no magical academies, public schools of wizardry, standardized arcane curricula, or continent-wide institutions that routinely train spellcasters.

The recognized paths into arcane practice are limited:

- **Private apprenticeship:** The most reliable route. An established wizard takes a learner and teaches through years of supervised study, copying, research, preparation, observation, and practical work. Apprenticeships are selective and may depend on patronage, family connections, demonstrated aptitude, or the master's personal choice. An apprentice is not guaranteed to become a capable wizard.
- **Self-directed study:** A rare individual may find a spellbook, treatise, private journal, or fragmentary manuscript and attempt to teach themselves. Texts are scarce, incomplete, difficult to interpret, and not necessarily safe or accurate. Possessing a book does not automatically grant the knowledge, spells, feats, or class abilities it describes.
- **Personal discovery:** An unusual individual may develop knowledge through experimentation and accumulated study. This is difficult, uncertain, and not a substitute for Pathfinder character advancement or prerequisites.

Most aspiring students never become recognized practitioners. Some remain assistants, copyists, or household researchers. Some abandon the effort. A few become capable in their own right.

## 4. The Collegium of the Nine Lamps

The Collegium of the Nine Lamps is an independent scholarly network concerned with records, languages, mathematics, medicine, history, law, natural philosophy, and related learning. It is not a magical academy, does not provide standardized wizard training, and does not control arcane practice. Individual scholars may preserve, copy, translate, or study magical manuscripts as historical or intellectual work. This does not make them spellcasters or instructors in wizardry.

A Collegium archive or scholar might know that a manuscript exists without possessing it, understanding it, or being willing to share it. Great Houses patronize scholars and archives, but the Collegium remains institutionally distinct from a noble house's private wizard.

## 5. Patronage, wealth, and influence

Great Houses employ wizards because the combination of rare learning, magical ability, and access to specialized services is strategically valuable. Employment may provide a stipend, residence, materials, library access, assistants, protection, and status. In return, the patron expects service, discretion, loyalty as defined by contract or relationship, and often control over research and finished work.

Their commercial role is deliberately limited as well. A patron may commission selected magic items for sale or for favored external buyers, while controlling the volume and timing of production so the market is not flooded. Preserving scarcity protects the value, exclusivity, and strategic usefulness of finished items. This is not a single continent-wide conspiracy or licensing authority; each patron and maker acts according to their own interests.

Established wizards can be very wealthy and socially influential. Their wealth is not simply a reward for casting spells; it reflects scarcity, years of learning, patronage, commissions, and the value of specialist services. Their position is still dependent on relationships and political circumstances. A dismissed, independent, or itinerant practitioner may have far less security.

A wizard's knowledge may be a house asset. Research, spellbooks, notes, and commissioned items may be claimed by a patron under contract, inheritance, or household custom. The death, defection, or disappearance of a house wizard can therefore mean the loss of expertise that cannot quickly be replaced. Do not assume every house owns a wizard, every wizard is loyal, or every patron has equal access to arcane services.

## 6. Lost and restricted knowledge

Much arcane knowledge is lost, forgotten, fragmented, suppressed, or misunderstood. Ancient books and surviving items may preserve techniques that contemporary practitioners cannot reproduce. A modern wizard may recognize an effect without knowing how its creator achieved it.

The reason this knowledge became scarce is **unresolved**. Do not establish a single catastrophe, purge, war, collapse, or deliberate suppression as the definitive explanation without a separate GM decision. Different regions and scholars may hold competing theories.

## 7. Rules boundary

Social rarity does not weaken player-character spellcasting or alter Pathfinder First Edition class mechanics. NPC spellcasting must also have a plausible rules basis. A character who qualifies for a class, spell, feat, or crafting option retains that ability; the setting may affect access to teachers, texts, facilities, services, time, and buyers, but not silently rewrite the rules.
