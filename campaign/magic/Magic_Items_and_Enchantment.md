# Magic Items and Enchantment

**Status:** ESTABLISHED campaign lore  
**Visibility:** GM reference; reveal details to players through plausible character knowledge  
**Baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Ruleset:** Pathfinder First Edition

## 1. Foundational principle

Magic items are real, exceptionally scarce in ordinary commerce, and very expensive relative to the means of most people. They are not mass-produced goods, ordinary blacksmith stock, or equipment that every professional soldier can expect to own. A +1 sword or suit of +1 armor can exist in the world, but it is a notable possession rather than routine kit.

Scarcity comes from the small number of capable practitioners, the time and knowledge required, difficult access to suitable materials, and the fact that most recognized item-makers work for powerful patrons. The market is restricted by institutions and incentives, not by a single universal conspiracy.

## 2. Who makes magic items

Most recognized arcane item-makers are wizards or other arcane practitioners attached to Great Houses or similarly wealthy patrons. Their patron's needs come first. A house wizard may create items for the household, its officers, favored retainers, diplomatic gifts, or carefully selected external buyers.

Some independent practitioners accept commissions, but they are uncommon and may lack the resources, security, materials, or time available to a great-house wizard. Divine institutions may possess or commission sacred items, and other qualified characters may craft items if they meet the published Pathfinder requirements. The setting does not reserve item creation exclusively to wizards.

There is no continent-wide enchanters' guild, central licensing authority, or universal registry of magical items. Local laws, house contracts, temple rules, trade privileges, and political pressure may regulate particular transactions, but no uniform continental system is established.

## 3. Why the market stays small

- **Few qualified makers:** The relevant knowledge, talent, feats, spells, and experience are uncommon.
- **Time and opportunity cost:** A capable wizard's attention is divided between patron service, research, security, and craftsmanship.
- **Materials and facilities:** Suitable materials, tools, and working conditions can be difficult to source. This is a practical limitation, not a new universal ingredient requirement.
- **Patronage:** Great Houses generally prioritize their own needs and may restrict outside commissions or sales.
- **Strategic value:** An enchanted weapon or protective item can give its owner a meaningful advantage. Patrons may prefer to retain, gift, or tightly control such equipment.
- **Commercial self-interest:** A maker or patron has little reason to flood a small market and undermine the value or exclusivity of finished work.

These pressures produce limited supply and high prices without requiring every wizard to coordinate with every other wizard. Some makers may sell more freely; others may refuse outside work altogether.

## 4. What exists

The world uses the full range of Pathfinder First Edition magic-item categories where they fit the setting. Their existence in the rules does not mean they are routinely available for purchase.

- **Weapons and armor:** +1 weapons and armor are established as possible possessions. More powerful enhancement bonuses and special abilities become increasingly exceptional. Some pieces are inherited, commissioned, captured, or retained by noble households.
- **Wondrous and protective items:** Amulets, rings, cloaks, belts, boots, and other useful objects can exist, but a particular item must be established or discovered; do not assume a shop carries it.
- **Utility items:** Practical enchantments may be commissioned by wealthy households, temples, ships, or specialist trades. They remain uncommon outside those patrons.
- **Religious items and relics:** Sacred objects may be held by temples or religious orders. Their value may be devotional, historical, political, or magical. Not every holy symbol or relic is mechanically magical.
- **Ancient and exceptional items:** Some objects survive from periods when different knowledge or techniques were available. A modern wizard may be unable to reproduce them. Their history and powers should be established individually.

Do not create a universal rule that all magic items are ancient, unique, sentient, or religious. Ordinary Pathfinder items can exist; they are simply not common retail stock.

## 5. Buying, commissioning, and finding items

Characters may acquire magic items through several routes:

- **Purchase:** Usually requires a major city, a wealthy patron, a known specialist, or a personal introduction. The item must actually be available; a settlement's size does not guarantee stock.
- **Commission:** A qualified maker agrees to produce an item if they have the required abilities, time, facilities, materials, and willingness. Patronage and political circumstances may matter as much as money.
- **Inheritance or gift:** A noble, temple, family, or patron may pass on an item with obligations or expectations attached.
- **Discovery or recovery:** Ruins, old estates, battlefields, vaults, and defeated foes may hold items. Discovery should follow the location's established history and the GM's treasure decisions, not a general assumption that every ruin contains magic.
- **Reward or service:** A patron may grant an item as payment, recognition, or a strategic favor.
- **Illicit trade:** Stolen, misrepresented, or illegally exported items may circulate, but a black market is not assumed to exist in every town.

A character who owns a magic item may attract attention from collectors, nobles, rivals, thieves, religious authorities, or people who recognize its provenance. This is a possible consequence, not an automatic punishment for acquiring treasure.

## 6. Price and Pathfinder rules boundary

Use published Pathfinder First Edition item prices, crafting costs, feats, prerequisites, caster-level requirements, spell requirements, skill checks, and crafting times as the mechanical baseline. The setting's scarcity is expressed through actual availability, access to qualified makers, patronage, time, facilities, and the willingness of owners to sell.

Do not add a universal price multiplier, mandatory rare ingredient, extra crafting check, additional crafting time, or extra prerequisite unless the GM separately approves it as a house rule. The world can make an item difficult to find without changing its rules price. If the GM later chooses to alter market prices, record that as an explicit economic house rule and apply it consistently.

Player characters who meet published crafting requirements can craft items. Their abilities are not suppressed because the world contains few practitioners. A PC crafter may become an unusually important person and may face commercial, political, or security consequences as their reputation grows.

## 7. Economic and political consequences

- A Great House with several enchanted weapons has a limited but meaningful military advantage; it does not equip an entire army with magic.
- A house wizard's death, defection, or loss can reduce the house's access to magical services and item production.
- A magical item may be an heirloom with family history, not merely a line on an equipment sheet.
- A wealthy merchant may own a protective item without having the ability to identify, repair, or reproduce it.
- A noble may attempt to buy, confiscate, inherit, or claim an item based on law, custom, debt, or political leverage. Such a claim is a story and legal question, not automatic ownership.
- Rare materials may have value to craftspeople, collectors, scholars, or patrons, but their value must be established by a real use, buyer, provenance, or scarcity. A rare find is not automatically a magic-item ingredient.

## 8. Open details

The following remain unresolved and should be developed only when needed:

- The number and identity of wizards employed by each Great House.
- Named independent enchanters and the locations of their workshops.
- Specific materials, suppliers, and trade routes used by particular makers.
- Actual local availability and sale prices for individual items.
- Whether particular houses maintain private collections, item vaults, or written restrictions.
- The detailed history of ancient item-making traditions and lost techniques.
