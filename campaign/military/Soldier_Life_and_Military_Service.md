# Soldier Life and Military Service

**Snapshot:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Status:** GM reference; practical portrayal guidance, not a universal service code  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY  
**Related records:** [Continental Military Institutions and Operational Doctrine](Continental_Military_Institutions_and_Operational_Doctrine.md), [Law, Justice, and Crime](../world/Law_Justice_and_Crime.md), [Everyday Life and Material Culture](../world/Everyday_Life_and_Material_Culture.md), [Agriculture, Food, and Seasonal Work](../world/Agriculture_Food_and_Seasonal_Work.md)

This record describes the human realities behind the setting's armed institutions. It is about the people who stand watch, maintain walls, escort merchants, crew vessels, guard noble households, and campaign in the field. It does not establish new units, troop totals, pay scales, service terms, or current deployments.

The setting's military is not one standardized national army. A soldier's life depends on who commands them, what obligation or contract brought them into service, where they are stationed, and whether they are on routine duty or active campaign.

## 1. What “soldier” means

The word covers people with very different lives:

- **Household guards** protect a lord, family, residence, or immediate person. Their duties may include ceremonial attendance, escort, gate security, and personal protection.
- **Garrison soldiers** hold a castle, gate, wall, harbor fort, mountain pass, or strategic position. Their main task is to remain reliable at a fixed place.
- **Professional troops** serve continuously or for extended periods and develop specialized military skills. Their exact prevalence differs by house.
- **Vassal retainers** serve a noble household under a specific oath, charter, or customary obligation. Their service is not a universal quota.
- **Local levies** are called for a defined defensive or service need. Their training, equipment, duration, and legal status vary.
- **Town watch** personnel patrol streets, gates, markets, and quays. They are public-order officers, not interchangeable with field soldiers.
- **Naval personnel** include warship crews, marines, pilots, harbor defenders, and support workers. A merchant sailor is not automatically a naval combatant.
- **Specialists and auxiliaries** include engineers, scouts, guides, farriers, smiths, surgeons, teamsters, cooks, clerks, laborers, and animal handlers.

A person may move between roles over a lifetime. A former levy may become a household retainer; a sailor may be pressed into wartime service only where lawful authority permits; a veteran may become a gate sergeant, trainer, guard, or civilian craft worker.

## 2. Recruitment and the decision to serve

There is no single recruitment system across the continent. People enter service through a mixture of household obligation, feudal duty, contract, patronage, local custom, personal ambition, and economic necessity.

A noble household may favor people known to the family or recommended by trusted retainers. A professional force may seek experienced recruits or train younger people over time. A town watch may draw from local residents who know the streets and community. A levy is called under the relevant local obligation and should not be treated as a standing volunteer army.

Motives are mixed. Service can offer regular food, status, training, protection, wages or customary support, access to patrons, and a route away from rural poverty. It can also mean separation from family, injury, harsh discipline, dangerous travel, uncertain pay, and dependence on a commander.

Do not assume every recruit is poor, every noble-born soldier is competent, or every professional serves from patriotic conviction. People have individual reasons and limits.

## 3. Training and competence

Training reflects the role and resources of the institution.

A household guard may be expected to handle weapons, maintain formation, recognize household protocol, escort important people, and respond quickly within a residence or town. A garrison soldier must know routines, signals, gates, walls, stores, watch rotations, and the local ground. A field professional needs greater endurance and coordination. A levy may arrive with useful practical experience but uneven drill.

Common training subjects may include:

- safe handling and maintenance of weapons;
- shield use and basic formation discipline;
- marching, carrying equipment, and maintaining pace;
- sentry procedure, challenge, alarm, and relief;
- obedience to lawful command and recognition of rank;
- care of armor, clothing, boots, and equipment;
- camp hygiene, fire safety, and food discipline;
- local terrain, routes, landmarks, and signals.

Archery, cavalry, engineering, siegecraft, naval combat, and specialized weapons require more sustained instruction and equipment. Do not assume that a few days of drilling makes a levy equivalent to a trained professional.

A soldier's skill is not captured by weapon proficiency alone. Reliability, awareness, judgment, endurance, cohesion, and the ability to follow a plan under stress matter greatly.

## 4. A garrison day

Routine garrison life is repetitive by design. The work is to remain ready without exhausting the people or neglecting the place they defend.

A typical day may include a watch change, inspection of gates and equipment, cleaning and repair, drill or weapons practice, stores checks, patrols, messages, meals, and periods of rest. Some personnel are assigned to kitchens, stables, workshops, stores, or administrative tasks rather than standing on a wall.

Night duty is tiring. A sentry must remain attentive in cold, rain, darkness, wind, noise, and boredom. Poorly managed rotations produce mistakes. A familiar gate may feel safe until a fire, false alarm, drunken crowd, or unexpected arrival breaks the routine.

Garrisons are communities as well as military posts. Soldiers form friendships, rivalries, habits, and informal hierarchies. They may gamble, tell stories, mend clothing, write letters, visit nearby taverns when permitted, and argue about food or officers. They also share cramped quarters, illness, noise, and the consequences of one person's misconduct.

## 5. Food, clothing, shelter, and equipment

Military life is materially ordinary. People need meals, dry clothing, sleep, clean water, fuel, and repairs whether or not a battle is expected.

Food depends on the post and supply arrangements. A garrison may rely on issued stores, local purchase, household provisioning, or a combination. Grain, bread, porridge, beans, preserved meat or fish, cheese, onions, and seasonal produce are plausible staples, but no universal ration or menu is established. Officers and wealthy retainers may supplement ordinary provisions; poorer personnel may have fewer choices.

Clothing is shaped by climate, duty, and wealth. A working soldier needs durable layers, a cloak or weather covering, serviceable footwear, and clothing that can be repaired. Uniformity may be stronger in a household or professional unit than among a levy. Livery, badges, colors, and distinctive equipment identify service, but should not be assumed to be standardized across all forces.

Weapons and armor require constant maintenance. Rust, damp, loose straps, cracked leather, damaged mail, blunt edges, broken bowstrings, and poorly fitted equipment are practical problems. A wealthy house may equip retainers; another may expect them to provide some of their own kit. The exact arrangement must be established for the institution in question.

Horses, mules, and other working animals require feed, water, grooming, hoof care, shelter, and skilled handlers. A mounted soldier's ability to fight depends on the health of the mount and the support needed to keep it fit.

## 6. Pay, obligation, and household ties

The economic terms of service vary. A professional may receive wages or maintenance; a household retainer may receive food, lodging, equipment, gifts, and status; a vassal's service may be owed under a charter; a levy may serve for a limited customary period with local support. These arrangements are not interchangeable.

No universal pay rate, service term, pension, or compensation schedule is established. Do not invent one as a continent-wide rule. When an individual contract matters, record its terms in that person's or institution's dossier.

Military service affects households. A person away for weeks or months leaves work undone, dependents without help, and sometimes debts unpaid. Families may receive support, but that depends on the institution and agreement. A soldier's death or disabling injury can create a sudden loss of income and labor.

Desertion, refusal, or delayed service may be treated as a breach of obligation, but the consequences depend on the legal basis, circumstances, and authority involved. A person who cannot report because a bridge washed out is not in the same position as someone who deliberately abandons a post.

## 7. Discipline, rank, and command

Military discipline is necessary because one person's action can endanger others. Its form varies by institution and commander.

Orders may concern watch rotations, equipment, weapons, movement, formation, camp rules, access to stores, treatment of civilians, and conduct while on leave. A competent commander explains enough for the task to be carried out; a poor commander may rely on fear, favoritism, or arbitrary punishment.

Rank and authority should be recorded only where established. A senior retainer is not automatically a formal officer in every military system. A noble's social rank may influence command, but competence and delegated authority still matter. A Council representative or Great House heir does not automatically hold field command.

Discipline may involve reprimand, extra duty, loss of privilege, restitution, confinement, removal from service, or formal punishment under local law and custom. Do not assume one universal military code.

## 8. Campaigning and life on the road

A field force is a moving community with a large logistical footprint. Combat is only one part of its activity.

Before and during a march, people must arrange food, water, fodder, carts, pack animals, route knowledge, repairs, messages, medical care, and protection for supplies. The column may include noncombatants and specialists. Wagons, animals, and stores can slow movement and create vulnerabilities.

A soldier on campaign may spend more time marching, digging, carrying, guarding, repairing, waiting, and searching for supplies than fighting. Weather can soak clothing, ruin food, flood roads, make river crossings dangerous, and injure animals. A delayed supply train can create more immediate trouble than an enemy seen at a distance.

Foraging and requisition affect civilians. Buying supplies, taking them under lawful authority, and stealing them are different acts with different legal and political consequences. Even lawful requisition can damage relationships if compensation is delayed or local stores are inadequate.

Camp life requires latrines, waste disposal, fire control, water discipline, animal lines, food preparation, guard rotations, and a plan for wounded people. Poor sanitation can weaken a force without an enemy's intervention.

## 9. Wounds, illness, and veterans

Military service exposes people to cuts, burns, fractures, infected wounds, exposure, exhaustion, parasites, respiratory illness, and disease associated with crowded quarters. A soldier may survive a battle but be unable to return to the same work.

Care may be provided by comrades, household healers, practical surgeons, physicians, temple charities, or divine spellcasters where actually available. A military post is not automatically staffed by a magical healer. See the campaign's [Medicine, Disease, and Healing](../world/Medicine_Disease_and_Healing.md) reference.

Veterans do not all share one response to service. Some are proud, some exhausted, some bitter, some quietly content, and some eager to return to civilian work. Experience may bring authority and useful knowledge, but it does not guarantee wealth, respect, or employment.

A veteran might become a trainer, guard, caravan escort, bailiff, craft worker, innkeeper, or tenant farmer. Others may live with chronic pain, disability, debt, or social isolation. Avoid treating every veteran as either a celebrated hero or a broken man.

## 10. Women, ancestry, and service

The setting includes women who rule, inherit, command, trade, study, and administer. Military participation should be determined by the relevant culture, institution, role, and individual history rather than by a universal assumption that women cannot serve. This does not establish equal recruitment or identical custom in every territory.

The same principle applies to ancestry. Elves, dwarves, humans, and other peoples may have different traditions of service, training, lifespan, and household obligation. Do not assume that all members of an ancestry share one military role or temperament. Ratfolk participation in surface military institutions may be constrained by prejudice and access, but no universal ban or formal ratfolk military institution is established.

## 11. Distinguishing the institutions in play

When portraying armed personnel, begin with their actual job:

- A **watch officer** is concerned with patrols, disturbances, warrants, public order, and local knowledge.
- A **gate guard** is concerned with access, traffic, inspection, tolls, and security procedures.
- A **garrison soldier** is concerned with the safety and readiness of a fixed position.
- A **household guard** is concerned with the person, family, residence, and interests they are sworn to protect.
- A **field soldier** is concerned with movement, formation, orders, supply, and the immediate military objective.
- A **sailor or marine** works within the demands of vessel, crew, weather, navigation, and shipboard command.
- An **engineer or specialist** may be indispensable without being a front-line fighter.

Their loyalties may overlap. A watch officer may be a local resident; a garrison soldier may owe service to a Great House; a household guard may have family in the town they police. Conflicting loyalties create human decisions, not automatic betrayal.

## 12. Open decisions and limits

The following remain deliberately unresolved:

- exact pay, ration, leave, pension, and compensation systems;
- universal or regional service terms;
- standard ranks, insignia, uniforms, and drill regulations;
- formal recruitment ages and minimum service periods;
- the availability of military surgeons and healers at particular posts;
- the prevalence of women in specific units and regional traditions;
- exact treatment of desertion, refusal, and misconduct;
- the social and legal status of camp followers and military dependents;
- the detailed daily routines of named garrisons and ships.

Develop these details when a character, location, or military event makes them relevant. The purpose is to make soldiers feel like people living within institutions, not to create a second rules system.
