# Feudal Law, Land Tenure, and Noble Obligations

**Status:** Initial campaign-wide legal framework established for play; detailed regional law and charter texts remain open.  
**Authority:** GM-approved worldbuilding framework, consistent with the existing Crown, Great House, minor-house, and Tallowmere records.  
**Purpose:** Give the GM a consistent reference for inheritance, landholding, vassalage, jurisdiction, and political consequences without pretending every territory follows one modern legal code.

## 1. Governing principle

The continent is a patchwork of hereditary lordships, charters, local customs, city privileges, clan agreements, and Crown authority. Law is real and consequential, but its reach depends on jurisdiction, written instruments, recognized custom, and the power to enforce a judgment.

There is no single uniform civil code that automatically overrides every local charter or custom. The Crown's authority is substantial, but Great House privileges and the post-Rebellion settlement constrain its practical reach.

## 2. Political authority and landholding

The following distinctions must be preserved:

- **Crown authority:** The monarch and Crown officers exercise royal jurisdiction and administration within the Crown's established rights. The Crown does not automatically administer every Great House territory directly.
- **Great House territory:** Each Great House controls a substantial territory under its own hereditary authority, subject to the post-Rebellion settlement, the Imperial Council, treaties, and any specific Crown rights established in canon.
- **Banner house:** A lesser noble house holds land or rights under a Great House and owes obligations defined by its charter, grant, homage, or accepted custom.
- **Crown vassal:** A lesser noble house owes its principal fealty directly to the Crown. It is not automatically subordinate to a nearby Great House.
- **Independent lord:** A lesser ruler or house outside direct Great House or Crown vassalage. Independence does not mean immunity from treaties, trade pressure, or stronger neighbors.
- **Charter holder:** A person, house, guild, clan, or community may hold a specific right such as a crossing, market, harbor, mine, or fishing privilege without owning all surrounding land.
- **Freeholder or customary community:** A community may hold recognized local rights without being a noble house or sovereign territory.

A liege relationship, strategic interest, charter right, and territorial claim are different legal facts. Never collapse them into one map polygon.

## 3. Homage and obligations

A vassal relationship may include some combination of:
- Acknowledgment of the liege's superior authority.
- Military service or provision of armed retainers when lawfully called.
- Local levy or defensive support.
- Tribute, rent, tax, or customary dues.
- Provision of food, transport, ships, animals, labor, or supplies.
- Attendance at court, council, or arbitration.
- Maintenance of roads, bridges, watch posts, fortifications, or other specified works.
- Respect for the liege's reserved rights and lawful jurisdiction.

**No universal quantities or service periods are established.** The actual obligation must come from a known charter, grant, treaty, custom, or explicit GM decision. A request for aid is not automatically a lawful summons; a lawful summons is not automatically unlimited.

A liege may have duties in return, including protection, recognition of tenure, access to justice, confirmation of lawful succession, and respect for the vassal's reserved rights. The scope varies by relationship.

## 4. Inheritance and succession

The setting has recognized female rulers and designated female heirs, including within the Great Houses. Therefore, a universal rule that excludes women from inheritance is not compatible with current canon.

For GM adjudication, use the following baseline unless a specific house charter or regional custom says otherwise:

1. A recognized heir has the strongest presumptive claim.
2. The house's established succession custom, charter, and prior recognition determine who is eligible.
3. Gender alone does not disqualify an heir.
4. The liege may be required to acknowledge or confirm succession where the holding is held under a grant or charter.
5. Recognition by the liege does not erase a legitimate rival claim, and possession does not automatically prove lawful succession.
6. Marriage may create alliances, claims, or expectations, but does not automatically transfer a holding or title.
7. A disputed succession becomes a political issue when a credible claimant, legal instrument, influential supporters, or practical challenge exists.

**Not established:** A universal order among children, rules for adopted heirs, legitimacy standards, treatment of collateral branches, the role of widowhood or regency, and the exact procedures for challenging an heir. These should be resolved for a specific house when the issue becomes relevant rather than by inventing a continent-wide statute.

## 5. Land tenure and customary rights

Landholding may include:
- Direct noble demesne.
- Tenant farms and rents.
- Hereditary or life-tenure holdings.
- Grazing, woodland, fishing, water, and quarry rights.
- Shared commons and seasonal use.
- Chartered commercial rights.
- Religious or institutional holdings.
- Clan-held subterranean or communal property.

A noble's territorial authority does not automatically extinguish tenant, freeholder, guild, clan, or customary rights. A house may own the land while others possess recognized rights to use it.

A boundary dispute may concern:
- Sovereignty or jurisdiction.
- A tenant's holding.
- A shared pasture or woodland.
- Water access or maintenance.
- Fishing grounds.
- A road, bridge, ford, toll, or ferry.
- Quarry, mine, or other extraction rights.

These are not interchangeable. A dispute over access or customary use does not automatically become a dispute over the political border.

## 6. Courts and jurisdiction

Use the following working hierarchy:

1. **Local authority:** Reeve, steward, charter officer, guild court, or community mediator handles ordinary matters within delegated authority.
2. **Lordship court:** A local lord or authorized official hears matters involving tenants, rents, local offenses, estate boundaries, and the lord's own rights.
3. **Liege arbitration or court:** A superior lord may hear appeals, charter disputes, vassal obligations, and conflicts between subordinate houses.
4. **Crown jurisdiction:** Royal officers and courts address matters within established Crown authority, royal officers, Crown vassals, and disputes properly brought to the Crown.
5. **Imperial Council:** The six-seat Council is a forum for major inter-house disputes, extraordinary taxation, major military levies and wars, significant treaties, royal succession recognition, relations with Kharadorn, and laws affecting Great House privileges. It is not a routine appeal court for every village boundary.

Exact appeal rights, criminal jurisdiction, evidentiary rules, trial procedure, and the boundary between Crown, noble, and clerical courts remain open.

## 7. Justice, punishment, and enforcement

Local authorities may rely on fines, restitution, customary penalties, guild sanctions, loss of privileges, detention, or referral to a superior court. The available punishment and process depend on the offense, local custom, and the authority hearing it.

A lord's judgment is only as effective as the officials and force available to enforce it. A legally correct ruling may be resisted, delayed, or ignored where authority is weak. Conversely, a powerful actor may impose an outcome without making it lawful.

Do not assume:
- Every noble has unrestricted power of life and death.
- Every dispute is settled by combat.
- Every accusation is resolved through a formal trial.
- A liege can seize any vassal holding without process or political cost.
- A local watch is equivalent to a standing army.

## 8. Marriage, wardship, and hostages

Marriage can create dynastic alliances, inheritance claims, dowry obligations, and political expectations. Its legal effects depend on the relevant house customs and agreements.

Wardship may serve as education, alliance, protection, or political leverage. Hostages are a possible instrument of treaty or submission, but are not an automatic component of every vassal relationship.

No current marriage compact, wardship arrangement, or hostage exchange is created by this framework.

## 9. Kharadorn and clan law

The dwarven empire consists of independent clans united under the main branch. Clan autonomy and internal agreements are important features of its political order.

- The High King and main branch hold the established imperial authority.
- Individual clans retain internal governance and customary rights.
- Clan obligations to the imperial union exist in principle, but individual tribute, military, labor, and resource obligations are not yet quantified.
- Surface charters such as Tallowmere's are distinct from internal clan governance.
- The Crown does not automatically exercise ordinary jurisdiction over Kharadorn clan affairs.

Do not treat a dwarven clan as a human-style banner house unless the specific relationship supports that description.

## 10. Independent cities and southern powers

Independent cities and southern lordships may be governed by hereditary rulers, councils, merchant compacts, charters, or combinations of these. Their independence is practical and legal, not absolute: they remain exposed to trade pressure, treaties, raids, debt, and stronger neighbors.

A city council's authority comes from its charter and local acceptance. Do not assume a modern democratic system, universal suffrage, or a single standard for civic representation.

## 11. GM adjudication: changes in landholding

When a player character or NPC acquires, loses, inherits, conquers, or is granted a holding, consider the legal and practical circumstances:

- The legal basis: inheritance, grant, purchase, marriage settlement, conquest, election, charter, or custom.
- The prior holder and any heirs or claimants.
- The authority required to recognize the change.
- The obligations and reserved rights that remain.
- The tenants, freeholders, guilds, clans, and communities affected.
- The difference between lawful title and effective control.
- The actors who benefit, lose, resist, or remain neutral.
- The evidence available to each actor.

A holding can change hands without every tenant changing allegiance. A title can be recognized while local officials refuse cooperation. A conqueror can control a castle while facing a legal, economic, or military struggle to govern its lands. The GM determines the actual response from the specific rights, evidence, people, and circumstances involved.

## 12. Open decisions for future play

These questions are deliberately left open until they matter:
- Exact inheritance customs by region and house.
- Crown and noble court appeal rights.
- The legal status of bastardy, adoption, and disputed marriages.
- Whether and when a liege may lawfully revoke a fief.
- The standard service obligations of banner houses.
- The legal and customary limits on noble criminal jurisdiction.
- The rights of religious courts and temple lands.
- The balance between clan autonomy and imperial authority in Kharadorn.
- The charters and constitutional customs of independent southern cities.

## 13. Campaign-start rule

This framework is available to the GM at the opening of the campaign on **1 Harvestfall, 243 AC**. It does not create a new legal crisis or change any existing title. Specific cases are resolved from their own charters, evidence, local custom, and the actors involved.
