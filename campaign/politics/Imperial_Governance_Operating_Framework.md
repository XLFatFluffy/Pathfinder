# Imperial Governance Operating Framework

**Status:** GM-approved worldbuilding expansion  
**Effective date:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Scope:** Imperial Council procedure, Crown authority, Great House representation, and political escalation rules

This document records the established Crown/Council framework as a usable GM reference. It does not turn the Imperial Council into a modern legislature.

## 1. The Imperial Council

The Imperial Council has six permanent seats, one representing each Great House. The monarch presides and may speak, propose, negotiate, and veto Council resolutions, but does not hold a seventh Great House vote.

The Council exists to preserve the post-Rebellion settlement, negotiate disputes among the Great Houses, advise the monarch, and provide a formal mechanism through which the Great Houses can resist or support major royal actions.

### Ordinary business

An ordinary Council resolution requires **four of the six Great House representatives**.

Ordinary resolutions include:

- recommendations to the Crown;
- administrative agreements among Great Houses;
- allocation of jointly controlled resources;
- recognition of customary inter-house arrangements;
- recommendations concerning trade, roads, and shared infrastructure;
- formal requests for royal investigation or arbitration.

An ordinary resolution is advisory unless the subject falls within the protected settlement matters below.

### Protected settlement matters

The Crown must obtain Council assent before:

- permanently altering a Great House's established hereditary privileges;
- imposing a new extraordinary realm-wide levy on the Great Houses;
- permanently expanding royal military obligations imposed on the Great Houses beyond established custom;
- transferring a Great House territory or hereditary right without that house's consent or a legally recognized forfeiture;
- abolishing or fundamentally altering the Imperial Council itself;
- changing the succession settlement governing the Imperial Seat.

A protected settlement measure requires **five of six Council votes**.

The monarch may refuse a protected measure, but doing so creates a constitutional crisis rather than silently nullifying the Council.

### War and emergency

The monarch retains authority to order immediate defensive action when the realm is under direct attack.

A prolonged offensive war against another recognized major power requires either:

- Council assent by four of six representatives, or
- a demonstrable immediate threat to the realm that makes delay unreasonable.

The Crown may act first in an emergency, but must present the action to the Council afterward.

The Council cannot command armies directly. It can constrain the political legitimacy, funding, and noble cooperation available to a royal war.

## 2. Council procedure

- The Council normally meets in Valedorn.
- The monarch or Royal Chancellor may call an extraordinary session.
- Any two Council representatives may jointly request a session on a matter affecting their houses.
- The Lord Chancellor prepares the agenda after consultation with the monarch and representatives.
- A representative may demand that a matter be entered into the record.
- Each representative has one vote.
- Proxy voting is permitted only through a written appointment from the same Great House and only for a specific session.
- A representative may abstain, but an abstention does not count as a vote against.
- A quorum requires **four representatives**.
- If fewer than four representatives attend, the Council may negotiate and record recommendations but cannot pass a binding Council resolution.
- The monarch may postpone a vote once for further negotiation; a second postponement requires agreement from at least three representatives.
- Council records are maintained by the Royal Chancery. Sensitive matters may be sealed by royal order, but sealed records remain records rather than being destroyed.

### Deadlock

When the Council cannot reach the required majority:

1. The Lord Chancellor attempts mediation.
2. The monarch may offer a compromise.
3. A representative may propose a revised motion.
4. If no agreement is reached, the issue remains unresolved unless the Crown has an independent legal authority to act.

Deadlock is a political outcome, not a failure of the system.

## 3. Appointment and removal of representatives

Each Great House chooses its own Council representative.

The house ruler may:

- appoint the representative;
- recall the representative;
- replace the representative;
- instruct the representative on major house policy.

The monarch cannot remove a Great House representative.

A representative who openly defies the house ruler may be recalled, but the ruler must accept the political consequences of replacing an experienced negotiator.

A Council representative does not automatically inherit the Great House or become its ruler.

## 4. Crown authority

The monarch retains direct authority over:

- Crown officers;
- royal household administration;
- royal correspondence;
- Crown lands;
- royal appointments where law permits;
- the royal courts within their established jurisdiction;
- immediate defense of the realm;
- diplomacy conducted under the Crown's authority.

The monarch does **not** automatically possess direct administrative authority over Great House lands merely because the Crown is sovereign.

The Great Houses retain hereditary rights, local courts, customary obligations, and internal administration subject to established law and the post-Rebellion settlement.

## 5. Crown authority versus Great House authority

The following distinctions are now operational:

| Matter | Crown | Great House |
|---|---|---|
| Imperial diplomacy | Primary | Consulted where interests are directly affected |
| Crown taxes | Primary within lawful authority | Local collection where granted |
| Great House internal succession | Limited legal oversight | Primary authority under house custom and law |
| Local courts | Crown courts for Crown jurisdiction | Noble courts within established jurisdiction |
| Great House land administration | No automatic direct control | Primary |
| Royal army | Crown command | Houses retain their own forces |
| Great House levies | Governed by law/custom | House controls its own levy within obligation |
| Realm-wide emergency defense | Crown coordinates | Great Houses owe established obligations |
| Trade regulation | Crown can regulate realm-wide matters | Houses regulate local markets within law |
| Roads crossing multiple jurisdictions | Crown may coordinate | Each authority maintains its own section unless otherwise agreed |

## 6. Great House political operating model

Each Great House should be treated as an internal political network rather than a single actor.

Every house has:

- a ruler;
- a recognized heir;
- immediate family;
- collateral family branches;
- household officers;
- military leadership;
- landed vassals;
- economic dependents;
- religious relationships;
- internal factions;
- long-term strategic objectives.

The ruler is the primary decision-maker, but the GM should distinguish the ruler's wishes from the interests of the wider house.

A ruler may be pressured by:

- an heir;
- a sibling;
- a spouse;
- a military commander;
- a wealthy vassal;
- merchants;
- religious authorities;
- council representatives;
- local customary interests.

This does not mean every house has an active internal crisis. Internal disagreement should be introduced only when established or when world events create a reason.

## 7. Great House decision hierarchy

When a Great House responds to an event, evaluate:

1. Immediate survival and security.
2. Protection of hereditary authority.
3. Protection of the recognized heir and succession.
4. Fulfillment of existing feudal obligations.
5. Preservation of major resources.
6. Maintenance of alliances and useful relationships.
7. Public reputation and legitimacy.
8. Long-term strategic goals.
9. Personal ambitions of important family members.

The house should not automatically choose the most dramatic option.

## 8. Political escalation ladder

Political disputes should normally escalate through:

**private complaint → formal petition → arbitration → sanctions or withholding obligations → local legal action → limited coercion → armed retainers → Great House intervention → Crown intervention → open war**

Skipping several stages requires a specific cause.

These are possible avenues, not a mandatory sequence. The GM should distinguish a private complaint, legal petition, arbitration, coercion, and open warfare rather than treating them all as the same kind of conflict.

## 9. Consequences for vassals

A vassal who fails an obligation may face:

- warning;
- demand for performance;
- arbitration;
- fines or compensation;
- loss of a specific privilege;
- temporary administrative intervention;
- reassignment of a charter;
- forfeiture proceedings;
- military coercion in extreme cases.

Forfeiture is exceptional. A noble house does not lose its hereditary holding merely because a player or NPC dislikes it.

## 10. Information and political knowledge

Council representatives know what their houses have officially communicated to them.

They do not automatically know:

- private plans of other houses;
- GM-only secrets;
- rumors that have not reached them;
- the contents of sealed records;
- every action of their own vassals.

The GM must keep information available to each representative separate from objective truth.

## 11. Current constitutional balance

The current political equilibrium is intentionally unstable but functional:

- King Aldric Calderan has substantial formal authority.
- The Great Houses retain substantial hereditary and territorial power.
- The Council gives the Great Houses a formal mechanism for collective resistance.
- No single Great House controls the monarch.
- Kharadorn does not control the Crown.
- The Crown does not control the Great Houses.
- Merchant interests can influence policy through finance and trade without possessing sovereign authority.
- Religious institutions possess influence and legitimacy but no automatic Council seat.
- The royal army is capable of enforcing Crown authority locally but is not strong enough to casually defeat all six Great Houses acting together.

This balance is the baseline against which future political changes are measured.

## 12. GM adjudication guidance

When a player character or NPC changes a political fact, begin with the legal mechanism and the people whose rights are affected. Inheritance, royal grants, vassalage, marriage, criminal exposure, arbitration, charter breach, seizure of a fort, and recognition of an heir create different rights and obligations. Consequences should follow the specific mechanism, evidence, jurisdiction, and political circumstances rather than being reduced to a generic reputation score.
