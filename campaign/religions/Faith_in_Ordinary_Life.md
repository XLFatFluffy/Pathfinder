# Faith in Ordinary Life

**Status:** GM-approved worldbuilding expansion  
**Baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC

This record describes how religion is encountered by ordinary people. It does not define a single universal practice. The gods are real; mortal doctrines, local customs, institutional interests, and individual interpretations vary.

## 1. Religion is more than temple attendance

Most people encounter religion through recurring moments rather than formal theology.

These may include:

- a short prayer before work;
- a blessing before a journey;
- a household shrine or religious object;
- a funeral or memorial;
- a seasonal ceremony;
- a vow made during danger;
- a charitable meal;
- a temple bell or public procession;
- a priest asked to witness a marriage or contract.

A person may be religious without being learned, devout without being doctrinally strict, or respectful of several gods without belonging to a formal institution.

## 2. Household practice

Household religion varies by region, ancestry, and means.

A modest household may keep a small image, token, lamp, or written prayer. Wealthier households may maintain a dedicated room, employ a chaplain, or make regular donations to a temple.

Household observance is often practical. People pray for safe childbirth, a good harvest, recovery from illness, a successful voyage, protection from fire, a fair judgment, or peace for the dead.

This does not mean worshippers believe every misfortune is a punishment from a god.

## 3. Temples and their work

A temple is a religious institution and often a local social institution.

Depending on its resources and tradition, it may provide:

- public worship;
- instruction;
- funerary services;
- marriage rites;
- charity;
- shelter;
- care for the sick;
- record keeping;
- mediation;
- food distribution;
- festivals;
- care for sacred property.

Not every temple can provide every service. A small shrine may have one caretaker and occasional visiting clergy. A major temple may maintain staff, archives, kitchens, schools, and charitable funds.

## 4. Clergy and spellcasters

Clergy are not synonymous with divine spellcasters.

A priest may be trained in doctrine, ritual, pastoral care, history, administration, or law without possessing spellcasting ability.

A divine spellcaster is uncommon and may attract attention, but magical ability does not automatically make that person the temple's leader. Seniority, learning, trust, institutional appointment, and political relationships can matter more.

A spellcaster may also be distrusted if their conduct contradicts the values they publicly represent.

## 5. Common rites of passage

### Birth and naming
Families may ask for a blessing, choose a name with religious significance, or make a dedication to a particular deity. Customs differ and are not mandatory everywhere.

### Coming of age
Some communities hold a formal rite; others treat adulthood as a legal or household transition. Religious instruction may accompany either.

### Marriage
A priest may witness or bless a marriage, but marriage law remains a matter of local custom and secular jurisdiction as well as religion.

### Death
Funerary rites are among the most consistent religious services. Pharasma is widely respected in matters of death, but local traditions and family preference shape the ceremony.

## 6. Oaths and promises

Religious oaths carry weight because they invoke a sacred witness and public reputation.

Not every promise is sworn before a god. Excessive oath-taking may be regarded as theatrical or suspicious.

Breaking an oath can create religious, legal, and social consequences, but the exact consequences depend on the institution, jurisdiction, and nature of the promise.

Do not assume every broken oath causes supernatural punishment.

## 7. Charity and obligation

Religious charity may include food, shelter, care for travelers, burial assistance, alms, education, and help during disaster.

Charity can be sincere and still create institutional influence. Temples that feed a district may gain public trust. A wealthy patron who funds a hospital may also gain prestige.

Disputes arise over who qualifies for aid, whether assistance should carry conditions, and whether a temple should help people condemned by secular authorities.

## 8. Religious education

Most people learn basic religious customs through family and community.

Formal theological education is more limited and may be offered by temples, monasteries, tutors, or scholarly institutions.

A person can know a familiar prayer without understanding a deity's doctrine in detail. A merchant may know Abadaran contract customs but little theology. A sailor may know Gozreh rites and Desnan travel prayers without being able to explain their historical origins.

## 9. Pilgrimage and travel

Pilgrimage may be undertaken for devotion, penance, gratitude, healing, study, or commemoration.

Travelers may visit major temples, shrines, memorials, or sites associated with a deity's history.

Pilgrimage is not equally accessible. Wealth, safety, distance, family obligations, and political conditions determine who can travel.

## 10. Religious disagreement

Disagreement may concern doctrine, ritual, property, authority, interpretation, or political involvement.

Two sincere worshippers of the same god may disagree sharply without either being a fraud.

Accusations of heresy can be sincere, politically motivated, or both. The GM should establish the facts of a particular dispute rather than treating every accusation as true.

## 11. The gods in public life

The existence of the gods is established campaign truth. Their intentions are not always clear to mortals.

A public event may be interpreted as divine favor, coincidence, providence, or political theater. The fact that a god exists does not mean every priest correctly understands that god's will.

Divine magic is real, but its mechanism remains unresolved. Do not turn that mystery into a universal explanation for every unusual event.

## 12. GM guidance

When portraying a religious person or institution, consider:

- what the person actually believes;
- how they learned it;
- what religious duties they perform;
- what their community expects;
- what the institution needs;
- what the person doubts;
- how faith interacts with livelihood, family, and politics.

Avoid portraying ordinary believers as either ignorant fanatics or perfect moral exemplars.

**Core idea:** Faith is woven into ordinary life, but religious authority is exercised by people and institutions with their own limits, interests, and disagreements.
