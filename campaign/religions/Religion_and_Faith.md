# Religion, Faith, and Religious Institutions

**Status:** ESTABLISHED — Stage 7 approved 2026-11-04  
**Ruleset:** Pathfinder First Edition

## 1. The Gods and the Pantheon

The Pathfinder gods are real within the setting. Their existence is certain, not an in-world mystery. The established Pathfinder pantheon, portfolios, alignments, domains, and divine traditions provide the foundation for religion.

The continent broadly recognizes the Pathfinder pantheon, but worship varies by region, culture, community, institution, and individual. No single god is established as universally dominant across the continent. Different communities may emphasize particular deities, and individuals may pray to different gods for different needs rather than maintaining exclusive devotion to one deity.

The gods' deeper history, relationships with mortals, methods of influence, and the precise ways divine power is granted or mediated remain unresolved. Mortal interpretations, scriptures, doctrines, and claims about divine signs may conflict even though the gods themselves are real.

## 2. Worship and Religious Life

Religious practice is part of ordinary society, but the presence of a temple or priest does not imply that supernatural magic is routinely available.

A priest may lead ceremonies, teach doctrine, maintain a shrine, counsel a community, conduct funerals or marriages, and preserve religious traditions without possessing divine spellcasting ability. Dedicated clergy and divine spellcasters are not interchangeable categories. Not every temple worker is a spellcaster, and religious authority does not automatically follow magical ability.

Local practice varies. A major city may support multiple temples and religious communities; a remote settlement may have only a modest shrine, a caretaker, or occasional visits from a traveling priest. Ratfolk communities and other distinct societies may maintain their own varied religious customs and local authorities.

## 3. Religious Institutions

There is no single supreme religious authority governing all faiths across the continent. Major faiths maintain their own hierarchies, institutions, traditions, and internal disputes. Their influence may extend across political borders, but their organization and authority are not uniform.

Common institutional forms include:

- **Temples:** Permanent centers of worship, ceremony, community service, teaching, and religious authority.
- **Monasteries:** Communities devoted to study, discipline, contemplation, service, or a particular religious tradition.
- **Shrines:** Smaller local places of worship maintained by communities, caretakers, or traveling clergy.
- **Cathedrals:** Major religious centers with regional or wider influence.
- **Religious orders:** Organizations devoted to a deity, doctrine, or mission, which may be charitable, scholarly, martial, or contemplative.
- **Clerical courts:** Religious authorities that hear internal disputes and matters of religious law, custom, or discipline.

These forms need not exist in every region or for every faith. Their powers and internal structures vary.

Religious institutions may own land, receive donations, provide charity and education, preserve records, influence ceremonies, advise rulers, and shape public expectations. They may condemn rulers or policies, declare individuals or teachings heretical, and compete with rival institutions. They can wield substantial social and political influence, but they do not universally govern secular territories, command Great House armies, or automatically overrule secular law.

## 4. The Religious Establishment and the Crown

The **Religious Establishment** is an established court faction or network seeking to preserve and expand the authority and influence of organized religion. It is gaining influence in Valedorn's court.

Senior religious authorities have recognized access to the royal court and may advise the monarch. The Religious Establishment does not hold an automatic seat on the Imperial Council: the Council's six permanent seats represent the six Great Houses. Religious authorities instead exert influence through relationships, advice, public pressure, negotiation, patronage, and alliances with members of the Crown and Great Houses.

The Religious Establishment is not a single unified church. Its constituent faiths and institutions can disagree, compete, and pursue conflicting interests. Religious leaders may cooperate with secular rulers when useful while resisting attempts to control their institutions.

Religious influence may touch royal legitimacy, coronations, marriage, funerals, inheritance customs, education, charity, and public morality. The exact legal force of religious rulings and the degree to which particular faiths influence secular law remain open.

## 5. Learned Orders and Scholarship

A scholarly tradition exists that is institutionally distinct from the religious hierarchies. It is not simply a priesthood or a body of divine spellcasters, though it may cooperate with temples and religious authorities.

Its scholars may study and preserve history, genealogy, mathematics, accounting, medicine, anatomy, astronomy, calendars, law, administration, languages, translation, natural philosophy, theology, and religious history.

Scholars may serve noble households, royal offices, major temples, and wealthy cities. Some may also be ordained clergy, but scholarship does not require ordination, and theologians need not be spellcasters.

The independent scholarly tradition is known as the Collegium of the Nine Lamps, a loose network rather than a single university. Its internal ranks, training system, governing body, geographic reach, and formal relationship with the Crown remain unresolved.

## 6. Divine Spellcasters

Divine spellcasters are uncommon and devote themselves to a god. The average person may live an entire life without witnessing a spell being cast.

Divine spellcasting is regarded as significant rather than routine. Those who demonstrate it may be respected as blessed servants, but reactions can also include fear, suspicion, or distrust. A village priest may serve for decades without casting a spell; a cleric capable of extraordinary healing or other powerful magic may attract considerable attention.

Major temples may have only a small number of divine spellcasters. Noble houses and powerful institutions may seek their services. However, magical ability does not automatically confer senior religious office, and a religious leader may possess substantial authority without being able to cast spells.

These social expectations do not alter Pathfinder First Edition class mechanics or arbitrarily weaken player-character spellcasting.

## 7. Temple Champions and the Defense of the Faith

Every established, permanent temple maintains or designates a dedicated defender, commonly called its Temple Champion or, in ordinary speech, the temple's paladin. This is a religious office and responsibility, not a promise that every Champion uses the Pathfinder paladin class. The Champion's actual class and abilities must obey Pathfinder First Edition requirements, including alignment and deity restrictions; depending on the faith and individual, an appropriate inquisitor, warpriest, fighter, ranger, or other suitable character may hold the office where a paladin cannot.

The Champion's primary duties are to defend the temple and its grounds, protect clergy and worshippers under its care, safeguard sacred property and relics, and investigate or confront threats that fall within the deity's concerns. A report of an undead presence near a Pharasma temple, for example, may prompt the clergy to assess the report and send the Champion to investigate. The Champion does not treat every rumor as proof or automatically assume jurisdiction over every local problem.

Temple clergy primarily maintain the institution and serve its congregation through worship, rites, counsel, charity, records, education, and care. Clerics may be present, but they are not automatically the temple's defenders and are not expected to abandon their ordinary duties for routine patrols. They may accompany or aid the Champion when danger is exceptional or when their particular abilities are necessary. A temple's senior priest and its Champion are separate offices unless a specific individual is established to hold both.

The office is not a standing army and does not grant general authority over secular law, noble forces, or neighboring communities. A Champion may request aid, cooperate with local authorities, hire assistance, or call upon the relevant religious order or hierarchy. Because the faiths have independent structures and no single supreme religious authority, responses beyond one temple depend on the relevant institution, regional relationships, and the scale of the threat.

A larger religious mobilization or crusade is exceptional. It requires a serious threat, appropriate religious authorization, resources, and often negotiation with secular powers. A Temple Champion cannot unilaterally declare a continent-wide holy war. Smaller temples may share a Champion or rely on a nearby institution; modest shrines and caretaker sites are not automatically expected to maintain a resident defender.

## 8. Faith and the Great Houses

The Great Houses do not each have a single exclusive patron deity or uniform religious identity. Their members may hold differing beliefs, and religious practices vary within each house.

The following are broad cultural tendencies for future development, not exclusive doctrines:

- **Vaelorian:** Old traditions, temple patronage, scholarship, and carefully managed relationships with religious authorities.
- **Kharadorn:** Strong Torag traditions and dwarven ancestral customs; religious authority may be closely connected to clan identity, oaths, and lineage. The Kharadorn claim of descent from Torag remains a dynastic tradition, not confirmed literal divine ancestry.
- **Edarth:** Traditional worship, public ceremonies, oaths, funerals, and the language of duty.
- **Calderan:** Religious legitimacy matters to the monarchy; the Crown cultivates relationships with influential temples and clergy.
- **Veyr:** Practical faith shaped by hardship, duty, death, endurance, and dangers along the frontier.
- **Merrowen:** Religious customs shaped by travel, sailors, trade, foreign contact, and maritime dangers.

## 9. Religious Tensions

Religious conflict may arise from several established fault lines:

1. **Religious and secular authority:** Institutions seek independence and influence, while rulers seek legitimacy and control.
2. **Competing interpretations:** The gods are real, but mortals can disagree about doctrine, scripture, divine signs, and what a deity demands.
3. **Wealth and property:** Temples, orders, and religious authorities may control land, donations, and valuable resources.
4. **Heresy and forbidden teachings:** Accusations may reflect genuine doctrinal conflict, political rivalry, institutional self-preservation, or some combination.
5. **Divine power:** The uncommon nature of divine spellcasting raises questions about devotion, selection, authority, and the limits of mortal understanding.

Religious zealots and fanatics remain prominent in the setting, but religious life is not uniformly fanatical, corrupt, or conspiratorial. Faith may be sincere, compassionate, political, coercive, contradictory, or deeply personal.
