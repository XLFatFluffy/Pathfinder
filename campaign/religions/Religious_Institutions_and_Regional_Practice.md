# Religious Institutions and Regional Practice

**Status:** GM-approved worldbuilding expansion  
**Baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC

The following institutions are established as examples of major religious presence. They do not form a single continent-wide church.

## 1. Valedorn — Temple of the Ledger

**Primary faith:** Abadar  
**Institution:** Temple and civic legal house  
**Role:** Contracts, accounting, public records, merchant arbitration, and charity.

The Temple of the Ledger is influential among merchants, magistrates, administrators, and guild officials.

Its priests are frequently consulted on contracts and commercial disputes, but its rulings do not automatically override secular courts.

## 2. Valedorn — Shrine of the Last Passage

**Primary faith:** Pharasma  
**Role:** Funerals, burial records, care for the dying, and preservation of genealogical records.

The shrine maintains records that are useful to both families and Crown officials.

Its priests are respected for their knowledge of death and inheritance customs.

## 3. Khar-Dum — Hall of the Forge Father

**Primary faith:** Torag  
**Institution:** Major dwarven temple and clan religious center  
**Role:** Oaths, craft blessings, funerary traditions, ancestral records, and clan ceremonies.

The Hall works closely with dwarven clans but does not replace clan leadership.

Its priests preserve old craft traditions and dispute records.

## 4. Tallowmere — Shrine of the River and Hearth

**Primary faiths:** Erastil and Gozreh traditions  
**Role:** Agricultural blessing, safe travel, fishing, weather observance, and seasonal community ceremonies.

The shrine serves both rural travelers and harbor communities.

Its clergy are practical and locally focused.

## 5. Northern Marches — The Lantern at the Wall

**Primary faith:** Iomedae with local Pharasma and Erastil traditions  
**Role:** Memorial services, frontier blessings, care for soldiers, and remembrance of the dead.

The institution is not a military command.

Its influence comes from supporting frontier communities and maintaining memorial records for those who die in northern service.

## 6. Western Highlands — The House of Returning Light

**Primary faith:** Sarenrae  
**Role:** Healing, charity, mediation, and care for travelers and the poor.

The institution is respected by both rural communities and urban merchants.

Its priests are known for attempting reconciliation before punishment.

## 7. Eastern Coast — Mariners' Chapel of the Open Sky

**Primary faiths:** Gozreh and Desna traditions  
**Role:** Sailors' rites, storm observance, navigation customs, funerals at sea, and blessings for voyages.

The chapel has strong relationships with shipowners and sailors but does not control Merrowen's fleet.

## 8. Southern Reaches — Local shrine tradition

The Southern Reaches do not possess a single dominant religious institution.

Small temples and shrines are often closely tied to local rulers, civic councils, guilds, and communities.

Abadar, Gozreh, Desna, Erastil, Sarenrae, Pharasma, and other gods may be worshiped in different combinations.

## 9. Religious courts

Religious courts may handle:

- clergy discipline;
- internal doctrine;
- temple property disputes;
- religious office;
- ceremonial matters;
- disputes between members of the same institution.

They do not automatically possess jurisdiction over all secular crimes.

The exact relationship between a religious court and a noble or Crown court depends on local law, charter, and custom.

## 10. Religious festivals

Major religious festivals are usually local or faith-specific rather than continent-wide compulsory holidays.

Common public religious occasions include:

- planting blessings;
- first-harvest ceremonies;
- harvest celebrations;
- sailors' departure rites;
- memorial days;
- funerary observances;
- temple anniversaries;
- saintly or heroic commemorations where locally recognized.

The calendar records the civil year. Religious institutions maintain their own ceremonial calendars.

## 11. Religious authority and politics

Religious institutions may influence:

- royal legitimacy;
- marriage;
- inheritance customs;
- burial;
- public morality;
- charity;
- education;
- records;
- political reputation.

They do not automatically control these areas.

A ruler may ignore a priest, but doing so can create political and social consequences.

## 12. Religious diversity

The same god may be understood differently by different communities.

Two temples dedicated to the same deity can:

- emphasize different doctrines;
- follow different liturgical customs;
- disagree about religious law;
- compete over property;
- disagree over political involvement.

This is intentional.

The gods are real. Mortal interpretation is not automatically correct.

## 13. Divine magic

The mechanism by which the gods grant or mediate divine spellcasting remains intentionally unresolved.

The following are established:

- the gods are real;
- divine spellcasters are uncommon;
- devotion matters;
- not every priest is a spellcaster;
- magical ability does not automatically confer religious office.

The deeper truth should emerge through campaign evidence rather than being preemptively solved.

## 14. Temple Champions

Every established, permanent temple maintains or designates a dedicated defender, commonly called its Temple Champion or its paladin. This is an institutional office rather than a universal Pathfinder class assignment. A Champion uses a class and abilities legal under Pathfinder First Edition; not every deity's alignment permits a literal paladin, so another suitable martial or divine class may fulfill the role.

The Champion's principal responsibility is defense: the temple, its clergy, worshippers under its protection, sacred property, and the community where a threat is directly relevant to the deity's concerns. The Champion may investigate reports of supernatural activity, desecration, undead, heresy, or other threats appropriate to the faith. The clergy assess information and provide religious, local, and historical knowledge; the Champion decides how to investigate and respond within the office's authority.

Clergy are primarily temple keepers and servants of the faithful. They conduct worship, rites, charity, records, counsel, education, and ordinary care. Clerics may serve at a temple, but are not automatically its defenders. They may support the Champion in exceptional danger or when their particular divine abilities are needed. The Champion and senior priest are distinct positions unless specifically established otherwise.

A Champion is not a general law officer or commander of a noble's forces. They may cooperate with secular authorities, request help, or appeal to their faith's wider hierarchy. A major mobilization or crusade is rare and requires appropriate religious authorization, resources, and consideration of secular consequences; an individual Champion cannot declare one alone. Small shrines and caretaker sites are not automatically required to keep a resident Champion. A small permanent temple may share a Champion with a nearby institution where its resources or circumstances require it.

The identities, statistics, training traditions, and current appointments of individual Champions remain open until separately established. Do not silently convert an existing senior priest, keeper, or named NPC into a Champion.
