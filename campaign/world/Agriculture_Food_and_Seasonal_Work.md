# Agriculture, Food Supply, and Seasonal Work

**Status:** GM reference; established economic dependencies with regional practices partly provisional  
**Baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY  
**Related records:** [Everyday Life and Material Culture](Everyday_Life_and_Material_Culture.md), [Foodways of Tallowmere and Its Hinterland](Foodways_of_Tallowmere_and_the_Hinterland.md), [Continental Economic Network](../economy/Continental_Economic_Network.md), [Tallowmere Regional Settlements](../locations/starting_city/Regional_Settlements_and_Landholders.md), [Tallowmere Flora and Fungi](../creatures/Tallowmere_Flora_and_Fungi.md)

This record explains how food is produced, moved, stored, and consumed across the setting. It is a practical guide for portraying farms, estates, markets, harvest work, and the consequences of weather or disrupted trade. It is not an automated calendar, crop simulator, or fixed schedule of seasonal events.

The continent's economy is primarily agricultural, feudal, maritime, and regional. Grain, livestock, dairy, wool, hides, orchard produce, timber, and fish support households and trade. Production and access vary by region, estate, charter, labor, weather, and transport.

## 1. The agricultural world

Most people live close to the work that feeds them. Farming is not a single occupation performed by a uniform class of independent landowners. It includes:
- Lords and noble households holding estates and rights.
- Estate stewards and bailiffs overseeing rents, stores, labor, and accounts.
- Freeholders managing their own land under local law and obligations.
- Tenant households cultivating land in return for rent, dues, labor, or agreed shares.
- Smallholders combining a little land with craft, transport, seasonal, or wage work.
- Landless laborers hired for planting, haymaking, harvest, building, and carting.
- Millers, bakers, brewers, carters, drovers, fishers, and merchants who move food from producer to consumer.

These categories are a useful social framework, not a universal legal classification. Exact tenure, rents, labor obligations, inheritance, and customary rights depend on the relevant lordship, charter, and local law. Do not assume every peasant is a serf, every farmer rents from a lord, or every noble directly manages every field.

A farm is a household economy as well as a food producer. Its stores, animals, tools, seed, fuel, labor, and obligations determine whether it can survive a poor season.

## 2. Major agricultural regions

### Western Heartlands and Golden Fields

House Edarth's territory is a principal agricultural heartland. Grain, livestock, dairy, wool, hides, and agricultural manpower are established strengths. The region's productive plains and valleys supply markets beyond the local estates.

Its strength creates dependencies as well as wealth. Grain must be harvested, dried, stored, guarded, and moved. Roads and river transport matter. A strong harvest does not mean every tenant household is comfortable; rents, debts, labor shortages, storage losses, and unequal access can leave some families vulnerable even in a productive district.

### Central Crownlands and Royal Basin

The central Crownlands combine agriculture with dense administration, markets, river transport, and political demand. Their production is important, but so is their ability to collect and redistribute goods through roads, markets, storehouses, and officials.

Crown provisioning, court demand, garrisons, and urban populations compete with ordinary consumers for supply. Taxation or requisition can move food toward an army or capital while making it less available to the villages that produced it.

### Southern Principalities

The fertile parts of the Southern Principalities are established agricultural sources. Their political fragmentation means there is no single uniform agricultural authority or market policy. Local rulers, towns, landholders, and independent cities may impose different tolls, rights, and obligations.

The region's fertility should not be confused with political stability or equal prosperity. Local floods, competing jurisdictions, road security, and the terms of trade can shape who benefits from production.

### Northern Holds and Pine Marches

The north produces timber, livestock, and other resources but depends on reliable grain imports. Short growing periods, severe cold in the far north, and long supply routes make storage and transport important. The northern population is not uniformly isolated or starving; these are structural vulnerabilities, not current crises.

House Veyr's responsibility for the Ancient Barrier adds a major provisioning burden. Food, fodder, fuel, clothing, repair materials, and replacement animals all have to reach frontier communities. The military importance of the north does not make its households immune to ordinary shortages.

### Kharadorn territories

Kharadorn's mountain and underground communities have distinct production systems. Dwarven metalwork, engineering, and specialist manufacture are major strengths, but the wider dwarven population depends on surface food, markets, and transport. Underground settlement does not eliminate agricultural needs.

Surface holdings and ports can support storage, exchange, livestock, and access to imported grain. Do not assume that all food is grown underground or that every dwarven settlement has the same access to surface farms.

### Merrowen maritime territories

Merrowen's shipping and ports connect food-producing regions to coastal markets and islands. Maritime power can move bulk grain and other provisions, but ships still require crews, maintenance, safe harbors, pilots, and favorable conditions.

Coastal communities may combine fishing, small-scale agriculture, salt work, ship service, and trade. Maritime access is an advantage, not a guarantee against shortages.

### Tallowmere and its hinterland

Tallowmere is a regional port and River Vale trade hub, not a major continental breadbasket. Its immediate food system is a network:
- Millhaven and the Northfields collect grain, flour, oats, and seasonal legumes.
- Nearby farms and estates provide livestock, dairy, orchard produce, and other agricultural goods.
- Saltwick supplies fresh and preserved fish.
- Brackenholt supplies charcoal, firewood, poles, and rough timber, with some hunting and gathering.
- Tallowmere's harbor and market connect local producers to wider inland-sea trade.
- Harthbridge competes for some land-carried trade and provides an alternative market.

Tallowmere's population of approximately 18,000 cannot be fed by a few gardens or fishing boats. The city depends on a wider supply catchment, river traffic, coastal shipping, market exchange, and stored food. A disruption to one supplier may be absorbed by alternatives; a sustained disruption across several routes becomes more serious.

## 3. Crops and cultivated food

Established campaign records name grain, oats, seasonal legumes, orchard produce, livestock, dairy, wool, hides, and fishing as important outputs. Exact crop varieties and regional acreage are not established.

Candidate temperate crops include wheat, barley, rye, oats, peas, beans, turnips, onions, leeks, cabbages, and other hardy vegetables. These are plausible examples, not a definitive crop register. Which crops dominate depends on soil, moisture, local custom, seed access, rents, labor, market demand, and storage.

Different crops serve different purposes:
- Some grains produce finer bread and command higher prices.
- Hardier grains may tolerate poorer soils or difficult weather better.
- Oats and other coarse grains can serve as food and animal feed.
- Legumes provide stored food and diversify the diet.
- Root crops can remain in the ground or be stored under suitable conditions.
- Orchard fruit can be eaten fresh, dried, cooked, pressed, or preserved.

A lord's demesne may prioritize rents and market sales; tenant households may prioritize subsistence, seed, dues, and obligations. A crop that sells well is not necessarily the crop a household can afford to eat.

## 4. Livestock and animal husbandry

Livestock provide food, materials, labor, transport, wealth, and status. Established everyday-life and regional records support cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, horses, donkeys or mules, chickens, geese, and dogs as familiar domestic animals, with local variation. Their practical husbandry, care, feed, working roles, and economic value are developed in [Animal Husbandry and Working Animals](Animal_Husbandry_and_Working_Animals.md).

### Cattle

Cattle provide milk, meat, hides, manure, and in some regions draft power. A household may keep a cow for milk without being able to slaughter it for meat except in special circumstances. Oxen can pull heavy ploughs and carts, but require feed and careful handling.

### Sheep and goats

Sheep provide wool, meat, milk in some practices, skins, and manure. Wool is an important trade good. Flocks need grazing, shepherding, protection, and seasonal attention to lambing and shearing. Goats can browse rougher ground and provide milk, but their appetite can damage young trees and hedges if poorly managed.

### Pigs

Pigs convert food scraps, mast, roots, and other feed into meat and fat. They can be kept in yards or turned out under controlled custom where woodland pannage is permitted. They are useful but destructive around gardens and poorly protected stores.

### Horses, donkeys, and mules

Horses are valuable for riding, hauling, and military service. Donkeys and mules can be useful pack and cart animals. Their value includes training, soundness, temperament, tack, feed, stable space, and the availability of skilled care. Losing a healthy horse can be a major household or merchant loss.

### Poultry and other small stock

Chickens and geese provide eggs, meat, feathers, and occasional sale income. They are vulnerable to foxes, dogs, theft, and disease. Small livestock may be among the few productive assets a poorer household can own.

No specific regional breeds, herd sizes, or annual production totals are established. Do not invent census figures to make an estate feel complete.

## 5. The work of the agricultural year

The calendar names months, but seasons are variable and do not follow the month names reliably. Agricultural work follows soil, plant growth, weather, labor availability, and local custom.

### Preparing land

Work may include repairing fences and gates, clearing drainage, maintaining ditches, preparing ploughs and harness, spreading manure, arranging seed, and organizing labor. Wet ground can delay work even when the calendar suggests planting should have begun.

### Planting and sowing

Seed is valuable. A household must reserve enough from the previous harvest or acquire it before sowing. Poor seed, late planting, damaged tools, or missing labor can reduce the crop long before harvest.

### Growing and tending

Fields require weeding, drainage, livestock control, repair of boundaries, and attention to pests and disease. Orchard trees require pruning and protection. Livestock need pasture, water, shelter, and fodder.

### Haymaking and fodder

Hay is not a minor by-product. It determines whether horses, cattle, and other animals can be kept through difficult months. A wet haymaking period can ruin or reduce stores. Straw, browse, roots, and other feed may supplement hay but do not replace every animal's needs equally.

### Harvest

Harvest is labor-intensive and time-sensitive. Grain must be cut, gathered, dried, threshed, winnowed, and stored. Fruit must be picked or processed before it spoils. Labor demand rises sharply, and households may hire workers, borrow help, or delay other tasks.

A harvest can be adequate in aggregate while individual holdings suffer losses. Flooded fields, poor drainage, crop disease, labor shortages, theft, rent demands, or lack of storage can produce unequal outcomes.

### Storage and winter preparation

Food stores need dry, secure spaces. Grain can be damaged by damp, insects, rodents, fire, or theft. Root vegetables and fruit need appropriate storage. Meat, fish, and dairy require preservation methods such as salting, smoking, drying, fermenting, or cool storage.

Winter preparation also includes fodder, fuel, clothing, roof repairs, tools, and transport. A household may have enough grain but still struggle if it lacks fuel or cannot keep its animals alive.

## 6. Storage, milling, and food movement

Food is not available to a city simply because it has been grown somewhere in the countryside.

Grain must be moved from fields to threshing floors and stores, then to mills, bakers, brewers, merchants, or consumers. Mill capacity, water levels, milling rights, cart availability, road condition, tolls, and storage losses all affect supply.

Millhaven is a key local node because it collects grain and operates the Long Mill. The mill's public grain measure and adjoining storage sheds are part of its commercial and civic importance. Mill rights and water access can therefore become legal and political disputes.

Barges move bulk goods along the lower River Vale. The river is navigable for suitable barges between Tallowmere and lower reaches near Millhaven in ordinary conditions. Upstream travel is slower and more dependent on water level, towpaths, and pilots. Coastal boats and inland-sea shipping connect Tallowmere to wider markets.

Food transport is expensive in labor, animals, vessels, time, loss, tolls, and risk. Grain is bulky and can be worth less than its transport cost over a long distance unless the market price justifies the journey.

## 7. Food markets and household access

Markets bring together producers, millers, bakers, brewers, fishers, merchants, carters, and consumers. Market access is shaped by charters, local tolls, guild privileges, customary rights, and the ability to bring goods to market.

Food prices vary with:
- quality and grade;
- season and harvest conditions;
- local supply and storage;
- distance and transport cost;
- tolls and market privileges;
- demand from garrisons, ships, noble households, and towns;
- credit, debt, and merchant competition.

Do not assign a universal price to a loaf, sack of grain, fish, or animal without deciding the market, date, quality, and circumstances. A table price is a local fact, not a continental constant.

Poor households are especially exposed to changes in staple prices because food takes a larger share of their available income. Wealthier households can substitute foods, draw on stores, buy from multiple suppliers, or use credit. A shortage does not affect every class equally.

## 8. Famine, scarcity, and food insecurity

A poor harvest is not automatically a famine. Between a failed field and widespread hunger lie stores, imports, alternative foods, household wealth, charity, credit, transport, and political choices.

Useful distinctions:
- **Poor yield:** a particular crop or holding produces less than expected.
- **Local shortage:** a community has less food available than it needs at ordinary prices.
- **Price pressure:** food remains available but becomes harder for some households to afford.
- **Supply disruption:** food exists elsewhere but cannot reach a market reliably.
- **Food insecurity:** households cannot reliably obtain enough suitable food, even if markets remain supplied.
- **Famine:** severe, sustained deprivation affecting a population, normally with mortality and broader social consequences.

The Crown, nobles, merchants, temples, and local communities may respond differently. Possible responses include opening stores, importing grain, restricting exports, changing tolls, requisitioning supplies, organizing relief, or protecting transport. Each response has costs and political implications. Do not assume benevolence or malice in advance.

No famine, continent-wide shortage, or general food crisis is established at the campaign's opening.

## 9. Weather, climate, and regional variation

The campaign calendar explicitly states that seasons are not fixed to months. They may arrive early or late, last months or years, and vary by region. A month called Harvestfall does not guarantee harvest weather.

Broad geographic tendencies provide context:
- The far north and Frozen Expanse experience severe cold, snow, ice, and exposed conditions.
- The Northern Holds and Pine Marches face cold-season supply and fodder concerns.
- The Western Highlands and High Peaks vary with elevation; passes and slopes respond differently to snowmelt and storms.
- The Western Heartlands and central agricultural plains support grain and livestock production.
- The Central Inland Sea is a major maritime system subject to storms, fog, and navigation hazards.
- The northeastern mountains shape Kharadorn access, surface supply, and mining conditions.
- The eastern forests and shore combine woodland resources with coastal weather.
- The Great Delta is low-lying and shaped by branching waterways, silting, and flooding.
- The Southwestern Marches are arid, with water storage, wells, and caravan access as structural concerns.
- The Southern Principalities contain fertile lowlands, forests, and river systems, but political fragmentation creates local variation.
- The Southern Islands depend heavily on maritime movement and safe anchorage.

These are broad tendencies, not detailed climate classifications. Exact local weather patterns, crop calendars, flood cycles, and drought histories remain open unless recorded in a regional reference.

## 10. Tallowmere's opening agricultural context

At 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC:
- Northfield harvest monitoring is recorded as WATCH. The expected harvest is adequate but uneven, with reported farm damage and labor shortages.
- Northern Watch autumn provisioning is underway as a seasonal project.
- No specific temperature, rainfall pattern, storm, frost, drought, flood, or severe weather event is established for the opening date.
- No famine or continent-wide trade collapse is established.
- Normal seasonal trade is assumed, but this does not mean every farm or household is equally prosperous.

The Northfield situation should be treated as an existing local concern, not inflated into a crisis without further evidence. A farm's damage, a shortage of labor, or an uneven yield may matter greatly to the household concerned while remaining a modest issue for the wider region.

## 11. GM portrayal guidance

When food or agriculture matters in play, establish the specific people and place before deciding the consequence.

Consider:
1. What is being produced, and by whom?
2. Who owns the land, crop, livestock, mill, storehouse, or transport?
3. What labor is required, and who supplies it?
4. What rights, rents, dues, or customs govern the work?
5. What can be eaten, sold, stored, or transported?
6. What alternatives exist if one crop, road, mill, or supplier fails?
7. Who has reserves or credit, and who does not?
8. What do ordinary people believe is happening, and what is actually known?

The GM does not need to track every field or sack of grain. Details matter when they affect a household, dispute, journey, market, army, or political decision. A believable world is not a spreadsheet with medieval hats.

## 12. Deliberately unresolved

- Exact crop rotations, seed varieties, acreage, and yields by region.
- Exact dates for planting, haymaking, harvest, and slaughter.
- Regional breeds and herd sizes.
- Estate-specific rent, labor, and storage arrangements.
- Precise food prices and market volumes.
- Full crop and livestock disease catalogues.
- Historical famines, major harvest failures, and their causes.
- Detailed climate normals and local weather records.

These are not missing facts that must all be filled before play. Develop them when they materially improve the setting or answer a question raised at the table.
