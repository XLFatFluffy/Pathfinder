# Animal Husbandry and Working Animals

**Status:** GM reference framework; practical husbandry guidance is not a census of local breeds or herds  
**Rules baseline:** Pathfinder First Edition  
**Campaign baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY until adapted for player-facing knowledge

Animals are part of the setting's food supply, transport, wealth, labor, warfare, and household life. They are not scenery that appears only when the party needs a horse or a steak. Their care costs time, feed, land, skill, and money. Losing an animal can damage a household's livelihood long after the immediate event.

This reference establishes a practical framework for portraying animal ownership and care. It does not establish exact regional herd sizes, breeds, prices, veterinary services, disease prevalence, or universal rules for animal ownership.

## 1. Animals have several kinds of value

An animal may provide more than one useful thing over its life. Its value is not limited to its sale price or the meat it yields.

- **Food:** meat, milk, eggs, fat, and other products.
- **Materials:** wool, hides, leather, horn, bone, feathers, manure, and other by-products.
- **Labor:** ploughing, hauling, pulling carts, carrying loads, turning mills or other equipment where locally used.
- **Transport:** riding, pack carrying, pulling wagons, and moving goods.
- **Reproduction:** breeding stock represents future animals and future income.
- **Status and military value:** trained riding horses, warhorses, hunting dogs, and well-kept breeding stock can signal wealth, office, or martial standing.

The same animal can serve different purposes in different households. A horse may be a noble's riding mount, a merchant's transport asset, a courier's livelihood, or a soldier's assigned mount. A cow may be kept for milk and breeding rather than slaughter. A pig may be a household's main store of future meat.

## 2. The principal domestic animals

The following are broad working categories suitable to the setting. They are not a claim that every species, breed, or use is equally common in every region.

### Horses

Horses are valuable because they combine transport, speed, status, and military use. Their value depends on training, health, temperament, age, strength, breeding, and the work expected of them.

Different horses are suited to different purposes. A riding horse, pack horse, cart horse, and trained warhorse should not be treated as interchangeable. A mount capable of carrying an armored rider in battle is a specialized and expensive animal, not simply any horse with a saddle.

Horse care includes:
- regular feed and clean water;
- grooming and inspection for cuts, swelling, parasites, and saddle sores;
- hoof cleaning and attention to lameness;
- suitable shelter, bedding, and rest;
- tack that fits and is kept in working condition;
- gradual conditioning for heavy work or long travel;
- competent handling, especially around crowds, fire, noise, and unfamiliar animals.

A tired or injured horse may remain alive but be unfit for travel or service. A mount can be lost through illness, accident, neglect, exhaustion, theft, or battle. Replacing a valuable trained horse may be difficult even for a wealthy household.

### Cattle and oxen

Cattle can provide milk, meat, hides, manure, and breeding stock. Some are trained for traction and hauling. Oxen are castrated cattle trained for draft work; they are generally slower than horses but can be strong and useful for heavy loads and ploughing.

Not every household that keeps cattle owns a trained plough team. Smallholders may share animals, hire a team, or exchange labor with neighbors. A household's access to draft animals can shape how much land it can work and how quickly it can move goods.

### Sheep

Sheep provide wool, meat, hides, and sometimes milk. Wool is valuable because it can be processed into cloth and traded over distance. Flocks require grazing, water, protection, shearing, lambing care, and attention to parasites and disease.

Sheep are vulnerable to predators, theft, exposure, poor pasture, and bad weather. A lost lamb may matter greatly to a smallholder, while a large estate may absorb some losses more easily. Grazing rights and access to seasonal pasture can become serious local disputes.

### Goats

Goats can provide milk, meat, hides, and hair or fiber in suitable varieties. They browse a wider range of plants than sheep and can survive on rougher ground, but poorly managed goats can damage young trees, hedges, and gardens.

Their ability to use marginal ground does not make them cost-free. They still need water, shelter, care during birth, and protection from theft and predators.

### Pigs

Pigs convert food scraps, roots, fallen produce, and suitable woodland forage into meat and fat. They are valuable to households that can feed and contain them. They can be destructive if allowed to root through gardens, field margins, or young woodland without permission.

Some regions may permit seasonal pannage, where pigs feed on fallen nuts or other woodland mast. This is a general practice, not an established right in every Tallowmere woodland. Do not assume Brackenholt or the Deep Bracken grants unrestricted pig-grazing access.

Pigs are often slaughtered when a household can preserve the meat and make use of the resulting fat, blood, offal, and other products. The timing depends on feed, household needs, and custom.

### Poultry and waterfowl

Chickens and geese provide eggs, meat, feathers, and other household products. Ducks may be kept where water and suitable space are available. Poultry can be maintained by households with limited land, but birds still need protection from foxes, dogs, theft, weather, and disease.

Eggs are valuable because they can be used without slaughtering a productive animal. Poultry keeping can be part of household work for children, older residents, or other family members, but it is not effortless or necessarily profitable.

### Donkeys and mules

Donkeys and mules can carry loads and perform useful work where terrain, cost, or local custom makes them suitable. A mule is the offspring of a male donkey and a female horse; it is often valued for strength and endurance, though individual animals vary.

They should not be treated as inferior horses. Their temperament, training, size, and suitability for a task matter more than a simple hierarchy of prestige.

### Dogs and cats

Dogs may be used for guarding, herding, hunting, tracking, and companionship. Their training and usefulness vary. A household dog is not automatically a trained hunting or war animal.

Cats may help control rodents around homes, granaries, stores, ships, and workshops. Their presence does not guarantee that a building is free of vermin.

## 3. Feed, water, and seasonal care

Animals require food even when they are not working. Grazing alone may not sustain every animal throughout the year, especially when pasture is poor, snow or mud limits access, or animals are kept in crowded urban stables.

Common feed categories include:
- pasture and browse;
- hay and dried grasses;
- straw and other bedding or roughage;
- oats and other grains where appropriate;
- roots and household by-products;
- stored fodder reserved for winter or difficult periods.

The value of fodder rises when animals cannot graze freely. A household may have enough food for people but insufficient hay for its animals. That can force it to sell stock, reduce the number of working animals, or spend money it needed for other purposes.

Water access matters. A stable or yard with no convenient water supply creates daily labor. Animals that work hard, travel, or produce milk need regular access to water and suitable rest.

Seasonal work includes:
- spring births and the care of young animals;
- pasture rotation and repair of fences or enclosures;
- shearing and wool handling;
- training and conditioning working animals;
- storing hay and fodder;
- winter shelter, bedding, and feed management;
- slaughter and preservation when household stores and animal condition make it appropriate.

The calendar's month names do not establish exact weather or husbandry dates. Actual conditions vary by region and year.

## 4. Stables, yards, and handlers

A stable is more than a place to tie a horse. It requires feed storage, water, bedding, cleaning, tack storage, ventilation, drainage, and people who know how to handle animals safely.

A wealthy household may employ grooms, stable hands, farriers, and other specialists. A smallholder may do most animal care within the household. A merchant may pay an inn or stable yard for care while traveling. A military garrison may assign animals to handlers and maintain stores for feed and equipment.

Stable work includes:
- feeding and watering;
- mucking out and replacing bedding;
- grooming and checking for injury;
- cleaning and repairing tack and harness;
- inspecting hooves and arranging specialist care;
- loading and unloading animals;
- separating unfamiliar or aggressive animals;
- keeping animals from damaging stored food or equipment.

A stable hand may know a horse's temperament and habits better than its owner. That knowledge is practical expertise, not merely menial labor.

Tallowmere has established stables and animal-associated spaces, but this does not establish a single central stable service, a citywide animal registry, or a named veterinary office. The scale and ownership of particular yards remain governed by their local records.

## 5. Animal health and treatment

Animal care is a skilled practical field, but the setting has no established modern veterinary system. Owners, grooms, shepherds, farriers, experienced farmers, hunters, and other handlers may recognize common problems through observation and experience.

Common concerns include:
- wounds, bruising, and infected bites;
- lameness, hoof damage, and saddle sores;
- parasites and skin conditions;
- poor feed, dehydration, and exhaustion;
- difficult births and loss of young animals;
- respiratory illness and digestive problems;
- spoiled fodder or contaminated water;
- injuries from harness, carts, falls, and overwork.

A person who knows horses may not know sheep. A shepherd may understand flock behavior but lack skill in treating a horse's hoof. A healer who treats people is not automatically qualified to treat animals.

Traditional remedies may help with comfort or basic care, but they are not guaranteed cures. Do not create a reliable herbal cure for a serious animal disease without explicit rules and canon approval.

An outbreak affecting livestock would have broad consequences. It could reduce meat, milk, wool, hides, draft power, transport, and military readiness. It could alter rents, credit, household survival, and trade. No specific livestock epidemic is established for Tallowmere or the wider campaign at the opening date.

## 6. Slaughter, food, and household wealth

Slaughter is an economic decision as well as a food decision. A household weighs an animal's immediate meat value against its future milk, eggs, wool, breeding, labor, or transport value.

Meat supply depends on:
- which animals are available and suitable for slaughter;
- household ownership and purchasing power;
- the time of year and condition of the animal;
- the ability to use or preserve the meat;
- access to salt, smoke, fuel, containers, and storage;
- local custom and any rights governing slaughter or sale.

A poor household may consume meat less often than a wealthy household, but it may still keep chickens, a pig, or a goat if it has the space and resources. A prosperous household may eat more meat while also preserving more stock for future use.

Do not assume every town has a large public slaughterhouse or that every butcher follows one universal regulation. Specific slaughter sites, fees, inspection practices, and waste rules remain local questions.

Animal by-products are economically important. Hides become leather; wool becomes cloth; horn and bone can become tools or fittings; manure improves fields; feathers can be used for bedding or fletching. Waste from slaughter and animal keeping must be managed, but the method varies by place and means.

## 7. Transport, trade, and military consequences

Animals are part of the transport system. A cart's capacity depends on the draft animals, harness, wheels, road, load, and condition of the team. A mounted traveler needs a fit mount and may need a place to rest or change animals. A caravan must carry fodder or arrange access to it along the route.

A fast messenger, a mounted soldier, a loaded wagon, and a livestock train do not travel at the same pace. Losing an animal can delay a shipment, strand a traveler, or leave a household unable to move its goods.

Military forces depend on horses and pack animals for scouting, messages, supply, officers, cavalry, and transport. These animals require handlers, fodder, water, shelter, and veterinary attention. A force that requisitions too many animals can damage the local economy and reduce the ability of farmers and merchants to work.

Military service does not mean every soldier owns a horse. Mounted service and the quality of the mount depend on the institution, rank, role, wealth, and specific obligation.

## 8. Class, ownership, and access

Animal ownership is uneven.

- **Smallholders:** may own a few poultry, a pig, a goat, or shared access to a draft team. Losses can be severe.
- **Tenant farmers:** may keep animals under customary or lease conditions, but the landholder's rights and local rules matter.
- **Craft households:** may keep animals for food, transport, or materials, depending on space and trade.
- **Merchants:** may own pack animals, cart teams, or horses, or hire transport instead.
- **Noble households:** may maintain riding horses, hunting dogs, breeding stock, and larger herds, supported by servants and estate resources.
- **Military institutions:** maintain or requisition animals according to their needs and obligations; the exact system differs by force.

A person who owns an animal may not own the land it grazes on. Grazing, watering, pannage, droving, and passage rights may belong to different authorities or customary users. The animal's presence on a field does not prove the owner has permission to use it.

## 9. Tallowmere and its hinterland

The established local food and settlement records identify livestock and dairy as supplies from nearby farms and estates. The exact breeds, herd sizes, and distribution are not established.

Tallowmere's working harbor, market, granaries, stables, cart traffic, inns, and workshops all depend on animals. Horses, mules, donkeys, and possibly oxen may be encountered in the city according to the job and district. Their feed, dung, injuries, noise, and disposal are ordinary urban concerns.

The Northfields and nearby holdings support agricultural and pastoral work. The regional wildlife catalogue identifies cattle, sheep, goats, pigs, horses, donkeys or mules, chickens, geese, and dogs as plausible farm animals, but it does not confirm a fixed local inventory or specific breeds.

The Brackenholt record establishes small-scale hunting, trapping, and woodland gathering alongside charcoal, firewood, poles, and timber. It does not establish a local livestock industry or unrestricted woodland grazing. The Siltfen supports limited grazing on firmer ground, but the exact stock, schedule, and rights remain unresolved.

Saltwick's established role centers on fishing and shore trade; do not assume it is a major livestock center without further evidence.

## 10. GM portrayal and adjudication

When an animal matters in a scene, consider:
1. Who owns it, and who is responsible for its care?
2. What work or value does it provide?
3. What does it need before and after the task?
4. Is it trained for this work, or merely capable of attempting it?
5. What happens if it is injured, exhausted, lost, or stolen?
6. Who has the practical knowledge to handle or treat it?
7. What rights govern the land, water, road, or pasture it uses?

Use Pathfinder First Edition rules for animal companions, mounts, animal handling, combat, and other mechanics where relevant. The setting's social and economic context should not silently change published class features, animal companion rules, or equipment statistics.

Do not turn every animal into a hazard or every livestock problem into a quest. Most animals are part of ordinary work. Their presence makes the world believable because people must feed them, clean up after them, repair their equipment, and make decisions about their value.

## 11. Open decisions

The following remain unresolved until a specific location or campaign event requires them:
- regional breeds, sizes, and animal populations;
- exact herd sizes and ownership patterns;
- local animal markets, fairs, and sale customs;
- veterinary specialists, farriers, and animal healers by name;
- common disease prevalence and recognized animal epidemics;
- exact fodder prices, feed rations, and winter stores;
- local slaughter rules, inspection, fees, and waste arrangements;
- the extent of pannage and other woodland grazing rights;
- the use and availability of ox teams in specific settlements;
- military remount systems, breeding programs, and requisition procedures.

Do not convert broad husbandry guidance into universal campaign law. Local charters, custom, wealth, geography, and actual play determine the details.
