# Building, Construction, and Maintenance

**Status:** GM reference framework; established regional materials and local building patterns are distinguished from general construction guidance  
**Campaign baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY until adapted for player knowledge

Buildings are not static scenery. They are expensive stores of labor and material that must be repaired, heated, lit, cleaned, defended, and adapted as households and businesses change. A house may stand for generations while its roof, rooms, doors, windows, and internal use change repeatedly.

This reference provides a practical framework for describing construction and maintenance across the setting. It does not establish a universal architectural style, building code, standard plan, or exact construction price.

## 1. Building is a process, not a single craft

A building project can involve several stages:
1. **Rights and site:** determine who owns the ground, what access or building rights apply, and whether the site is suitable.
2. **Materials:** obtain stone, timber, clay, lime, thatch, tile, slate, metal fittings, glass, and other materials where available.
3. **Preparation:** clear and level the site, arrange drainage, establish foundations, and protect nearby structures.
4. **Structure:** build foundations, walls, frames, floors, stairs, and supports.
5. **Weather protection:** complete the roof, coverings, doors, shutters, and exterior finish.
6. **Services and fittings:** provide hearths, chimneys or smoke outlets, storage, water access, drains, workshops, and other necessary features.
7. **Inspection and repair:** check that the building is sound, dry, safe, and fit for its intended use.

Not every project requires a new building. A shop may be altered from a former workshop; a warehouse may gain a loading door; a house may add a room above an existing structure. Reuse and adaptation are often cheaper than starting from bare ground.

## 2. Materials and their properties

### Stone

Stone is valuable for foundations, walls, defensive works, paving, bridges, quays, hearths, and other structures where durability matters. Its quality depends on the type of stone, how it is cut, the skill of the mason, and the conditions of the site.

Dressed stone requires more labor than rough rubble. A wall built from ordinary building stone is not automatically a fortress wall. Good masonry depends on foundations, bonding, mortar, drainage, and maintenance.

### Timber

Timber is used for frames, floors, roofs, doors, shutters, stairs, scaffolding, carts, tools, and many household fittings. Different woods have different strengths, durability, weight, and workability.

Green or poorly seasoned timber may warp, shrink, split, or move as it dries. Good builders consider the condition of the wood, how it is stored, and the purpose for which it will be used.

Timber structures can be repaired piece by piece, but fire, rot, insects, water, and poor joints can create serious weaknesses.

### Clay, plaster, lime, and earth

Clay and earth can serve as building materials, infill, roofing components, floors, and finishes where local practice and material allow. Plaster can protect and smooth walls; lime-based mortar and finishes may be used in suitable construction.

The exact local production of bricks, tiles, lime, plaster, and fired building materials varies. Do not assume every settlement has a kiln, brickworks, or specialist supplier.

### Roofing materials

Roof coverings may include thatch, shingles, tiles, slate, or other local materials. The choice depends on climate, slope, availability, cost, skill, and the building's purpose.

A roof must shed water and resist local weather. Poorly maintained thatch, broken tiles, loose slate, damaged shingles, and blocked gutters can all allow water into a building. No single roofing material is universal across the continent.

### Metal, glass, and fittings

Metal is used for nails, hinges, locks, tools, brackets, grilles, fastenings, and specialized structural fittings. More elaborate metalwork is expensive and may be commissioned.

Glass is valuable and may be used in wealthier homes, temples, offices, and specialist buildings. Many ordinary openings use shutters, oiled cloth, horn, or other coverings instead. Do not assume every room has glazed windows.

## 3. Foundations, walls, and roofs

A building's foundations must suit the ground. Poor drainage, soft soil, riverbank conditions, slope, and water movement can cause settling or structural damage. A stone foundation does not make the upper structure immune to rot or fire.

Buildings may combine materials. A stone or timber ground floor can support upper stories of timber framing, plaster, tile, or slate, as already established in Tallowmere's housing record. Older buildings may have been extended or repaired over time, leaving visible differences in materials and workmanship.

Walls can be load-bearing, framed, partitioned, or adapted from earlier structures. Interior divisions may change as families grow, businesses expand, or rooms are rented. A building's current layout need not match its original plan.

Roofs are among the most important maintenance concerns. They protect everything beneath them, and a small leak can damage beams, bedding, stock, tools, stored food, and plaster. A roof may be repaired repeatedly rather than replaced in one operation.

## 4. Heat, light, water, and fire

A building must support the life conducted inside it.

- **Hearths and braziers:** provide heat and cooking, but require fuel, safe placement, ventilation, and attention.
- **Chimneys and smoke outlets:** where present, require maintenance and can become dangerous if blocked or poorly built.
- **Lighting:** lamps and candles require fuel and care. Wealthier buildings may have more rooms and better lighting, but still face fire risk.
- **Water:** private wells, cisterns, pumps, public facilities, and carried water create different demands. A building's access to water does not prove the water is safe.
- **Drainage:** roofs, yards, streets, cellars, and foundations need ways to handle rain and runoff. A drain is not automatically a sewer.
- **Storage:** food, fuel, tools, and valuable goods require dry, secure spaces suited to their contents.

Fire risk depends on building materials, spacing, stored goods, fuel, weather, and available water. Stone walls can slow fire without preventing it from spreading through roofs, floors, doors, stored goods, or adjacent structures.

## 5. Repair and maintenance

A building requires regular attention. Common work includes:
- replacing roof coverings and repairing leaks;
- clearing gutters and drains;
- repairing shutters, doors, hinges, locks, and window frames;
- replacing damaged beams, boards, stairs, and floor sections;
- repointing or repairing masonry;
- renewing plaster, limewash, and other finishes;
- checking hearths, chimneys, ovens, and workshop furnaces;
- managing damp, pests, and stored goods;
- repairing yards, fences, gates, wells, and water channels.

A wealthy household may employ servants, stewards, builders, and specialist trades. A poorer household may patch a roof itself, borrow tools, share labor with neighbors, or postpone repairs until the damage becomes urgent.

Maintenance is not merely cosmetic. A neglected roof, blocked drain, weak stair, damaged retaining wall, or unstable chimney can injure people and destroy property.

## 6. Builders and skilled labor

Construction can involve:
- quarry workers and stone dressers;
- masons and wall builders;
- carpenters, joiners, and roof framers;
- roofers and thatchers;
- plasterers and finishers;
- smiths who make tools, nails, hinges, and fittings;
- glaziers and other specialist craftspeople where available;
- carters and laborers who move materials;
- surveyors, stewards, or officials who establish boundaries and authorize work.

These are occupational categories, not proof that every town has a separate guild for each craft. A master may employ family members and apprentices. Some skills are concentrated in a few workshops; others are common among householders and local laborers.

A building's cost includes more than materials. It requires transport, skilled labor, unskilled labor, tools, scaffolding, food for workers, time, access, and sometimes permission or compensation for neighboring properties.

## 7. Building type and social purpose

### Rural homes and farm buildings

Rural buildings are shaped by available materials, weather, livestock, storage, work, and household wealth. A farmhouse may include sleeping rooms, a hearth, storage, animal shelters, work areas, and outbuildings. Barns and granaries may be as important to the household as the main dwelling.

A smallholder's house may be modest but carefully maintained. A manor may have several buildings with different purposes, including kitchens, stores, workshops, stables, chapels, and accommodation for servants or retainers.

### Urban homes and shops

Urban buildings are often adapted to limited space and mixed use. Families may live above shops or workshops. Ground floors may be built of stone or timber, with upper stories extending over the street or rear yard where local construction allows.

Crowding, fire, drainage, storage, access, and property boundaries matter. A building may have a shopfront, workroom, cellar, shared stair, rear yard, and rented rooms under one roof.

### Warehouses and commercial premises

Warehouses prioritize storage, access, security, loading, and protection from damp and fire. A warehouse near the harbor may be valuable even if its construction is plain. A dry raised floor, strong doors, suitable roof, and cart access can matter more than ornament.

Workshops require space, light, ventilation, tools, materials, and safe handling of heat or hazardous substances. The intended use of a property can determine whether it is suitable at all.

### Temples and charitable buildings

Religious buildings may include worship spaces, offices, archives, kitchens, meeting rooms, sleeping quarters, gardens, and storage. Their scale and facilities vary widely. A small shrine is not a cathedral, and a charitable house is not automatically a hospital.

### Fortifications and civic structures

Walls, gates, towers, bridges, quays, granaries, cisterns, and garrison buildings are public or strategic assets with specialized construction and maintenance demands. Their presence does not guarantee that every part is in perfect repair or that the city can withstand an unlimited siege.

## 8. Tallowmere: materials, trades, and building pressures

Tallowmere's established building supply network includes:
- **Stonehollow:** ordinary building stone, including material used in repairs to roads, walls, quays, and private buildings.
- **Brackenholt:** charcoal, firewood, poles, and rough timber.
- **Local workshops:** masons, carpenters, smiths, cart repairers, and other craftspeople who support construction and maintenance.
- **Tallowmere's harbor and river:** routes for moving heavy materials, though loading, storage, cartage, and access remain practical constraints.

The city is built in stages. Older stone or timber ground floors may support upper stories of timber framing, plaster, tile, or slate. Districts differ: the Old Quays contain older and irregular waterfront buildings; the New Quays have larger yards, warehouses, and newer but uneven construction; Rivergate contains mills, cart yards, workshops, and river-facing premises; Greywatch Rise has the fortified seat and substantial household buildings.

Dorrik Slatehand is an established dwarf mason and repair contractor in Rivergate. He takes contracts from householders, warehouse owners, and the city. He is currently short one reliable apprentice and refuses work where a client expects sound construction at the price of a patch job.

Stonehollow's quarry families and Brackenholt's woodland workers are not interchangeable suppliers. Stone, timber, charcoal, poles, and finished building work pass through different rights, contracts, labor, and transport arrangements.

Tallowmere's fire and flood vulnerabilities are documented in the local housing, sanitation, and emergency-response records. No complete building code, universal inspection service, citywide repair department, or standardized construction price schedule is established.

## 9. Regional architectural character

The culture records establish different architectural tendencies without creating a single style for every household.

- **Vaelorian:** dressed stone foundations, timber and plaster upper structures, narrow courtyards, durable slate or tile roofs, bridges and retaining walls built for longevity; wealthier districts may use imported stone, metalwork, glass, mosaics, and carved wood.
- **Kharadorn:** architecture emphasizes load-bearing stone, practical durability, workshops, ventilation, and the engineering needs of surface and subterranean communities. A miner's home, merchant's townhouse, and master craftsperson's residence can differ substantially.
- **Veyr:** northern settlements favor buildings that shelter against wind and snow, with steep roofs where needed, stone or packed-earth foundations, timber frames, insulated walls, and central hearths.
- **Merrowen:** ports and coastal settlements require warehouses, repair yards, counting houses, and buildings suited to maritime work. Weatherproofing, access, storage, and repair capacity matter.
- **Edarth:** estate and agricultural architecture follows land, storage, mills, roads, household administration, and the needs of rural communities. No single uniform manor plan is established.
- **Crownlands and Valedorn:** royal, administrative, commercial, religious, and residential buildings reflect the city's role as a political and institutional center. Access, public order, storage, and representation shape construction.

These are broad patterns. Wealth, location, materials, local builders, and the purpose of a building matter more than applying a fixed architectural style to every member of an ancestry or region.

## 10. GM portrayal and adjudication

When describing or adjudicating a building, consider:
1. What is it used for: home, shop, warehouse, workshop, temple, stable, defense, or storage?
2. Who owns it, occupies it, maintains it, and has rights of access?
3. What materials are visible, and where did they come from?
4. How old is the structure, and what has been repaired or altered?
5. How does it handle weather, water, heat, light, and fire?
6. What does the building need that its owner cannot easily afford?
7. What would fail first under stress: roof, door, stair, wall, drainage, stores, or access?

Use damage and maintenance to create believable consequences. A leaking roof may ruin stored goods. A blocked drain may flood a cellar. A weak stair may make upper rooms inaccessible. A fire may destroy a building's contents even if its stone walls remain standing.

Do not make every old building a ruin or every wealthy building flawless. Maintenance, labor, and money determine condition.

## 11. Open decisions

The following remain unresolved:
- exact local production and availability of brick, tile, lime, glass, and specialist roofing materials;
- regional construction measurements, common plans, and standard room layouts;
- named builders, roofers, plasterers, glaziers, and other specialists beyond established NPCs;
- construction wages, material prices, project durations, and common contracts;
- detailed city building ordinances, inspection powers, and fire-code enforcement;
- exact condition and repair schedule of walls, gates, bridges, quays, drains, and public buildings;
- local differences in foundations, roofs, and building methods beyond established cultural guidance.

Do not invent a universal building code or standard construction price. Resolve details when a property, project, hazard, or campaign decision requires them.
