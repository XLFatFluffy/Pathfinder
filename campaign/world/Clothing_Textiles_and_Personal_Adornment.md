# Clothing, Textiles, and Personal Adornment

**Status:** GM reference framework; broad material practices established, local styles and supply details vary  
**Campaign baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY until adapted for player-facing knowledge

Clothing is shelter, equipment, household property, skilled labor, and social language. A garment tells something about the wearer's work, means, climate, access to trade, and concern for appearance. It is also expensive to replace. Most people maintain clothing rather than discard it as soon as it becomes worn.

This reference connects the established regional culture records and Tallowmere's named textile and leather businesses. It does not establish a universal fashion code, exact garment vocabulary, textile census, or fixed continental price list.

## 1. What clothing must do

Most clothing serves several purposes at once:
- protect the wearer from cold, wind, rain, sun, dirt, and injury;
- allow movement for work, riding, fighting, carrying, and travel;
- identify occupation, household, rank, affiliation, or ceremonial role;
- preserve modesty according to local custom and personal preference;
- display wealth, skill, taste, religious devotion, or family memory.

A person's clothing is shaped by climate, occupation, household means, access to materials, and the work they expect to do. The same person may own different clothing for work, travel, worship, formal visits, and celebration.

Clothing should not be treated as a rigid ancestry uniform. People borrow styles, adapt garments, wear gifts, buy imported goods, and make practical choices. Regional patterns matter, but individuals remain individuals.

## 2. Materials and their uses

### Wool

Wool is a major practical textile because it provides warmth and can be woven into durable outer garments, cloaks, blankets, hose, and other clothing. Quality varies with fiber, preparation, spinning, weave, finishing, and dye.

Wool can be heavy, warm, and slow to dry when soaked. It is useful in northern and upland regions, but garments still need care and repair.

### Linen and plant fibers

Linen is useful for shirts, underlayers, head coverings, household cloth, and other items worn close to the skin. It can be washed more readily than heavy outer garments, though its production requires skilled processing.

Flax and hemp are possible textile crops, but local cultivation is not established everywhere. Imported fibers may supply places without a suitable or developed local crop. Nettle fiber and other plant fibers are possible materials, not a confirmed regional industry.

### Leather, hide, and fur

Leather is used for footwear, belts, gloves, straps, harness, armor components, bags, and protective clothing. Its quality depends on the hide, tanning, preparation, thickness, and workmanship.

Fur may provide warmth and status. Its availability and value depend on the animal, region, trade, and quality of preparation. Do not assume fur is common simply because a region is cold.

### Fine and imported textiles

Fine cloth, silk, elaborate embroidery, imported dyes, and specialized fabrics are more accessible to wealthy households and people connected to trade. Such goods are not automatically available in every market or every town.

The possession of imported cloth can signal access to trade, patronage, or wealth. It does not prove that the wearer is noble. A merchant may own an expensive cloak; a noble may deliberately dress plainly.

## 3. From raw fiber to finished garment

Textile production involves several kinds of skilled labor. The exact organization varies by region and workshop.

A common sequence may include:
1. **Preparing fiber:** cleaning, sorting, washing, combing, or carding wool and preparing plant fibers.
2. **Spinning:** turning prepared fiber into thread or yarn.
3. **Weaving or knitting:** making cloth or other textile forms.
4. **Finishing:** washing, fulling, stretching, brushing, or otherwise treating cloth to improve its texture and performance.
5. **Dyeing and decoration:** applying color, pattern, embroidery, trim, or other ornament.
6. **Cutting and sewing:** turning cloth into garments fitted to a person or made in common sizes.
7. **Repair and alteration:** mending seams, patching holes, replacing fastenings, resizing, and extending a garment's useful life.

These are broad craft stages, not proof that every region has a separate guild or specialist for each operation. A household may perform some work itself, while a merchant town may rely on workshops and specialized trades.

A tailor does not necessarily spin or weave the cloth they sell. A cloth merchant may buy finished bolts from elsewhere. A dyer may work for several merchants or workshops. The chain can involve many people without one of them controlling the entire process.

## 4. Ordinary garments and layers

The following are useful general categories, not a mandatory costume for every person.

- **Underlayers:** linen or other lighter cloth worn next to the skin. These can be washed more often than heavy outer clothing.
- **Work garments:** durable shirts, tunics, trousers or hose, skirts, aprons, oversmocks, and other practical clothing suited to a task.
- **Outer layers:** wool coats, cloaks, mantles, hoods, wraps, and weatherproof garments.
- **Footwear:** shoes, boots, sandals, or other coverings suited to local conditions and work. Quality and repair matter more than fashion for many laborers.
- **Headwear:** hoods, caps, veils, hats, and coverings used for warmth, work, modesty, or status.
- **Belts and fastenings:** practical means of carrying tools, securing garments, and displaying small personal objects.
- **Protective clothing:** gloves, aprons, leather pieces, padded garments, and specialized workwear.

Do not assume modern underwear, standardized sizing, or identical clothing across the continent. Garments are cut and worn according to local custom, materials, climate, and available skill.

## 5. Clothing, work, and class

### Laborers and rural households

Working people favor durable clothing that can be patched, cleaned, and altered. Garments may be stained by soil, grease, smoke, fish, dye, metalwork, animal care, or cargo. A person may keep one better garment for worship, a wedding, a market negotiation, or a formal visit.

A poor household may pass clothing between family members, alter a larger garment for a growing child, or use worn cloth as patches, rags, bedding, or other household material. This is not evidence of carelessness. Cloth represents many hours of labor and is worth preserving.

### Craftspeople and merchants

Craftspeople may wear better-quality garments than laborers, especially when dealing with customers or representing a workshop. Their clothing must still allow them to work. Aprons, sleeves, belts, gloves, and protective layers may be more important than decoration.

Merchants and clerks may use cleaner, better-fitted garments to signal reliability and status. Better cloth and dyes can communicate wealth without requiring jewelry or elaborate ornament.

### Nobles and court households

Noble clothing can display wealth, lineage, office, mourning, marriage alliances, religious observance, and political allegiance. Fine fabric, embroidery, fur, jewelry, imported dyes, metal fasteners, and specialized tailoring are costly.

Fashion can change more quickly near court because people observe and imitate influential households. A style may become associated with a patron or political circle without becoming a formal uniform. Do not invent a court dress law or faction color code unless one is established.

## 6. Regional character

Regional cultural records establish broad tendencies. They are not rigid dress codes.

### Vaelorian

Vaelorian clothing favors durable quality and careful construction. Common garments include fitted coats, layered clothing, cloaks suited to mountain weather, sturdy boots, and wool, linen, leather, and locally produced textiles.

Wealthier clothing may use fine embroidery, imported dyes, silk, metal fasteners, jewelry, and carefully selected fabrics. Vaelorian status can be communicated through fit, workmanship, an old family clasp, or exceptional embroidery rather than excessive ornament.

### Veyr and the Northern Holds

Northern clothing favors layers, wool, leather, fur where available, durable boots, gloves, and weatherproof outer garments. People judge equipment by whether it works. Decorative clothing exists, particularly among nobles and prosperous merchants, but practical quality is widely respected.

### Merrowen

Maritime clothing favors layers, wool, linen, leather, weatherproof outer garments, and footwear suited to wet surfaces. Wealthy merchants may wear imported cloth and jewelry, while dock workers wear patched clothing suited to hard labor.

A sailor's clothing may show salt, tar, rope wear, and repeated repairs. A merchant who spends little time on deck may dress very differently from a pilot or deckhand.

### Crownlands and Valedorn

Fashion changes more quickly near court. Clothing can signal political affiliation, patronage, mourning, rank, and access to imported goods. Courtly influence may spread outward through merchants, servants, visiting nobles, and imitation, but not every provincial household follows the latest style.

### Kharadorn and Edarth

The current culture records establish dwarven craft, household, and working traditions, and Edarth's landed and agricultural social structure, but do not yet define a detailed regional clothing system. Use the general material framework and the specific character's work, household, and status rather than inventing a uniform dwarven or Edarth costume.

## 7. Tallowmere: clothing, leatherwork, and laundry

Tallowmere has several established services relevant to clothing and personal equipment.

### Thread & Thimble

Sella Marr, a halfling tailor and cloth merchant, owns Thread & Thimble. Repairs and practical clothing are the core business. She keeps common sizes and fabrics, while fine garments are made to measure.

The documented stock and services include:
- noble's outfit by commission, starting at 75 gp; fabric and ornament can raise the cost;
- six plain wool cloaks at 5 sp each;
- five daily clothing-repair jobs, priced from 2 sp to 1 gp depending on damage;
- twelve tailored work shirts at 3 sp each;
- four heavy wool coats at 3 gp each, seasonal stock.

These are listed opening quantities and asking prices, not unlimited inventory or a guarantee that a particular size or fabric is available. Fitting and sewing take time; rush work costs more when Sella's workroom is busy.

### The Split Hide

Hadran Voss is a human leatherworker and harness-maker. His work focuses on durable leather goods, repairs, straps, harness, and waterproofing. His products are functional rather than fashionable.

The listed business is a practical source for leatherwork and repairs, not a general clothing boutique or proof of a large local tanning industry. Its stock and capacity are limited by the shop record.

### The Blue Heron

The Blue Heron offers a listed laundry service with capacity for four loads per day at 2 sp per load, returned the next day. This is an established commercial service for travelers and customers able to pay. It is not evidence that all households use professional laundries or that Tallowmere has a citywide laundry system.

### Household care and repair

Households may wash smaller items themselves, share washing arrangements, or pay someone for difficult or bulky loads. Mending, patching, altering, turning garments, replacing fastenings, and repairing footwear extend the life of clothing.

No complete roster of Tallowmere weavers, spinners, dyers, fullers, cobblers, shoemakers, laundresses, or soapmakers is established. Do not create these providers simply because a medieval town would need the work; some may be unnamed or supplied through trade, but their specific identities and arrangements remain open.

## 8. Washing, repair, and the life of cloth

Washing clothing requires water, cleaning materials, space, time, and sometimes fuel for hot water. Heavy wool garments and outer layers are difficult to wash and dry. Linen underlayers and work cloth may be washed more often.

Damp clothing can remain uncomfortable or unusable in cold or rainy weather. A crowded home may have little room to dry bedding and garments without making the interior damp. A traveler may pay for laundry because wet clothing is miserable to wear and difficult to dry on the road.

Common repair includes:
- patching holes and reinforcing seams;
- replacing buttons, ties, laces, hooks, and other fastenings;
- turning or relining a garment where practical;
- resizing clothing for a new wearer;
- repairing soles, straps, and leather panels;
- using worn fabric for household cloth or patches.

A garment may be valuable because of its fit, memories, family association, or quality, not merely because it is expensive. A patched coat may be a cherished possession. A fine garment can be a loan, inheritance, gift, or political statement.

## 9. Personal adornment and social signals

Personal adornment includes jewelry, belts, clasps, brooches, pins, ribbons, badges, religious tokens, family marks, and carefully maintained accessories.

A small object may carry more meaning than an expensive garment. An old clasp may identify a family; a religious token may mark devotion; a badge may indicate office or service; a mourning ribbon may signal a recent death.

Do not assume that every color, symbol, or ornament has a universal meaning. The significance of a garment or accessory depends on local custom, the wearer, the occasion, and who recognizes it.

Personal grooming also communicates care and status. Hair, beards, nails, skin, scent, and cleanliness vary with occupation, available water, time, fashion, religious practice, and wealth. A laborer may be clean before a formal meeting and filthy after a shift. A noble may employ attendants but still travel in weather that ruins a carefully prepared outfit.

## 10. GM portrayal

When clothing matters in a scene, consider:
1. What work does the person do, and what marks would that work leave?
2. What weather and terrain must the garment withstand?
3. Is the clothing for work, travel, worship, ceremony, court, or ordinary home life?
4. Is it new, maintained, altered, inherited, borrowed, or patched?
5. What does the material, fit, color, or ornament communicate to the people present?
6. Can the wearer afford to replace it, or would damage be a real loss?
7. Does the character know the local custom, or are they misreading a signal?

Use clothing to support characterization and the setting's material reality. Do not make every garment a coded faction uniform or every poor person visibly ragged.

## 11. Open decisions

The following remain unresolved:
- exact textile crops and local production areas;
- regional weaving, spinning, fulling, and dyeing centers;
- common local garment names and patterns;
- availability and price of particular imported fabrics;
- local footwear and cobbling practices beyond named shop stock;
- named dyers, weavers, fullers, laundresses, and soapmakers;
- any formal dress codes, sumptuary rules, or legally protected insignia;
- exact meanings of colors, family marks, and ceremonial accessories beyond specific established examples;
- regional variation in grooming customs and everyday adornment.

Do not invent a universal fashion code or a complete textile industry merely to close these questions. Develop details when a character, region, social event, or economic dispute makes them relevant.
