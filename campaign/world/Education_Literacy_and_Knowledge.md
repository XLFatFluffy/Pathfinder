# Education, Literacy, and the Life of the Mind

**Status:** GM reference; broad social patterns established, institutional details partly unresolved  
**Baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY  
**Related records:** [Cultures and Societies](../Cultures_and_Societies.md), [Everyday Life and Material Culture](Everyday_Life_and_Material_Culture.md), [Religion and Faith](../religions/Religion_and_Faith.md), [Crownlands and Valedorn Culture](../cultures/Crownlands_and_Valedorn_Culture.md), [Vaelorian Culture and Homelands](../cultures/Vaelorian_Culture_and_Homelands.md), [Kharadorn Dwarven Culture and Khar-Dum](../cultures/Kharadorn_Dwarven_Culture_and_Khar_Dum.md)

Education in this setting is not a universal public system. Knowledge is transmitted through households, work, apprenticeships, temples, noble households, military service, commercial practice, archives, and specialist scholarly networks. Literacy is valuable but not universal. Formal schooling is uneven, expensive, and shaped by class, region, ancestry, occupation, and patronage.

The goal is not to decide how many people can read in every village. The goal is to understand who needs particular skills, how they learn them, who controls access, and what knowledge can realistically travel.

## 1. The shape of education

There is no single continental school system, ministry of education, or universal curriculum. Most people learn what they need through family and work. Formal instruction is more likely where a household, institution, employer, temple, or patron can support it.

Common routes into knowledge include:

- **Household teaching:** parents, grandparents, older siblings, and relatives teach practical skills, customs, religious practice, stories, and household accounts.
- **Apprenticeship:** a craftsperson or merchant teaches a learner through supervised work. Practical competence matters more than abstract examination.
- **Noble household instruction:** tutors and household officers teach children expected to inherit land, command people, negotiate, or administer an estate.
- **Temple and religious instruction:** clergy teach doctrine, ritual, reading where available, charity, moral teaching, and the maintenance of religious records.
- **Commercial learning:** merchants, factors, scribes, moneychangers, and shipmasters teach arithmetic, weights, measures, contracts, navigation, and account keeping.
- **Military instruction:** soldiers and household officers teach weapons, riding, discipline, command, engineering, logistics, and the duties of service.
- **Specialist scholarship:** trained scholars preserve and interpret records, languages, mathematics, medicine, law, natural philosophy, astronomy, and history.
- **Self-directed learning:** motivated adults may learn from books, observation, correspondence, travel, or the help of a literate friend, but access to texts and teachers is uneven.

A person may be highly skilled without being literate. A master carpenter, shepherd, sailor, midwife, or veteran may possess deep technical knowledge learned through practice and memory. Conversely, the ability to read does not make someone wise, honest, or competent.

## 2. Literacy and numeracy

Literacy is not universal. No reliable continental percentage or regional census is established.

Reading and writing are separate skills. Some people can recognize names, signs, prayers, or common account marks but cannot compose a letter. Others can read fluently but write slowly or awkwardly. A merchant may be comfortable with figures and contracts while having little interest in poetry or theology.

Numeracy is often more widespread in practical forms than full literacy. Farmers estimate seed, land, labor, livestock, and stores. Carters judge loads and distance. Builders measure timber and stone. Merchants calculate costs, weights, interest, shares, and exchange. A person may be very capable with numbers without using written arithmetic.

### Who is likely to be literate?

The following are broad tendencies, not guarantees:

- Noble heirs and children of wealthy households are likely to receive formal instruction in reading, writing, accounts, law, history, and languages.
- Senior household officers, stewards, reeves, clerks, judges, and administrators often need practical literacy.
- Merchants, lenders, factors, shipmasters, and established craft masters may use written contracts and accounts.
- Scribes, stationers, legal clerks, scholars, and many senior clergy require literacy as part of their work.
- Some soldiers, officers, engineers, and quartermasters learn reading, measurement, and accounts for practical reasons.
- Rural households may have one literate member who handles correspondence, rents, receipts, or dealings with officials.
- Many laborers, servants, fishers, and smallholders may rely on memory, oral agreements, marks, witnesses, or a trusted scribe.

Do not assume that a commoner is illiterate merely because they are poor, or that every noble can read complex legal documents. Education depends on opportunity, household priorities, aptitude, and practical need.

## 3. Childhood learning

Children learn through participation. They observe work long before they are trusted with its full responsibility.

A rural child may learn:
- animal care, milking, feeding, and seasonal work;
- seed, soil, weather signs, and field boundaries;
- gathering, wood, water, fuel, and household repair;
- food preparation and preservation;
- local customs, family obligations, and religious observances;
- simple counting, measures, and trade when the household needs them.

An urban child may learn:
- household work and market customs;
- a parent's or employer's trade;
- handling coins, counting stock, and recognizing measures;
- reading or writing if accessible;
- shop work, errands, delivery, and customer behavior;
- the rules and expectations of the child's district, guild, or household.

Children of nobles receive more formal instruction, but their education is not purely academic. They may be taught riding, weapons, etiquette, lineage, religion, estate administration, diplomacy, law, and the management of servants and retainers. A future ruler must learn how to command and negotiate as well as how to read.

Children still play, work, argue, imitate adults, and make mistakes. Childhood is not a miniature version of formal adult life.

## 4. Apprenticeship and occupational learning

Apprenticeship is a major route to skilled work. It may be formalized by a guild, craft association, merchant house, or written agreement, or it may be an informal arrangement within a family or workshop.

An apprentice learns by:
- watching skilled work;
- preparing tools and materials;
- performing simple tasks;
- repeating procedures under supervision;
- learning measurements, quality standards, and safety;
- dealing with suppliers and customers;
- understanding the reputation and obligations of the trade.

An apprentice is not automatically a child. Adults may change trades, enter a new workshop, or learn a specialized skill later in life, although doing so can be expensive and difficult.

A craft master may teach practical knowledge without teaching reading. A literate merchant may understand contracts but have little skill in ship repair or metalwork. The GM should treat occupational expertise as its own form of knowledge.

The existing Tallowmere guild and workshop records establish local apprenticeship and craft relationships. They do not establish a single universal guild law across the continent.

## 5. Noble and administrative education

Noble education prepares a person for their expected role. A future landholder may study:
- family genealogy and succession;
- charters, homage, land tenure, and customary obligations;
- rents, stores, accounts, and estate management;
- local law, petitions, and arbitration;
- military service, fortification, and logistics;
- diplomacy, etiquette, and court behavior;
- history, geography, languages, and religion.

The depth of instruction varies. A minor house may rely on a family member, steward, chaplain, or neighboring patron. A Great House can draw on tutors, archives, senior officers, and specialist scholars, but wealth does not guarantee that every heir is diligent or well taught.

Administrative literacy matters because government relies on written orders, seals, charters, accounts, appointments, and correspondence. A clerk who understands the form of a document may have considerable practical influence even without noble rank.

The Crownlands and Valedorn contain schools and archives among their urban institutions. Their names, admission rules, curricula, and scale remain unresolved. Do not invent a single royal academy or assume that every clerk attended one.

## 6. Religious education

Temples and religious institutions may teach doctrine, ritual, reading, moral instruction, charity, and the preservation of records. The form and extent of instruction vary by faith, institution, local resources, and the clergy involved.

Religious education may include:
- prayers, hymns, and sacred stories;
- observances, festivals, and rites of passage;
- the history and teachings of a faith;
- ethical duties and charitable obligations;
- reading sacred texts where copies and teachers are available;
- care for the poor, sick, travelers, or dead;
- training for clerical service or a religious order.

A temple is not automatically a school. Some institutions may teach children or train novices; others may focus on worship, charity, burial, or pastoral care. Religious literacy does not automatically imply broad academic learning.

Clergy are not automatically spellcasters. The gods are real, but divine magic remains uncommon and its precise mechanism is unresolved.

## 7. The Collegium of the Nine Lamps

The **Collegium of the Nine Lamps** is the established name of an independent scholarly tradition. It is not a magical academy and does not provide a standardized education in wizardry or arcane spellcasting. Its scholars may preserve, copy, translate, or study old magical texts as part of broader scholarship, but access to such texts does not make the Collegium a formal school for spellcasters. Arcane instruction remains private and uncommon, usually passed through apprenticeship or pursued independently from rare manuscripts. It is a loose network of scholars, archives, tutors, translators, genealogists, mathematicians, physicians, legal historians, and natural philosophers. It is not a single university that controls all scholarship.

Its stated principles include:
- preserving records;
- distinguishing evidence from speculation;
- training competent scribes and scholars;
- maintaining copies of important texts;
- providing expertise to rulers, merchants, temples, and wealthy households.

The Collegium attempts to remain institutionally independent. Great Houses patronize individual scholars and archives, creating unavoidable political pressure. Its scholars are not automatically neutral or correct; their records can contain omissions, bias, lost sources, and competing interpretations.

The Collegium accepts students and scholars from outside Vaelorian culture, although wealthy Vaelorian patronage has historically influenced some archives and institutions.

**Still unresolved:** its internal ranks, training system, governing body, geographic reach, local houses or chapters, admission customs, and formal relationship with the Crown. Do not turn it into a centrally administered university or a secret order.

## 8. Regional educational character

### Vaelorian

Education is strongly associated with lineage, precedent, craft, scholarship, and long-term family reputation. Wealthy families may teach reading, writing, history, genealogy, accounting, law, languages, music, mathematics, etiquette, and geography. Common families may prioritize literacy, trade skills, agriculture, craft, or practical accounting.

Long lifespans allow some family knowledge to be transmitted directly across generations. They do not remove economic necessity or guarantee that every elf receives extensive schooling.

### Kharadorn

Children learn practical skills and clan history. Formal instruction may include reading, arithmetic, measurement, engineering principles, law, genealogy, religious practice, and craft apprenticeship.

Education is expected to connect knowledge with responsibility. A young dwarf may be expected to understand why a bridge is safe, how a tool should be maintained, and what a family record means before being entrusted with major work.

This does not mean every dwarf is an engineer or that all clan instruction follows one curriculum.

### Veyr and the Northern Holds

Education is shaped by the needs of frontier households, local administration, military service, and survival in difficult conditions. Practical knowledge of weather, routes, stores, animals, repairs, and the Barrier's maintenance is valuable. Officers and officials need records and correspondence, but no universal northern school system is established.

### Edarth and the Western Heartlands

Education is closely tied to household duty, landholding, estate management, military obligation, agriculture, and customary law. Noble heirs need to understand rents, tenants, oaths, service, and local authority. Rural families teach practical work and seasonal knowledge through participation.

### Merrowen and maritime communities

Practical education may emphasize navigation, tides, weather signs, vessel handling, port customs, cargo, weights, measures, and commercial correspondence. A skilled pilot or shipmaster may possess specialized knowledge that is not available in books. No single Merrowen naval academy or universal pilotage school is established.

### Crownlands and Valedorn

The capital's administrative, legal, commercial, and scholarly institutions create demand for clerks, scribes, advocates, tutors, translators, and educated officials. Schools and archives exist as part of the city's institutional landscape, but their individual identities and admission systems remain open.

### Tallowmere

Tallowmere's shops, legal offices, temples, guilds, and merchant houses create practical demand for literacy and numeracy. Pell & Daughter provides scribal and stationer services; it is not a school. The city has tutors and skilled tradespeople in its social fabric, but no named public school, formal academy, or comprehensive local education system is established.

## 9. Books, records, and the cost of knowledge

Books are labor-intensive objects. A manuscript requires materials, a trained hand, time, copying, correction, and storage. A wealthy institution may own a library, but that does not mean its books are available for public borrowing.

Common forms of written material include:
- charters and legal instruments;
- household accounts and rent rolls;
- merchant ledgers and contracts;
- letters and sealed correspondence;
- religious texts and service books;
- genealogies and family histories;
- maps, itineraries, and navigational notes;
- medical and craft manuals;
- chronicles, poems, songs, and copied stories.

Documents may be written on parchment, paper, wax tablets, boards, or other available surfaces according to purpose and local practice. The precise availability and cost of paper, ink, parchment, and books varies; do not assume every village has a bookshop.

Written records can be lost, damaged, forged, miscopied, altered, or deliberately withheld. An old document is not automatically authentic. A seal may establish an issuing authority without proving that the underlying claim is true.

## 10. Languages, translation, and specialist knowledge

The campaign has not yet established a complete language map, formal scripts, or universal language policy. Do not invent a detailed linguistic taxonomy merely to populate this reference.

The Collegium's translators and the presence of multiple ancestries and political traditions establish a need for translation and interpretation. A translator may know a language but not a specialized legal, religious, nautical, or technical vocabulary.

Knowledge is often divided by specialization:
- a genealogist may understand family records but not military logistics;
- a pilot may read weather and coastlines but not legal charters;
- a physician may understand anatomy but not divine magic;
- a temple scholar may know theology but not every local custom;
- a steward may understand accounts and rents but not the law of a distant principality.

Expertise is bounded. A knowledgeable NPC should be useful within their field and uncertain outside it.

## 11. Education, status, and social mobility

Education can create opportunity, but it does not erase class, ancestry prejudice, wealth, patronage, or legal status.

A talented commoner may become a respected craft master, clerk, physician, shipmaster, or scholar. Advancement may depend on a patron, a guild, a temple, a noble household, or a rare opportunity. A literate servant may gain influence through access and competence without acquiring noble status.

Women may be educated where family custom, wealth, institutional opportunity, and personal circumstances allow. Existing female rulers, heirs, merchants, scholars, and administrators establish that formal learning and authority are not exclusively male. This does not establish universal equality of access.

Ratfolk face widespread prejudice in surface societies. Their own underground communities preserve practical and cultural knowledge through family, neighborhood, craft, and mutual-aid networks. Do not assume surface institutions provide them equal access to tutors, guilds, archives, or formal education.

## 12. Knowledge and player characters

When deciding what a character knows, distinguish:
- **Common knowledge:** information most people in the relevant society would know.
- **Local knowledge:** facts learned by living or working in a particular place.
- **Occupational knowledge:** skills and information learned through a trade or service.
- **Educated knowledge:** information likely to be available to a person with formal instruction.
- **Specialist knowledge:** information requiring focused study or professional experience.
- **Rumor or belief:** something the character has heard, which may be wrong.
- **Secret knowledge:** information restricted by access, secrecy, or deliberate concealment.

A character's ancestry alone does not grant every cultural fact. Upbringing, class, education, occupation, travel, and backstory determine what they reasonably know. Apply Pathfinder skill checks where appropriate, but do not make a player roll to know ordinary facts their character would plainly have learned.

## 13. Open decisions

- The full language and script landscape of the continent.
- The Collegium's formal ranks, training, governing body, regional presence, and admission customs.
- Names and locations of specific schools, archives, libraries, and tutors.
- How common basic literacy is in each region and class.
- The extent of temple-based instruction and charitable schooling.
- The formal relationship between guild apprenticeship and civic regulation.
- The availability and cost of books, writing materials, and private tutors.
- The education of specific Great House heirs beyond what their dossiers establish.
- Whether any regional society has distinctive examinations, scholarly titles, or professional credentials.

These questions can remain open until a character, location, institution, or campaign event makes them relevant. The setting needs believable routes into knowledge, not a fictional education ministry.
