# Everyday Life and Material Culture

**Status:** GM-approved worldbuilding expansion  
**Baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC

This record establishes ordinary life at the level players can actually feel at the table. It is deliberately practical. Not every detail is universal, and local custom always matters.

## 1. The basic rhythm of life

Most people organize life around daylight, work, weather, food, family obligations, religious observance, and local authority.

A farmer's year is shaped by planting, maintenance, harvest, slaughter, preservation, and winter preparation.

A merchant's year is shaped by contracts, markets, shipping seasons, credit, taxes, and road conditions.

A noble's year is shaped by estate management, court appearances, marriages, military obligations, hospitality, correspondence, and political relationships.

A sailor's year is shaped by weather, tides, cargo, ship maintenance, and the risks of travel.

The calendar provides dates. Actual life is governed by what must be done before nightfall.

## 2. Food

### Common breakfast

A working household may begin the day with:

- porridge;
- bread;
- cheese;
- leftover stew;
- fruit when available;
- eggs where affordable;
- weak beer or small beer;
- water or watered wine in wealthier households.

Breakfast is usually practical rather than ceremonial. For the established local supply chain, named food businesses, household patterns, and public eating in Tallowmere, see [Foodways of Tallowmere and Its Hinterland](Foodways_of_Tallowmere_and_the_Hinterland.md).

### Common meals

Stews are important because they efficiently combine grains, vegetables, beans, meat scraps, or fish.

Bread quality varies dramatically by wealth.

The poorest households may rely heavily on coarse grains, beans, onions, cabbage, turnips, and whatever meat or fish can be obtained cheaply.

Middle-class households eat more varied food and preserve more meat, cheese, fruit, and fish.

Noble households can afford fresh meat more regularly, imported spices, fine wines, sugar or other luxury ingredients where trade permits, and specialist cooks.

## 3. Food preservation

Without refrigeration, households rely on:

- salting;
- smoking;
- drying;
- pickling;
- fermenting;
- storing grain;
- root cellars;
- sealed jars;
- cool storage rooms;
- winter ice where locally available.

A failed harvest is therefore not simply an agricultural problem. It is a storage problem, a price problem, a transport problem, and eventually a political problem.

## 4. Drinking water

Clean water is valuable.

Cities use wells, cisterns, rivers, springs, and managed waterworks where available.

People distinguish between water that looks clean and water known to be safe.

Beer, wine, fermented drinks, and boiled beverages may be used partly because they are familiar and easier to store, but this should not be treated as a universal rule that medieval people never drank water.

## 5. Childhood

Children are expected to contribute to household life.

A rural child may learn:

- animal care;
- planting;
- harvesting;
- tool maintenance;
- cooking;
- gathering;
- basic accounts.

An urban child may learn:

- household work;
- a parent's trade;
- market customs;
- reading or writing if accessible;
- guild skills.

Noble children receive more formal education but also face expectations involving etiquette, lineage, religion, riding, weapons, administration, and marriage.

Children still play.

Common games include throwing games, races, dice, board games, guessing games, songs, wrestling, mock fighting, and improvised toys.

## 6. Marriage

Marriage is simultaneously personal, economic, religious, and political.

Among commoners, practical concerns include:

- household labor;
- property;
- dowry or bridewealth customs;
- inheritance;
- family approval;
- ability to support children.

Among nobles, marriage may also determine alliances, claims, inheritance, military obligations, and succession.

Marriage customs vary by faith and region. There is no single continental marriage law.

## 7. Death and funerals

Death creates immediate practical work.

Someone must:

- notify relatives;
- prepare the body;
- arrange religious rites;
- determine burial or other approved disposition;
- preserve or distribute property;
- settle debts;
- record inheritance;
- care for dependents.

Funerals may be solemn, communal, private, religious, or heavily shaped by local tradition.

The dead are remembered because inheritance and identity depend on remembering who came before.

## 8. Medicine

Medical knowledge is uneven.

Common treatment includes:

- cleaning wounds;
- bandaging;
- splinting;
- herbal preparations;
- rest;
- diet;
- heat or cold;
- basic surgical practice;
- midwifery;
- setting broken bones.

Some physicians and healers possess substantial practical knowledge.

Magic exists but is not routine medical infrastructure. A person cannot assume that a temple can provide magical healing on demand.

A wealthy noble may employ a physician. A village may rely on an experienced midwife, healer, herbalist, or barber-surgeon.

## 9. Illness

For the fuller GM treatment of caregivers, learned medicine, injury, disease, sanitation, childbirth, disability, class access, and Pathfinder healing, see [Medicine, Disease, and Healing](Medicine_Disease_and_Healing.md). The details in that reference distinguish proposed practice from established local institutions.


People understand disease through a mixture of observation, tradition, religion, and incorrect theories.

Common responses include:

- isolation;
- cleaning;
- burning contaminated materials;
- herbal remedies;
- prayer;
- dietary changes;
- calling a physician or priest;
- appealing to local authorities during outbreaks.

Superstition and useful practice may coexist in the same household.

## 10. Clothing

Clothing communicates occupation and status.

Common people favor durable wool, linen, leather, and locally produced textiles.

Merchants and craftsmen may own better-quality dyed cloth.

Nobles can afford:

- finer cloth;
- elaborate embroidery;
- imported textiles;
- jewelry;
- fur;
- specialized tailoring.

A person's clothing also indicates whether they work with animals, soil, metal, food, ships, or books.

## 11. Work

Most people work.

A city may contain:

- smiths;
- carpenters;
- masons;
- tanners;
- bakers;
- brewers;
- tailors;
- potters;
- chandlers;
- shipwrights;
- scribes;
- moneylenders;
- merchants;
- guards;
- porters;
- laborers;
- physicians;
- entertainers;
- prostitutes;
- priests;
- scholars.

Not every occupation exists at every settlement.

## 12. Money and credit

Coinage exists, but daily exchange does not require everyone to carry large quantities of coin.

Common transactions may use:

- copper and silver coins;
- barter;
- credit;
- written obligations;
- account entries;
- wages paid periodically.

Large merchants may conduct business through letters of credit, debt instruments, contracts, and account books.

Exact prices should be established only when relevant to play. Do not pretend that every tavern meal has a universal price independent of region, season, scarcity, and quality.

## 13. News and communication

Most people learn news through:

- travelers;
- merchants;
- sailors;
- priests;
- soldiers;
- market gossip;
- public announcements;
- letters.

Written correspondence is valuable because literacy is not universal.

A noble household may maintain regular messengers. Merchants may maintain networks of correspondence. Rural families may receive news days or weeks after an event.

This means rumors can travel faster than verified facts.

## 14. Law

People encounter law through:

- local lords;
- magistrates;
- guild authorities;
- village custom;
- noble courts;
- Crown courts;
- religious institutions in matters of internal religious authority.

A person's social status can strongly affect how quickly a dispute is heard and what remedies are available.

Written law matters, but custom matters too.

## 15. Crime

Common crimes include:

- theft;
- fraud;
- assault;
- poaching;
- smuggling;
- trespass;
- illegal debt collection;
- counterfeiting;
- tax evasion;
- property disputes.

Punishment varies by jurisdiction and social status.

A minor theft in a poor village and a major theft from a noble warehouse may be treated very differently.

## 16. Fire

Fire is one of the most serious urban hazards.

Cities therefore maintain some combination of:

- wells and water barrels;
- bucket lines;
- watchmen;
- firebreaks;
- rules concerning open flame;
- masonry construction;
- controlled hearths.

Poorer districts with crowded timber structures are more vulnerable.

A warehouse fire can become a political event if it destroys tax records, trade goods, military stores, or evidence.

## 17. Horses and animals

A horse is valuable property.

Owners care about:

- feed;
- hoof care;
- saddle fit;
- injuries;
- breeding;
- stable conditions.

A dead horse is not simply an inconvenience. It may represent lost transport, lost trade capacity, or a serious financial blow.

Dogs, cats, chickens, goats, pigs, sheep, cattle, and working animals are common according to region and household wealth.

## 18. Streets and sanitation

Urban cleanliness is a constant labor problem.

Cities use:

- drains;
- ditches;
- gutters;
- cesspits;
- collection labor;
- designated dumping areas;
- private household cleaning.

Conditions vary greatly by district.

A wealthy street may be maintained carefully while a crowded old quarter is muddy, foul-smelling, and difficult to clean.

## 19. Entertainment

People seek entertainment because apparently even medieval humanity refuses to spend every waking hour repairing roofs.

Common entertainment includes:

- music;
- dancing;
- storytelling;
- drinking;
- gambling;
- wrestling;
- races;
- fairs;
- religious festivals;
- public executions where permitted;
- theater or dramatic performances;
- board and dice games.

Nobles may sponsor performers, hunts, tournaments, feasts, and elaborate celebrations.

## 20. Social class

Class is visible in:

- clothing;
- housing;
- speech;
- education;
- diet;
- legal access;
- ability to travel;
- ability to survive bad harvests;
- marriage options.

A wealthy merchant may live better than a poor knight while still lacking noble title.

A noble may be land-rich but cash-poor.

A priest may have social authority without personal wealth.

## 21. Hospitality

Hospitality is important because travelers can be dangerous, useful, or simply desperate.

A household may offer:

- water;
- food;
- a place by the fire;
- stable space;
- directions;
- local news.

Noble hospitality is formal and politically meaningful.

Breaking hospitality expectations can damage reputation even when no formal law has been violated.

## 22. What people fear

Ordinary fears include:

- hunger;
- fire;
- disease;
- bandits;
- abusive officials;
- failed harvests;
- war;
- debt;
- losing children;
- losing land;
- storms;
- dangerous animals;
- supernatural threats.

Most people do not wake up expecting a monster attack.

They wake up expecting work.

That distinction matters. The supernatural is frightening partly because it violates the normal assumptions on which ordinary life depends.
