# Foodways of Tallowmere and Its Hinterland

**Status:** GM reference; established local food businesses and supply relationships with illustrative household patterns  
**Snapshot:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY  
**Scope:** Tallowmere, the River Vale settlements, Northfields, and the Eastern Shore  
**Related records:** [Agriculture, Food Supply, and Seasonal Work](Agriculture_Food_and_Seasonal_Work.md); [Everyday Life and Material Culture](Everyday_Life_and_Material_Culture.md); [Tallowmere Shops, Stock, and Prices](../locations/starting_city/Shopkeepers_and_Stock.md); [Regional Settlements and Landholders](../locations/starting_city/Regional_Settlements_and_Landholders.md)

## 1. Purpose and authority

Food is one of the easiest ways to make the setting feel inhabited. It tells players what grows nearby, what travels by river or sea, who has money, who works long hours, what a household can preserve, and what becomes scarce when a route is disrupted.

This document synthesizes established local food sources and named Tallowmere businesses. It does not establish a single cuisine shared by every household. The meals below are practical portrayals drawn from approved food inventories and the campaign's agricultural framework. Where a detail is not already in a local record, it is marked as a likely pattern or left open.

The following local foundations are established:
- Tallowmere is a regional port and River Vale trade hub with a population of approximately 18,000. It depends on a wider food catchment, river traffic, coastal shipping, market exchange, and stored food.
- Millhaven collects grain and supplies flour, oats, and seasonal legumes. Its Long Mill and storage sheds are important parts of the local food chain.
- Nearby farms and estates provide livestock, dairy, orchard produce, and other agricultural goods.
- Saltwick supplies fresh and preserved fish. Its small boats, net lofts, smokehouses, and seasonal salt handling are established.
- Brackenholt supplies charcoal, firewood, poles, and rough timber. Fuel is part of food production because ovens, hearths, boiling, brewing, and preservation all consume it.
- Tallowmere's named food businesses include the Copper Ladle, the Blue Heron, the Crooked Oar, Three Grain Bakery, and Salt & Root Provisioners. Their listed inventories are opening reference stock, not a promise of endless supply.
- Existing local menus include coarse bread, bean and onion stew, fish stew, sausage, cheese, bread and dripping, onion and fish pie, meat pies, flatbread, ship biscuit, smoked fish, dried beans, dried fruit, salt, and ship provisions.

## 2. The food supply chain

A meal in Tallowmere may have passed through several hands before reaching the table.

### Grain and bread

Grain is gathered from farms and transported to Millhaven and other local stores. Milling turns it into flour or meal; bakers then use fuel, labor, ovens, and reliable deliveries to produce bread and pies. The Three Grain Bakery's small and coarse loaves, white loaves, flatbread, meat pies, sweet buns, and ship biscuit illustrate different grades and uses.

Bread quality is visible social information. Fine white bread requires better flour and costs more. Coarser wheat or rye loaves are more economical. A household's bread tells something about its means, but not everything: a laborer may buy better bread on payday or for a feast, while a wealthy household may deliberately serve coarse bread to workers.

### Fish and shore produce

Saltwick supplies fresh and preserved fish to Tallowmere and other shore settlements. Fresh catch is more dependent on weather, boats, handling, and the speed of transport. Smokehouses and salt handling make fish available beyond the day it is caught.

The Copper Ladle's fish stew and the Crooked Oar's onion and fish pie are examples of how a changing catch can enter ordinary cooked food. Salt & Root's smoked fish is a more durable provision. Exact fish species remain unresolved.

### Meat, dairy, and livestock

Nearby farms and estates supply livestock and dairy. Meat may be eaten fresh after slaughter or preserved by salting, smoking, drying, or other suitable methods. Cheese travels and stores more readily than fresh milk. Sausage and meat pies can use cuts or trimmings that are less convenient to sell as premium fresh meat.

Do not assume every household eats meat daily. Animals are wealth, labor, breeding stock, and sources of milk, wool, hides, and manure. Slaughtering a productive animal has a cost beyond the immediate meal. For the wider household, transport, and labor value of domestic animals, see [Animal Husbandry and Working Animals](Animal_Husbandry_and_Working_Animals.md).

### Vegetables, fruit, and legumes

The Northfields and nearby holdings provide seasonal legumes, orchard produce, and cultivated food. The exact varieties and harvest volumes remain open. Beans and onions already appear in the Copper Ladle's menu. Dried beans and dried fruit appear in Salt & Root's stock.

Fresh produce is more seasonal and perishable than grain or dried staples. A household with a garden may eat what is ready; a city household without growing space relies on market purchases, preserved food, or suppliers.

### Fuel and cooking

Brackenholt's charcoal and firewood supply supports domestic hearths, bakeries, taverns, brewers, and food preservation. Fuel cost and availability shape what can be cooked. A dish that requires long simmering consumes more fuel than bread reheated or food cooked quickly over a small fire.

Fuel is not an invisible unlimited resource. Households use hearths efficiently, share ovens where custom allows, and make use of residual heat. The exact arrangements of Tallowmere's communal ovens and neighborhood fuel contracts are not established.

## 3. What people eat

Meals vary with household wealth, work, season, health, and access to markets. These are patterns for portrayal, not fixed menus for every person.

### Poor households and day laborers

A poor household is likely to rely heavily on coarse bread, porridge or other grain preparations, beans, onions, cabbage or other hardy vegetables when available, and small quantities of fat or preserved food. Stew stretches ingredients and can be reheated or shared. Fish scraps, salted fish, cheese, or sausage may add flavor and protein when affordable.

The Copper Ladle's bean and onion stew with coarse bread is a useful public example of filling, inexpensive food. Bread and dripping at the Crooked Oar is another simple dockside meal. A meal may be hot and satisfying without containing much meat.

### Artisans, clerks, and modestly comfortable households

A household with steadier income can afford more varied bread, cheese, eggs, vegetables, occasional meat or fish, and better-preserved stores. Meals may include a pot of stew, a pie, or bread with a cooked accompaniment. The household may buy prepared food during busy workdays rather than cook every meal.

The distinction from poorer households is often frequency, variety, quality, and security of supply rather than completely different ingredients.

### Sailors, carters, and travelers

Travel food must survive handling and time. Bread, hard cheese, onions, salted meat, dried beans, dried fruit, and smoked fish are practical. Ship biscuit is hard-baked for storage. Water and drinkable provisions require suitable containers and planning.

Salt & Root's one-person weekly ship provision bundle is a listed commercial product, not a universal ration standard. Its stock demonstrates that Tallowmere has a market for durable food assembled for crews and expeditions.

A sailor ashore may seek hot stew, fresh bread, and a drink rather than another meal of preserved provisions. The Crooked Oar caters to working crews; the Blue Heron offers a cleaner, more expensive dining room for established sailors and merchants.

### Wealthy and noble households

Wealthier households can afford more fresh meat, better cuts, finer bread, varied dairy, seasonal fruit, imported ingredients where trade permits, and specialist cooks. They can also maintain stores and buy from several suppliers. Their advantage is not merely luxurious flavor: they have more options when a particular food becomes scarce.

A noble household may serve a formal meal with multiple dishes and controlled presentation, but no single continental feast menu is established. Local produce, guest status, religious observance, season, and household fashion shape the table.

Do not assume every noble eats extravagantly at every meal. Estate management, travel, military service, and personal preference can produce plain meals even in wealthy households.

## 4. Meals through an ordinary day

A working person's meal schedule follows labor and daylight more than a rigid clock.

- **First meal:** bread, porridge, cheese, leftovers, fruit when available, or eggs where affordable. A worker leaving early may eat quickly before going to a field, quay, workshop, or boat.
- **Midday meal:** bread and cheese, a packed meal, leftovers, a market purchase, or a hot bowl from a food stall. Work crews may eat together if the job and weather allow.
- **Evening meal:** the main cooked meal for many households, often a stew, pie, cooked grain, vegetables, or fish/meat when available. The pot can be adjusted to what remains in the larder.
- **Between meals:** laborers may eat bread, fruit, cheese, or other portable food. Wealthier households may have more formal service and additional courses.

This is a useful pattern, not a universal schedule. Ship crews, guards, bakers, night watch, and harvest workers eat when shifts and tasks permit.

## 5. Eating in public

Tallowmere's named establishments show different relationships between food, price, and status.

### The Copper Ladle

Della Rusk's Market Ward stall serves dockworkers, servants, travelers, apprentices, and others who need a hot meal at low cost. Its listed menu includes coarse bread, bean and onion stew, fish stew, sausage and bread, hard cheese, small beer, seasonal spiced cider, and packed travel meals.

The stall closes when the day's food is gone. Its menu and prices can respond to deliveries and scarcity. It is a practical place to hear market talk because customers come from many working occupations, but it is not a neutral or complete source of city opinion.

### The Crooked Oar

Pellik Sorn's New Quays tavern serves dock laborers, sailors between voyages, and casual crews. Its food includes bread and dripping, onion and fish pie, and sausage plates. It is loud, crowded, and inexpensive. It is a working tavern, not automatically a criminal den.

### The Blue Heron

Veyra Dane's Old Quays inn serves established sailors, small merchants, visiting factors, and travelers seeking cleaner accommodation. Its meal prices distinguish poor, common, and good meals. Meat or fish appears in the common meal when available. Veyra's experience as a ship's cook is part of the establishment's character.

### Three Grain Bakery

Hesta Pellor's bakery supplies households, taverns, and ship crews. It rises before dawn and sells several bread grades, flatbread, pies, sweet buns, and ship biscuit. Bulk orders require notice and a deposit. Flour prices and river traffic affect what she can offer.

### Salt & Root Provisioners

Ollo Venn and Merra assemble preserved food, grain, salt, oil, and imported staples for shipmasters, caravan leaders, boarding houses, and bulk buyers. They are a provisioning business, not a fresh produce stall. Large orders require notice and may draw stock from multiple suppliers.

These businesses are not the entire city's food economy. They are established examples that help the GM portray distinct customers, supply relationships, and price sensitivity.

## 6. Cooking, preservation, and household equipment

Most households do not have a modern kitchen. Cooking depends on a hearth, fuel, pots, knives, bowls, storage vessels, and the time and skill of whoever prepares food. Wealthier homes may have more space, better equipment, separate kitchen staff, ovens, and specialist cooks. Poorer households may cook in a shared or cramped space.

Common practical techniques include boiling, stewing, baking, roasting, frying where suitable fat is available, salting, smoking, drying, pickling, and fermenting. The method depends on the ingredient and the household's equipment.

Preservation is a form of planning:
- Grain must be kept dry and protected from pests.
- Beans and dried fruit must be stored against damp and vermin.
- Salted and smoked foods need suitable storage and protection from spoilage.
- Cheese and dairy require attention to temperature and handling.
- Fresh fish and meat should be used or preserved promptly.
- Fuel must be reserved for cooking, heating, craft, and winter use.

A household's larder is a record of its resources and foresight. A full grain bin does not guarantee a comfortable winter if the family lacks fuel, fodder, or access to other foods.

## 7. Seasonality and the opening date

The campaign begins on 1 Harvestfall, but the calendar does not guarantee a particular harvest condition or weather event. At the opening, Northfield harvest monitoring is WATCH: expected harvest is adequate but uneven, with reported farm damage and labor shortages. This is not a regional famine or citywide food crisis.

Harvestfall can bring grain, fruit, and other seasonal produce into markets, but it is also a period of intense labor. A household may have food around it and still lack time to prepare elaborate meals. Prices and quality vary by supply, storage, transport, and who is buying.

Duskreach and winter preparation may increase demand for preserved food, fuel, salt, and storage space. This is a broad seasonal expectation, not a confirmed shortage.

## 8. Food as social information

A meal can quietly reveal:
- Whether a household has secure access to land, wages, or credit.
- Whether a family can afford fresh food or relies on preserved stores.
- Whether a worker is eating at home, buying a hot meal, or carrying provisions.
- Whether a host is honoring a guest, managing appearances, or economizing.
- Whether a port is receiving regular deliveries.
- Whether a household has enough fuel and time to cook.
- Whether a person's status is being acknowledged or deliberately ignored.

Food is also a social obligation. Hospitality may require offering a guest something to eat or drink, but the expected generosity varies by relationship, status, custom, and means. A modest household may offer the best it has; a noble host may be judged by the abundance and presentation of a feast.

Do not reduce every meal to a class marker. People have preferences, habits, memories, appetites, and dislikes. A poor household can take pride in its cooking; a rich person can be fussy or indifferent.
