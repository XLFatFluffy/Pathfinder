# Law, Justice, and Crime

**Status:** GM reference; campaign-wide legal principles established, many local procedures remain unresolved  
**Baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY  
**Related records:** [Feudal Law, Land Tenure, and Noble Obligations](../politics/Feudal_Law_and_Landed_Obligations.md), [Everyday Life and Material Culture](Everyday_Life_and_Material_Culture.md), [Medicine, Disease, and Healing](Medicine_Disease_and_Healing.md), [Tallowmere Government and Law](../locations/starting_city/Government_and_Law.md), [Imperial Governance Operating Framework](../politics/Imperial_Governance_Operating_Framework.md)

Law is one of the ways power becomes visible in ordinary life. It governs land, inheritance, debts, trade, violence, public order, and the rights people claim against one another. It is not a single uniform code administered equally across the continent.

The setting's legal order is a patchwork of Crown authority, Great House privileges, local lordships, charters, guild rights, customary law, clan agreements, and religious jurisdiction. A rule may be clear in one town and disputed in another. A lawful judgment may still be difficult to enforce; a powerful person may impose an outcome without making it lawful.

## 1. The sources of law

The following sources may overlap:

- **Royal law and decree:** rules and decisions issued by the monarch and Crown officers within established royal authority.
- **Great House law and privilege:** hereditary rights, territorial administration, local courts, and reserved powers recognized under the post-Rebellion settlement.
- **Feudal charter or grant:** written terms governing landholding, vassalage, service, dues, tolls, rights, and obligations.
- **Local custom:** practices recognized by a community or jurisdiction through long use and accepted authority.
- **Town or market ordinance:** rules governing public order, markets, weights, measures, sanitation, fire safety, harbor operations, and civic works.
- **Guild privilege and trade custom:** rules governing apprenticeship, craft standards, market access, contracts, and internal discipline.
- **Clan agreement:** internal obligations and rights within Kharadorn's independent-clan structure.
- **Religious law and discipline:** rules governing clergy, doctrine, temple property, religious office, and internal disputes within a faith or institution.

These sources are not interchangeable. A guild may regulate its members without possessing general criminal jurisdiction. A temple may discipline its clergy without automatically trying every secular offense. A noble may hold land without owning every customary right exercised upon it.

## 2. Jurisdiction: who has the right to hear a matter?

Jurisdiction depends on the people involved, the location, the subject, and the authority under which the dispute arises.

### Local and community authority

Reeves, stewards, charter officers, guild officers, and community mediators may address ordinary matters delegated to them. Their authority may include collecting dues, hearing minor complaints, recording agreements, arranging restitution, and referring serious disputes upward.

### Lordship courts

A local lord or appointed magistrate may hear matters involving tenants, rents, local offenses, estate boundaries, property, and rights reserved to the lordship. The exact scope depends on the charter and local custom.

### Liege authority

A superior lord may hear disputes involving vassal obligations, charter interpretation, succession recognition, or conflicts between subordinate houses. A liege's authority is real but not unlimited; the legal basis and political costs matter.

### Crown courts

Royal officers and courts address matters within established Crown jurisdiction, including Crown officers, Crown vassals, royal administration, and disputes properly brought before the Crown. The monarch does not automatically administer every Great House territory directly.

### Imperial Council

The Imperial Council is an aristocratic negotiating institution, not a routine appeal court. Its remit includes major inter-house disputes, extraordinary taxation, major military levies and wars, significant treaties, recognition of royal succession, relations with Kharadorn, and laws affecting Great House privileges.

### Religious courts

Religious courts may handle clergy discipline, internal doctrine, temple property, religious office, ceremonial matters, and disputes within an institution. They do not automatically possess jurisdiction over every secular crime.

A dispute may involve more than one authority. A merchant contract may be a private civil matter, a guild matter, and a charter question at the same time. The GM should identify the specific right being claimed before deciding who can hear it.

## 3. Civil disputes and ordinary justice

Most encounters with law are not dramatic criminal trials. They involve obligations, property, access, and money.

Common civil disputes include:
- unpaid debts and broken contracts;
- disputed wages or shares;
- inheritance and marriage settlements;
- tenancy, rent, and boundary disagreements;
- grazing, woodland, water, fishing, and quarry rights;
- market measures, quality, and fraudulent sales;
- harbor fees, cargo loss, salvage, and damage;
- guild membership, apprenticeship, and trade privileges;
- road, bridge, ferry, and toll obligations.

Many such disputes are settled through negotiation, witnesses, written records, sureties, arbitration, or restitution. A formal hearing may be costly and slow. A wealthy party may prefer to negotiate; a poor party may have little choice but to accept an unfavorable settlement.

The law's practical value depends on access. A person may possess a valid claim but lack the money, literacy, witnesses, social standing, or political support needed to press it.

## 4. Crime and public order

Common offenses in towns and rural communities include:
- theft, burglary, and livestock stealing;
- fraud, false measures, and forged documents;
- assault, threats, and unlawful injury;
- murder and manslaughter;
- arson and reckless fire-setting;
- trespass, poaching, and unlawful cutting;
- smuggling, evasion of tolls, and harbor violations;
- counterfeiting and fraudulent coin;
- illegal debt collection and coercion;
- damage to roads, bridges, mills, ships, and public works;
- disorder, public violence, and interference with lawful officials.

The same act may be treated differently depending on location, harm, status, and the rights involved. Poaching a lord's reserved deer, taking a neighbor's chicken, and killing an animal that is destroying a crop are not automatically the same legal case.

A crime may also be concealed as an accident, private quarrel, lawful seizure, abandoned property, or customary penalty. A plausible explanation is not proof.

## 5. Investigation and evidence

There is no continent-wide professional detective service. Information is gathered by the people and institutions with local authority, access, and interest.

Possible sources include:
- the city or village watch;
- reeves, stewards, and bailiffs;
- household guards and estate officers;
- harbor officials, shipmasters, and pilots;
- guild officers and commercial witnesses;
- neighbors, servants, travelers, and market sellers;
- physicians, healers, and religious officers where their knowledge is relevant;
- written accounts, contracts, charters, seals, and correspondence.

Evidence may include direct testimony, physical traces, documents, damaged property, stolen goods, accounts, wounds, witness reputation, and a person's opportunity or motive. Reliability varies. A witness may be honest but mistaken; a document may be genuine but incomplete; a confession may be coerced or self-serving.

The setting does not establish a single continental standard of proof, a universal jury system, or modern forensic practice. Do not assume that every jurisdiction has professional investigators, standardized evidence rules, or reliable recordkeeping.

A magistrate may rely on local knowledge and testimony. A noble court may consider status, oath, reputation, and obligation. A guild may rely on its own records and the testimony of recognized members. These differences should be portrayed as institutional variation, not random inconsistency.

## 6. Hearings, trials, and judgment

A legal hearing may be informal or formal. It may involve a reeve, magistrate, lord, guild officers, witnesses, advocates, or representatives of a charter authority.

A typical matter may require:
- identifying the parties and the claim;
- establishing which authority has jurisdiction;
- hearing the accounts of those involved;
- reviewing relevant documents and witnesses;
- considering local custom and prior decisions;
- deciding what remedy or penalty is lawful;
- arranging enforcement or referral to a superior authority.

This is a useful model for GM adjudication, not a universal procedural code. Exact rules of pleading, representation, appeal, oath-taking, and evidence remain open by region and jurisdiction.

Trial by combat is not a universal substitute for legal process. Do not introduce it simply because the setting is feudal. A particular house or jurisdiction may recognize a specific form of ordeal or combat only if the GM establishes it.

## 7. Punishment and remedies

Available consequences may include:
- warning or formal reprimand;
- apology or public correction;
- restitution or compensation;
- repayment of debt;
- fines;
- forfeiture of specific goods or privileges;
- guild suspension or expulsion;
- restrictions on market or harbor access;
- temporary detention or imprisonment;
- corporal punishment;
- banishment or removal from a jurisdiction;
- capital punishment where the relevant law permits it.

The availability and use of punishment depend on the offense, local custom, jurisdiction, evidence, status, and the authority enforcing the decision. Do not assume a universal tariff of penalties.

Restitution is often practical where property or money is involved. A fine paid to the lord is not the same as compensation paid to the victim. A person may be punished by an authority while the injured party receives little or nothing.

Imprisonment may serve to hold a person awaiting judgment, secure a hostage or debtor where law permits, punish an offense, or protect the public. It is expensive to maintain and may be used unevenly. Do not assume every town has a large purpose-built prison.

## 8. Status, privilege, and unequal access

The law is not socially neutral in practice. Noble birth, wealth, guild membership, citizenship or charter status, ancestry, reputation, and access to patrons can affect how a complaint is received and enforced.

A noble may claim the right to be heard by a higher court. A guild master may have access to officials that an unconnected laborer lacks. A merchant may afford a scribe and witnesses. A poor tenant may depend on a steward or local mediator who is also responsible for collecting the rent.

This inequality does not mean every judgment is predetermined. Officials may act from duty, conscience, fear, self-interest, or a desire to preserve their reputation. A well-supported claim can matter, especially when a superior authority has reason to intervene.

Ratfolk face widespread prejudice in surface societies. That prejudice may affect credibility, access to guilds, treatment by watch officers, and willingness of officials to investigate a complaint. It does not establish that every official is hostile or that ratfolk lack all legal rights.

## 9. Law and the use of force

The watch, garrison, household guard, vassal retainers, and field forces have different responsibilities.

- A **watch** handles ordinary patrols, public order, warrants, and local enforcement.
- A **garrison** protects a fort, walls, gates, port, or strategic position.
- A **household guard** protects a noble household and its immediate interests.
- **Vassal retainers** serve under specific obligations and command relationships.
- A **local levy** may provide defense or service where custom and law permit.
- **Crown and Great House forces** are not interchangeable and do not automatically command one another.

A magistrate may issue a warrant but need the watch to carry it out. A lord may have lawful authority but lack the manpower to enforce a decision far from the seat. A powerful noble may use retainers to intimidate a witness, but doing so can itself create a legal and political problem.

Armed force does not automatically make an act lawful. Nor does a lawful judgment guarantee that it can be enforced without negotiation or resistance.

## 10. Law in trade and guild life

Commercial disputes often arise from the ordinary machinery of trade:
- unpaid invoices and loans;
- damaged or missing cargo;
- quality disputes;
- false weights and measures;
- breach of contract;
- harbor allocation;
- market stall privileges;
- workshop safety and property damage;
- apprenticeship terms;
- competing charter or toll rights.

A guild may discipline its members, regulate craft standards, or control access to a privileged market. It does not automatically have jurisdiction over outsiders or serious crimes.

Merchants may prefer private settlement because litigation exposes accounts, delays cargo, damages reputation, and consumes time. A signed contract can be powerful evidence, but its interpretation may still be disputed.

## 11. Tallowmere as a local example

Tallowmere's law operates under House Denvor's hereditary charter, confirmed by House Kharadorn after the Great Rebellion. Denvor authority covers the city and its modest surrounding lordship. Kharadorn retains strategic harbor and inland-sea defense rights but does not administer ordinary city life.

Established local offices include:
- Town Reeve: civic administration, records, local dues, market ordinances, and communication with the Chartered Council.
- Harbor Master: vessel registration, anchorage, harbor safety, cargo declarations, quay assignments, and harbor fees.
- Chief Magistrate: hearings, warrants, fines, civil disputes, and criminal proceedings delegated by the lord.
- Captain of the Watch: patrols, arrests, and coordination with the garrison in emergencies.
- Castellan of Greywatch: noble seat, household guard, stores, and defensive readiness.
- Master of Stores: grain reserves, emergency supplies, and materials held for city defense.

The watch handles ordinary order. The garrison protects Greywatch, walls, gates, and key defensive positions. Harbor officials regulate vessel movement and safety. Their jurisdictions overlap during fires, riots, attacks, and major harbor incidents.

Minor civil matters may be heard by officials or guild arbiters. Serious crimes go to the Chief Magistrate or lord's court. The lord retains ultimate local authority but frequent personal intervention undermines routine administration.

Tallowmere is not under permanent martial law. The watch is more visible near the market, gates, and quays than in poorer peripheral streets. Response quality varies with staffing, leadership, weather, and the importance of an incident.

The city's local court procedures, tax schedule, watch strength, garrison numbers, and exact charter wording remain unresolved. Do not invent them as fixed facts.

## 12. GM adjudication guidance

When a legal issue arises, establish the specific matter rather than reaching for a generic law-and-order response.

Ask:
- What right, duty, or harm is at issue?
- Where did it occur?
- Who are the parties, and what is their status?
- Which authority has jurisdiction?
- What charter, custom, contract, or law applies?
- What evidence exists, and who can access it?
- Who can afford to pursue the matter?
- What remedy is available and who can enforce it?
- What would make the authority hesitate, compromise, or refuse?

A small dispute should usually remain small unless the people involved, the rights at stake, or the wider political circumstances justify escalation. Not every theft becomes a conspiracy; not every noble crime becomes a rebellion; not every poor claimant is ignored. The result should follow the evidence, the jurisdiction, and the people making the decision.

## 13. Open decisions

- Detailed regional criminal procedure and appeal rights.
- The exact relationship between Crown, noble, charter, guild, and religious courts in each territory.
- Rules of evidence, oath-taking, witnesses, advocates, and written records.
- Whether particular jurisdictions recognize trial by ordeal or combat.
- Local prison facilities, detention customs, and the use of bail or sureties.
- Specific criminal penalties and compensation practices by region.
- The legal status of bastardy, adoption, disputed marriage, and wardship.
- The rights of freeholders, tenants, ratfolk communities, and other marginalized groups under particular charters.
- The extent of clerical jurisdiction over marriage, inheritance, and religious property.

These details should be developed when a specific case or location makes them important. This reference is not a universal law code.
