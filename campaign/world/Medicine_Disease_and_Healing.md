# Medicine, Disease, and Healing in the Realm

**Status:** GM reference framework; broad practices are proposed, not a universal medical code  
**Snapshot:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY until knowledge is deliberately adapted for player materials  
**Rules baseline:** Pathfinder First Edition

This record describes how ordinary people understand illness, injury, birth, disability, aging, and care in a high-medieval society using Pathfinder First Edition rules. It is a setting framework, not a modern medical textbook. It distinguishes practical care, learned medicine, folk belief, religious service, and supernatural healing.

No particular epidemic, endemic disease, medical guild, continent-wide hospital system, or universal standard of medical education is established by this document.

## 1. The central distinction: care is not the same as a cure

Most people who become ill or injured are cared for by family, neighbors, household staff, or whoever is available. Care may include rest, food, warmth, clean water, washing, bandaging, splinting, nursing, and practical help with daily tasks. Such care can make a real difference even when the caregiver cannot identify the underlying condition.

A healer may be skilled at recognizing common problems without being able to cure them. A physician may understand anatomy and regimen but still be unable to stop a severe infection. A temple may provide comfort, shelter, food, and burial rites without having a cleric capable of casting healing magic.

Keep these categories separate:

- **Nursing and household care:** tending, feeding, washing, keeping watch, and helping a patient rest.
- **Practical healing:** cleaning and dressing wounds, setting simple fractures, treating minor complaints, assisting childbirth, and recognizing when a case is beyond one's skill.
- **Learned medicine:** trained or book-learned practitioners who use observation, inherited medical theories, regimen, diet, and prepared treatments. Training and quality vary.
- **Folk practice:** local remedies, charms, warnings, and inherited beliefs. Some may help; others may be ineffective or harmful.
- **Religious care:** prayer, rites, charity, shelter, spiritual counsel, and burial.
- **Supernatural healing:** spells, divine intervention, alchemy, and other effects governed by Pathfinder rules and established campaign canon.

Do not let one category silently substitute for another.

## 2. Who provides care?

There is no single licensed medical profession across the continent. Local law, wealth, religion, craft tradition, and access to trained practitioners shape who is available.

### Household and community caregivers

Most ordinary care is provided by relatives, servants, neighbors, and experienced community members. A household may know how to clean a wound, prepare a warm drink, recognize a fever, or keep an injured person comfortable. A village may rely on one or two people with a reputation for practical skill.

Care is labor. Someone must fetch water, prepare food, change bedding, tend fires, clean soiled clothing, and remain with the sick. Illness can therefore burden an entire household, particularly where every adult's work is needed.

### Midwives and birth attendants

Childbirth is commonly supported by experienced women who have learned through practice, family tradition, and observation. A midwife may be respected in her community without belonging to a formal institution or possessing magical ability.

Knowledge and outcomes vary. Complications can exceed a midwife's skill, and a wealthy household may seek additional help. Do not assume a midwife can reliably prevent maternal or infant death, nor that every community has access to one.

### Barber-surgeons and practical specialists

A barber-surgeon or similar practitioner may handle tasks such as shaving, tooth extraction, lancing, wound dressing, basic setting of fractures, and treatment of surface injuries. Their competence is individual, not guaranteed by title. A person skilled in one procedure may be poorly equipped to diagnose internal illness.

### Physicians and learned healers

A wealthy noble, major merchant, or important institution may retain a physician or consult one from a larger town. Learned practitioners may study anatomy, diet, pulse, urine, fever patterns, inherited medical authorities, and the balance of bodily conditions. Their theories are not modern science, and their treatments are not automatically effective.

A physician's reputation may rest on education, patronage, successful cases, family tradition, or social standing. A clever and observant practitioner can be useful even when some of their underlying theories are mistaken.

### Herbalists and apothecaries

Herbalists gather, prepare, dry, store, and sell plant materials. Apothecaries may compound medicines, salves, oils, powders, and other preparations. Their knowledge varies widely. Some are careful craftspeople; others repeat unreliable recipes or sell adulterated goods.

A plant's presence in a medical recipe does not prove that it works. Poisonous substances, unsafe dosages, contamination, and mistaken identification are real risks. Specific remedies should be recorded only when the GM decides they matter.

### Temples and religious institutions

Temples may offer shelter, food, nursing, burial support, prayer, and charity. A temple with a capable divine spellcaster may provide supernatural healing, but that service depends on actual personnel, spells, resources, religious priorities, and willingness.

Clergy are not synonymous with spellcasters. A priest who hears confession, teaches children, keeps records, mediates disputes, or organizes alms does not thereby possess healing spells. See [Faith in Ordinary Life](../religions/Faith_in_Ordinary_Life.md) and [Tallowmere's local religious institutions](../locations/starting_city/Local_Religion_and_Institutions.md).

## 3. Injury and emergency care

Common injuries in a working society include cuts, burns, crush injuries, falls, broken bones, animal bites, infected wounds, eye injuries, drowning, exposure, and injuries caused by tools, carts, boats, and heavy loads.

The first response is often practical:
- stop immediate bleeding with pressure and dressings;
- move the injured person away from ongoing danger;
- keep them warm and monitor breathing and awareness;
- wash dirt from a wound where possible;
- immobilize a suspected fracture and avoid unnecessary movement;
- seek a more skilled practitioner for serious injury.

These are broad fictional care practices, not guarantees of survival. Infection, blood loss, shock, internal injury, and poor sanitation can make apparently simple wounds dangerous.

A clean-looking wound can still become infected. A patient who seems better may worsen later. A healer's uncertainty is more believable than a guaranteed diagnosis.

## 4. Illness and disease

People distinguish sickness by visible signs and familiar patterns: fever, cough, weakness, pain, vomiting, diarrhea, rash, swelling, difficulty breathing, confusion, or wasting. They may not know the cause. A disease name used by a village can describe a cluster of symptoms rather than a single modern diagnosis.

Potential categories for future setting use include:

- **Respiratory illness:** coughs, colds, chest infections, and illnesses worsened by smoke, damp housing, crowding, or cold.
- **Gastrointestinal illness:** vomiting and diarrhea associated with contaminated food or water, spoiled stores, poor sanitation, or infection.
- **Wound infection:** redness, swelling, pain, fever, discharge, and worsening function after injury.
- **Skin and parasite conditions:** lice, fleas, mites, fungal complaints, and other conditions associated with close quarters, animals, clothing, and bedding.
- **Childhood illness:** fever and contagious sickness that can spread through households and schools.
- **Chronic conditions:** recurring pain, impaired mobility, breathing problems, sensory loss, and long-term weakness.
- **Animal disease:** illness among livestock, working animals, and pets, with consequences for food, transport, labor, and wealth.

These are broad categories, not established endemic diseases. No named local outbreak, mortality rate, seasonal epidemic pattern, or pathogen is established. A major outbreak requires deliberate GM approval and a coherent cause, timeline, transmission pattern, and social consequence.

## 5. Sanitation, water, and housing

Disease risk is shaped by daily infrastructure as much as by individual behavior.

Relevant conditions include:
- access to safe drinking water;
- wells located near privies, drains, or waste pits;
- disposal of human and animal waste;
- crowded sleeping rooms and shared bedding;
- ventilation and smoke from indoor fires;
- storage of food away from pests and damp;
- washing of hands, tools, vessels, and cloth;
- drainage, standing water, and refuse collection;
- burial or disposal of carcasses.

Tallowmere's established urban infrastructure includes drains, wells, refuse, cellars, granaries, stables, and a working harbor. Do not assume that every district is equally clean or equally supplied. Wealthier households can afford space, servants, better food, and cleaner water sources; crowded households have fewer options.

Sanitation failures should have mundane causes and identifiable consequences. A blocked drain, contaminated well, leaking grain store, or overcrowded lodging is more useful than an unexplained plague.

## 6. Childbirth, infancy, and childhood illness

Birth is a household and community event, not a routine hospital procedure. The mother may be supported by relatives and an experienced birth attendant. Wealthy families can call on additional help, but wealth does not eliminate danger.

Pregnancy and childbirth may involve:
- food and rest shaped by household resources and custom;
- assistance from experienced women;
- ritual observances and prayers;
- preparation of clean cloth, water, bedding, and warmth;
- recovery supported by family labor;
- social expectations that vary by region, status, and household.

Infants are vulnerable to infection, poor feeding, exposure, and complications. A child's illness can disrupt an entire household. Do not treat childhood mortality as an invisible statistic or use it as casual grim decoration; if it matters in a story, give it human consequences.

## 7. Disability, chronic illness, and aging

People live with lasting injuries, impaired mobility, chronic pain, sensory disabilities, and conditions that limit work. Their experiences depend heavily on household support, wealth, occupation, community attitudes, and access to practical accommodations.

Some disabilities are visible; others are not. A person may be able to work with adaptation, have good days and bad days, or require assistance only in certain environments. Avoid assuming that disability automatically makes a character helpless, bitter, saintly, or defined by suffering.

Older people may hold valuable experience and social authority while also facing pain, reduced strength, poor eyesight, or chronic illness. Long-lived ancestries may experience aging differently, but do not assume every elf or dwarf is healthy simply because they live longer.

## 8. Medicine, magic, and Pathfinder rules

Pathfinder First Edition mechanics govern actual supernatural healing. Do not invent free magical treatment or make every temple a healing station.

- A spellcaster must have an appropriate class, level, spell access, and resources.
- Spell availability depends on the actual NPC and institution, not merely the presence of a temple.
- Healing magic may close wounds or restore hit points according to the spell, but it does not automatically answer every question about infection, disease, disability, poison, trauma, or long-term recovery.
- Disease and poison mechanics should use appropriate Pathfinder rules when they become relevant.
- Alchemy is a skilled craft, not a universal substitute for medicine or magic.
- A potion, salve, or herbal preparation should have an explicit mechanical or narrative effect if it is meant to reliably cure something.

The campaign has not established a universal healing tradition, medical school, magical hospital network, or supernatural immunity from ordinary disease.

## 9. Cost, class, and access

Medical care is not equally available. A wealthy household may pay for a physician, servants, better food, clean bedding, and time away from work. A laborer may delay seeking help because the cost of missing a day is itself dangerous. A tenant farmer may rely on household knowledge and local charity. A merchant may have access to an apothecary but still distrust a costly physician.

Institutions may provide charity, but charity has limits and may be shaped by religious affiliation, reputation, status, or local politics. A healer may accept payment in coin, food, services, or future obligation. Do not establish universal prices until a specific service and local market are defined.

## 10. Fear, stigma, and medical belief

People may attribute illness to bad air, spoiled food, divine displeasure, curses, imbalance, witchcraft, or contact with the dead. Beliefs vary by education, region, faith, and personal experience. A belief can influence behavior even when it is false.

Disease stigma can affect the sick, disabled, poor, travelers, ratfolk, and people whose work brings them into contact with waste or animals. Do not make prejudice medically justified by default. Ratfolk are not inherently diseased or contagious. Ordinary urban rats and ratfolk communities are separate subjects.

A physician who disagrees with a priest, a midwife who distrusts an apothecary, or a patient who refuses treatment can create believable conflict without requiring any party to be foolish or malicious.

## 11. Tallowmere: current local baseline

The following are established:
- Tallowmere is a working harbor city with wells, drains, refuse, cellars, granaries, stables, temples, and crowded residential areas.
- Magic is real but uncommon in ordinary life.
- The local temples and clergy are documented, but clergy are not automatically divine spellcasters.
- Tallowmere has an established local care network: Lantern Hospice and its administrator Sister Alwen; hospice stores clerk Brother Halen; independent midwife and practical healer Edda Moss; herbalist and trained healer Fenna Wale; alchemist Miraq Ashwhisk; and the House of the Dawn's basic charitable care. Their distinct roles and limits are documented in [Medical Care in Tallowmere](../locations/starting_city/Medical_Care_in_Tallowmere.md).
- No named local physician or routine public magical-healing service is established. Specific spellcasting must be checked against an actual NPC build and current availability.

The following remain unresolved:
- The exact layout, number of beds, resident clinical staff, and daily capacity of Lantern Hospice.
- Whether Tallowmere has a separate formal infirmary or dedicated sickhouse beyond the established charitable hospice.
- Whether a physician visits Tallowmere regularly from another settlement, and which practitioners serve the harbor, garrison, or noble residences.
- Which diseases are locally familiar and which are rare or imported.
- Whether any formal medical guild, school, or licensing custom exists.

Do not invent a named practitioner or facility merely to fill this section. Add one when the PCs need medical services or the local record requires it.

## 12. Future records

When a specific disease or medical institution becomes important, give it its own record with:
- name and local terminology;
- canon status and provenance;
- symptoms and likely course;
- transmission or cause, if known;
- who recognizes it and what they believe;
- practical care and known limitations;
- Pathfinder rules reference, if applicable;
- access, cost, and social consequences;
- distinction between public belief and GM truth.

The setting should feel medically believable without turning every injury into a lethal ordeal or making every disease a plot device.
