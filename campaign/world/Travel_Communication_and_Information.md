# Travel, Roads, Shipping, and Communication

**Status:** GM reference; major routes and local travel frameworks established, exact continental travel times unresolved  
**Baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY  
**Related records:** [Continental Economic Network](../economy/Continental_Economic_Network.md), [Agriculture, Food Supply, and Seasonal Work](Agriculture_Food_and_Seasonal_Work.md), [Education, Literacy, and Knowledge](Education_Literacy_and_Knowledge.md), [Tallowmere Regional Geography](../locations/starting_city/Regional_Geography_and_Points_of_Interest.md), [Tallowmere Settlements and Landholders](../locations/starting_city/Regional_Settlements_and_Landholders.md)

Travel and communication determine how people experience distance, authority, trade, danger, and news. Regional weather tendencies and their practical effects are summarized in [Weather, Climate, and Seasonal Hazards](Weather_Climate_and_Seasonal_Hazards.md). A royal decree may be clear in Valedorn and arrive weeks later in a distant settlement. A merchant may know that a ship is overdue before a noble receives an official report. A road may exist without being safe, maintained, or equally useful to every traveler.

The setting uses high-medieval transport and communication. Roads, rivers, ships, horses, carts, messengers, merchants, temples, and personal networks carry people and information. There is no instantaneous communication network or universal postal service established.

## 1. Modes of travel

### On foot

Walking is the most accessible form of travel. It is slow, physically demanding, and strongly affected by weather, terrain, road quality, load, footwear, and the traveler's condition. A person on foot may travel with a cart, animal, or group and be limited by the slowest member.

### Riding

A mounted traveler can cover more ground than a person on foot under suitable conditions, but horses require feed, water, rest, and care. A rider's speed depends on the mount, terrain, road, weather, load, and whether the animal can be changed or rested along the route.

A horse is valuable property. Exhausting or injuring one can create a serious financial and logistical problem.

### Carts and wagons

Carts and wagons move goods, tools, supplies, and people who cannot travel comfortably on foot. They are constrained by road width, surface, slope, bridges, fords, axle condition, draft animals, and the weight and balance of the load.

A road suitable for a mounted messenger may be poor for a loaded grain wagon. A bridge may carry foot traffic and riders while being unsafe for heavy carts.

### Caravans and merchant trains

Caravans combine wagons, pack animals, guards, drivers, merchants, and hired labor. They provide shared protection and pooled logistics, but travel at the pace of the train and require coordination, food, fodder, and stopping places.

A merchant caravan is not automatically a military convoy. Guards may be hired for protection, but their authority and equipment do not make them a lawful field force.

### River travel

River travel can move bulk cargo more efficiently than roads when the waterway is navigable and suitable vessels are available. Barges and other working craft depend on water depth, current, banks, towpaths, landing places, bridges, locks or other obstructions where they exist, and the skill of crews.

Downstream and upstream journeys are not equivalent. A barge may move easily with the current and require towing, poling, or hauling to return upstream. Floods, low water, debris, ice, and damaged banks can alter travel conditions.

The River Vale is an established regional trade route. Barges connect Tallowmere with lower reaches and river settlements, including Millhaven. Exact journey times depend on vessel, load, water level, direction, crew, and stops.

### Coastal and inland-sea shipping

Ships carry people and cargo over longer distances than most land routes can manage economically. Their speed and reliability depend on vessel type, wind, current, weather, crew, cargo, harbor access, pilotage, and the need to avoid dangerous waters.

The Central Inland Sea is a major maritime system under Kharadorn influence. Merrowen's eastern and southern maritime territories form a separate but connected commercial sphere. These systems are not one unified naval jurisdiction.

Commercial ships are not automatically warships. A merchant vessel may carry defensive weapons and hired guards without being part of a naval fleet. Naval escorts, patrol craft, warships, and harbor defenses are distinct military resources.

### Ferries and local crossings

Ferries serve rivers and channels where bridges are absent, unsuitable, or too expensive. Their availability depends on water levels, weather, crew, vessel condition, and local rights. A crossing charter may grant a family or authority the right to operate the ferry and collect fees.

Fordmere is an established crossing-charter center. Its ferry and crossing arrangements are local facts, not evidence that every river crossing uses the same system.

## 2. Roads and infrastructure

The continent has six established major trade corridors:
- Northern Road.
- Western Mountain Road.
- King's Road.
- Inland Sea Route.
- Eastern Coastal Road.
- Southern Trade Road.

Their economic relationships are established; exact courses and surveyed distances remain subject to cartographic review. Do not invent a precise route alignment or travel time from a broad corridor name.

Roads vary greatly. Important routes may be maintained by the Crown, a Great House, a local lord, a charter holder, a town, or several authorities sharing responsibility. A road crossing jurisdictions may be maintained in sections, with disagreements over costs and standards.

Road infrastructure may include:
- bridges, fords, ferries, and causeways;
- culverts and drainage ditches;
- mile or boundary markers where locally established;
- gates, toll posts, and customs points;
- inns, stables, yards, and market stops;
- watch posts, patrol stations, and fortified crossings;
- road surfaces of packed earth, gravel, stone, timber, or mixed construction.

Do not assume that every major road is paved, straight, guarded, or maintained to a consistent standard. A road can be strategically important and still be muddy, rutted, narrow, or neglected.

### Maintenance and authority

A road's use does not determine who owns it. A lord may hold the land beneath a road while a charter grants passage or toll rights to another authority. A town may maintain a bridge used by merchants from several territories. A ferry operator may possess a charter without owning the river.

Road repair can require labor, timber, stone, carts, tools, money, and agreement between jurisdictions. A damaged bridge may remain unrepaired because the authorities dispute who must pay, not because no one knows how to fix it.

## 3. Inns, stables, and stopping places

Travelers need places to rest, feed animals, repair equipment, obtain food, and exchange information. These services vary in quality and availability.

Possible stopping places include:
- inns and alehouses;
- roadside taverns;
- monasteries or religious hospices where they accept travelers;
- manor yards and estate guesthouses;
- ferry houses and crossing settlements;
- merchant compounds and caravan yards;
- military posts and guarded waystations;
- private homes where hospitality or kinship applies.

An inn may provide a bed, floor space, food, drink, stable room, fodder, and storage, but not every establishment offers all services. A wealthy traveler may rent a private room; a poor traveler may sleep in a common room or stable loft.

Hospitality is governed by custom, price, reputation, local security, and the host's resources. A traveler should not assume free accommodation simply because a place has a religious building or noble household.

No complete continental inn or waystation network is established. Named stops should be added only where a route or journey requires them.

## 4. Travel time and distance

The continent's approved working dimensions are approximately 1,700 miles north to south and 1,300 miles east to west at its widest point. This does not provide surveyed route distances.

For local journeys, use the distances and practical estimates in the relevant regional record. Tallowmere's hinterland distances are explicitly approximate and may be adjusted when the regional map is properly scaled.

For wider travel:
- use the calibrated map where available;
- distinguish straight-line distance from actual route distance;
- account for rivers, passes, coastlines, bridges, ferries, roads, tolls, and borders;
- account for the party's pace, load, mounts, health, and purpose;
- distinguish a fast messenger from a loaded wagon or a large caravan;
- account for rest, resupply, weather, and the need to stop.

Pathfinder First Edition overland travel rules provide a mechanical baseline when the party's speed and conditions are known. They do not replace the campaign's unresolved route geometry. Do not assign exact continent-wide journey times until the route has been traced and its conditions are known.

## 5. Weather and seasonal travel

The calendar month does not determine the weather. Seasons can arrive early or late, last months or years, and vary by region.

Travel may be affected by:
- mud and saturated ground;
- flooded roads, fords, and low bridges;
- snow, ice, and exposed cold;
- heat and water scarcity;
- mountain pass conditions;
- fog and coastal storms;
- river levels, debris, and damaged banks;
- wind and visibility at sea.

A route may become slower without becoming impassable. A river crossing may be dangerous for wagons but usable by experienced boatmen. A mountain pass may be difficult rather than closed. State the specific condition and its effect rather than applying a blanket seasonal penalty to an entire region.

No particular storm, flood, frost, drought, or road closure is established for 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC.

## 6. Travel, law, and political boundaries

The continent's political structure is divided among the Crown, Great Houses, minor houses, independent cities, clans, and charter authorities. Travelers may cross several jurisdictions during one journey.

Relevant concerns may include:
- tolls and crossing fees;
- market and harbor customs;
- local weapons or public-order rules;
- chartered rights and restricted property;
- military checkpoints during an actual emergency;
- safe-conduct letters or diplomatic credentials where a specific authority requires them;
- obligations owed by a traveler, household, merchant, or vassal.

There is no universal passport or border-control system established. Do not assume every traveler must present papers at every boundary. Equally, do not assume that a noble's seal or merchant's charter grants unrestricted passage everywhere.

A traveler may be welcome as a guest in one jurisdiction and subject to a local toll or inspection in another. The exact authority must be supported by the relevant charter, local law, or established custom.

## 7. Communication methods

### Face-to-face and local speech

Most information travels through conversation: household members, neighbors, markets, inns, workshops, ships, temples, garrisons, and local officials. People hear what their contacts know, not everything happening in the world.

### Letters and written documents

Written correspondence is valuable because literacy is not universal. Letters may be carried by household servants, hired messengers, merchants, ship crews, religious travelers, or trusted intermediaries.

A letter may be delayed, lost, damaged, intercepted, forged, or opened. A seal may help identify the sender or show whether a document has been disturbed, but it does not prove the contents are true.

### Official messengers

Noble households, the Crown, military commands, and major institutions may maintain messengers or hire reliable couriers. A messenger's speed depends on route, mount, weather, safety, and whether fresh horses or relay arrangements are available.

No continent-wide royal post with guaranteed schedules is established. The Crown may have organized courier arrangements for official business, but their scale, stations, and reach remain unresolved.

### Merchant correspondence

Merchants maintain commercial contacts through letters, account books, contracts, factors, shipmasters, and agents. Commercial networks can move information across political borders, but they are not neutral or omniscient. A factor may know the status of one cargo without knowing a rival's private plans.

### Religious and scholarly networks

Temples, religious orders, scholars, archives, and the Collegium of the Nine Lamps preserve records and exchange knowledge. Their reach depends on institutional connections, travel, patrons, and the availability of copies and messengers.

A scholar may know a historical source but not a current event. A priest may know a local community's needs but not the private affairs of a distant temple.

### Public announcements

Town criers, posted notices, bells, market announcements, guild meetings, and official readings communicate information to people who cannot read or do not receive private letters. Public notices may be selective, delayed, misunderstood, or challenged.

### Signals

Bells, horns, flags, beacon fires, and other visible or audible signals may be used locally for warnings, musters, harbor instructions, or emergencies. Their meanings are established by local practice. Do not assume a continent-wide standardized signal code.

## 8. The speed and reliability of news

Information moves at different speeds:
- A person who witnesses an event knows it immediately.
- A nearby settlement may hear about it through a traveler or local messenger.
- A merchant network may carry news along established commercial routes.
- An official report may take longer because it must be written, sealed, approved, and delivered.
- A distant court may receive an incomplete or distorted account before a verified report arrives.

Rumors can travel faster than verified facts. A dramatic story may be repeated at several inns before anyone receives a reliable account. A sealed letter may be accurate but arrive after the situation has changed.

Information delay depends on distance, route, weather, available messengers, ship schedules, security, institutional priorities, and the willingness of intermediaries to carry it.

Do not give an NPC knowledge simply because the GM knows something has happened. Ask how the information could have reached them, when it arrived, and what they would believe.

## 9. Maps, charts, and navigation

Maps are practical documents, not perfect representations of the world. Their accuracy depends on the surveyor, purpose, scale, age, political interests, and the quality of the source.

Different maps may show:
- roads and distances;
- ports, anchorages, and coastal hazards;
- property and charter boundaries;
- river channels and crossings;
- military positions and fortifications;
- pilgrimage routes and religious sites;
- merchant itineraries and toll points.

A map may omit minor settlements, private roads, local paths, or politically sensitive features. A sailor's chart may prioritize soundings and hazards rather than inland borders. A lord's estate map may emphasize rights and rents rather than the most convenient route.

The campaign's authoritative map artwork and calibrated atlas layers govern visual placement. Written travel guidance does not authorize new coordinates, route geometry, or political boundaries.

## 10. Tallowmere's local travel and communication

Tallowmere is connected to its hinterland by the River Vale, local roads, the Eastern Shore, and the routes toward the Western Uplands and South Road.

Established local routes include:
- River Vale Road;
- Western Track;
- Quarry Road;
- Eastern Shore Road;
- South Road;
- the river and its established landing places.

The local settlement record provides approximate journeys to Millhaven, Brackenholt, Stonehollow, Ashcombe Manor, Fordmere, Saltwick, and Harthbridge. These are table-planning estimates, not surveyed map measurements.

Tallowmere's communication channels are distinct and unevenly connected. The city's established NPC information-network dossier in `world_engine/Tallowmere_NPC_Information_Network.md` records likely channels and their limits; the local public-life reference describes where ordinary conversation takes place. These are descriptive GM references, not a secret organization or automated information system.

### Civic notices and official records

Routine agenda notices of the Chartered Council are posted at Reeve's Hall and the principal market. These notices communicate matters placed on the public agenda; they do not make every petition, council discussion, court matter, or administrative record public. Commercially sensitive disputes, security matters, private petitions, and personnel questions may be heard in closed session.

Reeve Marrec Holt's channels include petitions, notices, civic records, ordinary residents, and merchants. He is a strong source for matters that have reached civic administration, not a universal source for private crimes, Underways affairs, or noble household decisions. Harbor Master Ysella Vane has a narrower but more precise view of shipping, berth allocation, cargo declarations, and practical harbor disruptions. Harbor paperwork is evidence of recorded activity, not proof of criminal intent.

A public notice can be read by literate residents and repeated orally to others. Do not assume every resident sees a notice, understands its legal implications, or receives it at the same time.

### Commercial and regional reports

Merchants, factors, warehouse workers, ship crews, guilds, and carriers exchange information as part of doing business. Their knowledge is often current within their own trade and route, but may be partial or self-interested.

- **Harbor and shipping:** Ysella Vane, captains, crews, quay workers, and harbor paperwork are the strongest local channels for arrivals, berth disputes, cargo movement, and disruptions.
- **Warehousing and trade disputes:** Meret Sorn and warehouse contacts are likely to hear about storage, fire precautions, labor, credit, and cargo issues. Commercial gossip may be incomplete or strategically framed.
- **Grain and river traffic:** Harlan Tebb and Millhaven's millers hear from farmers, river carriers, grain merchants, and mill workers. Their strongest knowledge concerns harvests, river levels, milling, and grain movement around Millhaven.
- **Fuel and woodland work:** Mara Bellwood and Brackenholt workers hear about woodland access, charcoal, fuel buyers, and labor conditions.
- **Quarry and road work:** Dagna Vale and Stonehollow families hear about stone demand, quarry access, road conditions, and construction labor.
- **Eastern shore:** Edda Rusk and Saltwick households hear about fishing, nets, coastal conditions, and local shore access.

A merchant's report can be valuable without being neutral. A shipmaster may know that a vessel has not arrived; they may not know whether it was delayed, diverted, seized, or lost.

### Ratfolk community channels

Tallowmere's ratfolk have several overlapping networks, not a single authority or unified intelligence service. Tikka Vell is a public-facing advocate for market access; Miraq Ashwhisk is commercially connected through customers and workshops; Nix-of-the-Cistern prioritizes household safety and trusted routes; Vessa Reedtail is a practical mediator concerned with consent and neighborhood security. Serren Coil and the Underway Couriers provide a route-and-message contact, but their knowledge is bounded by their routes, contacts, and willingness to take a job.

These channels can carry news unavailable to surface officials, especially about particular passages, household concerns, and local safety. They do not grant universal knowledge of the Underways, and no one contact speaks for all ratfolk.

### Religious, household, and informal channels

Temples, clergy, charitable workers, household servants, neighbors, workshops, inns, and public gathering places carry news through repeated personal contact. Their knowledge follows their work and relationships. A temple may hear about illness, bereavement, charity needs, or a dispute brought to its clergy; it does not automatically know private confessions or the affairs of distant temples. A servant may know the routines of one household without knowing its lord's political plans.

The Blue Heron, Crooked Oar, Copper Ladle, Bellmarket, the quays, and neighborhood spaces provide places where information is exchanged. Their patrons and staff do not form a unified rumor network. See [Tallowmere Public Life, Neighborhoods, and Entertainment](../locations/starting_city/Public_Life_Neighborhoods_and_Entertainment.md).

### Letters, couriers, and the limits of delivery

Letters may be carried by household servants, hired messengers, merchants, ship crews, religious travelers, and trusted intermediaries. The Underway Couriers are an established local ratfolk network, but their existence does not imply a universal delivery service. No citywide public postal service, guaranteed delivery schedule, or comprehensive courier registry is established.

A sender must choose a carrier they can reach and trust. The carrier may know the destination but not the contents; a recipient may recognize a seal but not know whether the letter's account is truthful. A letter can be delayed, lost, damaged, intercepted, forged, or opened. These are possibilities, not automatic complications. Most routine messages should arrive unless circumstances give a reason otherwise.

### How to adjudicate what someone knows

For a piece of information, distinguish:
1. **Observation:** what the person directly saw or heard.
2. **Record:** what an office, ledger, letter, petition, or notice actually documents.
3. **Report:** what a trusted or untrusted intermediary told them.
4. **Inference:** what they believe the facts mean.
5. **Rumor:** what has been repeated without reliable confirmation.

When a PC asks who would know something, start with the closest relevant channel. Then consider whether the source is accessible, whether they have reason to share, whether confidentiality or self-interest matters, and how long the information has had to travel. Do not make a rumor accurate merely because it is useful to the plot.

The city has no single omniscient information network.

## 11. GM adjudication guidance

When a journey or message matters, establish:
- who is traveling or sending the message;
- what they carry and how quickly they need to arrive;
- the route and jurisdictions involved;
- the mode of travel and available support;
- weather, terrain, and road or water conditions;
- the information or goods that may be delayed, lost, intercepted, or misunderstood;
- who can plausibly learn of the journey or message;
- what alternatives exist.

Do not make every road dangerous, every letter intercepted, or every messenger late. Ordinary journeys and messages should usually work unless a specific circumstance gives the GM a reason otherwise.

Travel and communication should create meaningful constraints without becoming bookkeeping for its own sake.
