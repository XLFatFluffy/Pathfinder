# Weather, Climate, and Seasonal Hazards

**Status:** GM reference framework; broad regional tendencies are established, detailed local patterns remain open  
**Campaign baseline:** 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC  
**Visibility:** GM-ONLY until adapted for player knowledge

Weather shapes travel, food, work, health, military operations, trade, and ordinary household decisions. It should be part of the setting's physical reality without becoming a random event generator or an excuse to force hardship into every scene.

The campaign calendar establishes named months and a seasonal framework, but seasons are not fixed to those month names. They may arrive early or late, last months or years, and vary by region. This reference explains how to use that framework consistently. It does not establish a precise climate map, weather normals, a month-by-month forecast, or any current storm, drought, frost, flood, or heat event.

## 1. Calendar and actual seasons

The calendar provides dates and named months. It does not guarantee that the weather follows a modern four-season schedule.

A month called Harvestfall does not guarantee dry harvest weather. A month called Frostwane does not mean every region is frozen. The same date can bring different conditions in the north, the mountains, the inland sea basin, and the southern principalities.

For play, distinguish:
- **Calendar season:** the cultural and administrative period associated with the month or observance.
- **Actual weather:** the conditions people experience on a particular day or week.
- **Long seasonal tendency:** a regional pattern that may persist for months or years.
- **Exceptional event:** a storm, flood, drought, heat wave, deep freeze, or other unusual occurrence that requires an actual campaign decision.

Do not create a fixed weather table that automatically determines events. The GM chooses conditions according to location, current campaign circumstances, and the needs of the scene.

## 2. Continental environmental tendencies

The following are broad tendencies derived from the established geography and ecological framework. They are not precise climate classifications or guarantees about a specific day.

### Northern Wilderness and Frozen Expanse

The far north experiences severe cold, snow, ice, exposed conditions, and difficult surface travel. Wind exposure and limited shelter can be as dangerous as low temperatures. Frozen crossings may be possible in some conditions, but ice strength and reliability must never be assumed from the calendar alone.

The Northern Holds and Pine Marches are not uniformly frozen. Their settlements, forests, valleys, and coasts vary. Winter stores, livestock protection, fuel, fodder, road maintenance, and long supply routes are structural concerns. No current shortage or exceptional cold event is established at the campaign opening.

### Western Highlands and High Peaks

Elevation creates strong local variation. Valleys, sheltered slopes, exposed ridges, and high passes can experience different conditions on the same day.

Rain, snowmelt, fog, wind, slope failures, and stream levels affect movement and work. A pass may remain open while a nearby high trail becomes dangerous. Do not assume every pass closes each winter or that every highland settlement has the same climate.

### Green Valleys and Western Forests

Sheltered valleys and woodland country can retain moisture, support productive growth, and offer some protection from wind. Local rainfall, soil, drainage, canopy, and elevation matter. Heavy rain may swell streams and damage tracks; a dry period can still stress crops and woodland growth.

The presence of managed woodland does not mean every forest is equally damp or sheltered. Clearings, ridges, old stands, and exposed edges can differ sharply.

### Western Heartlands and Golden Fields

The Western Heartlands and agricultural plains support grain and livestock production. Their prosperity depends on workable soil, planting conditions, haymaking weather, harvest conditions, storage, and transport.

Rain at the wrong time can delay planting, spoil hay, soften roads, or make harvest difficult. A dry spell can affect pasture, water, and yields. These are practical risks, not evidence of a current crop failure.

### Central Crownlands and Royal Basin

The central inland river basin supports agriculture, markets, roads, and dense settlement. River crossings, low-lying fields, roads, and urban drainage can be affected by heavy rain or seasonal high water.

The Crownlands are not a single uniform weather zone. Local river courses, terrain, and distance from the inland sea matter. Exact local weather patterns remain open.

### Central Inland Sea

The inland sea is a major maritime system. Wind, storms, fog, visibility, waves, and changing conditions can affect vessels, harbor access, and coastal travel.

A sheltered harbor may offer protection from some winds while remaining vulnerable to a different direction. A ship's captain, pilot, or harbor master may judge conditions differently from a traveler looking across the water from shore. Do not treat the sea as uniformly calm or uniformly stormy.

### Northeastern Mountains and Khar-Dum

The northeastern mountains experience elevation-driven weather, exposed passes, cold conditions at higher levels, and difficult access during poor weather. Surface settlements and mountain routes are affected by snow, ice, wind, runoff, and rockfall.

Underground communities are not isolated from surface conditions. Their food, fuel, trade, entrances, ventilation, and supply movement still depend on weather outside.

### Eastern Forests, Eastern Shore, and Great Delta

The eastern forests and shore combine woodland conditions with coastal exposure. Wind, salt spray, rain, river levels, and storm damage can affect trees, paths, harbors, and coastal settlements.

The Great Delta is low-lying and shaped by branching waterways, silting, flooding, and changing channels. High water can disrupt travel and settlement access, but no specific flood or channel shift is established without a recorded event.

### Southwestern Marches

The Southwestern Marches are arid, with dry valleys, plateaus, oases, and water-storage concerns. Water scarcity is structural. Heat, drought, sudden runoff, and caravan access matter, but no current drought is established.

Arid does not mean rain never falls. When rain does occur, dry ground and channels may respond abruptly. Do not assume every storm is harmless simply because the region is usually dry.

### Southern Principalities and Southern Islands

The Southern Principalities include fertile lowlands, forests, and rivers, but political fragmentation and local geography create variation. River transport, crop cycles, flooding, and harvest labor matter.

The Southern Islands depend heavily on maritime movement, safe anchorage, and inter-island travel. Wind and storms can isolate a settlement without requiring a blockade or hostile action.

## 3. Weather and ordinary work

Weather affects people through the work they must complete.

### Agriculture and food

- Rain may help crops but delay ploughing, planting, haymaking, or harvest.
- Dry conditions may improve cutting and threshing while stressing pasture, gardens, and water supplies.
- Frost can damage crops, fruit, and vulnerable animals.
- Wind can damage orchards, roofs, stored materials, and exposed crops.
- Flooding can destroy fields, damage bridges, contaminate wells, and delay transport.
- Poor weather during haymaking can reduce winter fodder even when grain harvest is adequate.

A poor weather period does not automatically produce famine. Stores, imports, credit, alternative crops, household wealth, and political choices determine how far hardship spreads.

### Woodland and quarry work

Wet ground makes timber movement, charcoal transport, and cart traffic difficult. Rain can interfere with charcoal burning and damage older woodland tracks. Wind and dry conditions can increase fire risk around active burns and stored fuel.

Quarry work is affected by water, mud, visibility, unstable slopes, and the condition of access roads. A wet quarry face or hidden pool can be dangerous without any supernatural cause.

### Roads and travel

A road's condition depends on surface, drainage, traffic, maintenance, slope, and weather. A route suitable for a mounted messenger may be poor for a loaded wagon. A river crossing may be safe in ordinary conditions and dangerous after heavy rain.

Travelers need to account for:
- mud and standing water;
- swollen streams and unsafe fords;
- damaged bridges and washed-out approaches;
- poor visibility, fog, snow, or darkness;
- animal fatigue and the availability of fodder;
- delays at ferries, gates, or exposed crossings.

Do not assign a fixed travel penalty to every rainy day. The relevant question is what the weather has done to the particular route and traveler.

### Rivers, coasts, and shipping

River levels affect barges, ferries, mills, landings, and water quality. High water may improve navigation in one reach while making another dangerous. Low water can expose shoals, restrict loaded craft, or leave vessels grounded.

Sea conditions affect departure decisions, cargo handling, pilotage, and harbor traffic. A captain may delay sailing because the risk to vessel and crew outweighs the value of an early arrival. That is ordinary professional judgment, not cowardice.

### Health and household life

Cold, damp, heat, smoke, poor ventilation, contaminated water, and crowded shelter can affect health. Wet clothing and bedding take time and fuel to dry. Cold households may concentrate around a hearth; hot weather can make stored food spoil faster and crowded rooms unpleasant.

Weather can increase the burden on charities, healers, and household caregivers. It does not automatically create a disease outbreak or overwhelm every local institution.

## 4. Tallowmere and its hinterland

Tallowmere sits on the northwestern shore of the Central Inland Sea, where the navigable River Vale reaches the coast through a sheltered estuary. Its local weather and hazards are shaped by both river and sea.

Established local conditions and vulnerabilities include:
- Rivergate and lower waterfront streets can become muddy or flood-prone after heavy rain.
- Drains may clog; cellars and storage spaces may take water.
- Floodwater can carry silt, refuse, and animal waste into places that are normally dry.
- The Old Cistern is especially useful during dry periods and fires, but its capacity, refill source, inspection routine, and allocation rules remain unresolved.
- The harbor and river are working waterways. Conditions near docks, mills, drains, and refuse are not automatically suitable for drinking.
- Brackenholt's older woodland tracks can become dangerous after heavy rain.
- The lower River Vale is navigable for suitable barges in ordinary conditions, while upstream travel is more dependent on water level, towpaths, and pilots.
- The Central Inland Sea is subject to storms, fog, and navigation hazards.

These are established vulnerabilities, not a current weather report. At 1 Harvestfall, 243 AC, no specific temperature, rainfall pattern, storm, frost, drought, flood, or severe weather event is established.

Tallowmere's working population experiences weather differently. A dockworker may lose a day's pay when ships cannot unload. A carter may be delayed by mud. A household may spend more on fuel after a cold spell. A merchant may lose perishable goods when a ship is delayed. A wealthy household may have better shelter and stores but still face disruption to its business or travel.

## 5. Seasonal expectations without a fixed weather calendar

The following are broad working expectations, not guaranteed conditions for named months.

- **Thaw and early growth:** muddy roads, swollen streams, damaged crossings, wet ground, and the return of agricultural work may overlap.
- **Late spring and summer:** longer daylight can support travel and labor. Heat, dry spells, storms, insects, and water demand vary by region.
- **Harvest period:** dry weather is useful for cutting and storage; rain can delay work and damage crops or hay. Harvest labor competes with other work.
- **Late autumn:** wet roads, shorter days, wind, and early cold can complicate transport and winter preparation.
- **Deep winter:** cold, snow, ice, storms, and limited forage matter most in northern and upland regions; milder areas may still experience rain, damp, and difficult travel.

The calendar's named seasons and observances remain culturally important even when actual weather does not match popular expectations. People may complain that the season is late, early, or behaving strangely without implying supernatural cause.

## 6. Reading weather and local knowledge

People understand weather through experience, work, and local signs.

- Farmers watch soil, clouds, wind, dew, plant growth, and the condition of animals.
- Sailors and pilots judge wind direction, swell, cloud, visibility, current, and harbor conditions.
- Shepherds and drovers watch pasture, water, shelter, and animal behavior.
- Foresters and charcoal burners pay attention to wind, dryness, smoke, and the condition of tracks.
- Carters judge road surface, stream depth, bridge approaches, and the condition of their animals.
- Urban residents notice damp cellars, leaking roofs, blocked drains, smoky rooms, and the condition of public water points.

Local knowledge is valuable but bounded. A sailor may read coastal weather well and know little about mountain snow. A farmer may recognize signs of rain without being able to predict a storm's path. A witness can be mistaken without being foolish.

Folk beliefs about weather may influence behavior. They are not automatically true, and they should not be used as a shortcut to supernatural explanation.

## 7. GM adjudication

When weather matters, establish:

1. **Where are the characters?** Region, elevation, exposure, water proximity, and shelter matter.
2. **What are they doing?** Walking, riding, hauling, sailing, farming, fighting, camping, or waiting.
3. **What is the actual condition?** Rain, wind, heat, cold, fog, snow, or a period of settled weather.
4. **What has it changed?** Visibility, footing, water level, fire risk, food storage, animal condition, or travel time.
5. **Who is prepared?** Clothing, shelter, equipment, stores, local knowledge, and money change the outcome.
6. **What is the consequence?** Delay, extra labor, discomfort, damage, lost goods, danger, or no meaningful effect.

Do not punish players merely because weather exists. It should create texture, choices, and consequences that follow from the situation.

Do not use weather to smuggle in an unapproved disaster. A flood, crop failure, destructive storm, or prolonged drought can affect food, trade, health, politics, and military logistics. It requires a deliberate GM decision and a coherent timeline.

## 8. Deliberately unresolved

The following remain open:
- exact regional climate classifications and local averages;
- prevailing wind directions by coast, valley, and season;
- detailed rainfall and snowfall patterns;
- local frost, growing-season, and drought histories;
- exact seasonal timing of river floods, low water, and coastal storms;
- long-term seasonal anomalies and their historical frequency;
- weather lore and sayings associated with particular settlements;
- the specific weather conditions on any future campaign date unless established during play.

No weather table, climate simulator, or automatic seasonal event system is required. The GM chooses weather in context and records it when it becomes consequential.
